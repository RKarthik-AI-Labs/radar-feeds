Abstract

Cyber-range and capture-the-flag scenario construction remains labor-intensive because authors must coordinate infrastructure, vulnerable services, attack paths, artifacts, and success conditions. LLMs can propose detailed exercises, but plausible descriptions do not guarantee deployable software or verifiable objectives. We present ScenarioForge, which turns high-level requirements into resource-grounded topologies, services, traffic, segmentation, and challenge chains, then instantiates and validates them in the Common Open Research Emulator (CORE). The ScenarioForge Execution Dataset contains 226 materialized scenarios covering 294 Vulhub images and all 85 custom-built challenge-resource generators developed with our AI-assisted scaffolding. All 226 passed construction, preview, sequencing, deployment, strict runtime validation, and live artifact checks; their 867 steps—each an individually verifiable challenge objective—comprise 457 vulnerability-backed and 410 generated-resource-backed instances. In a separate 50-prompt matched benchmark, ScenarioForge+Qwen3.8 and ScenarioForge+GPT5.6 each achieved 50/50 (100%) topology/build success and 160/160 intent fields; direct frontier generation reached 41/50 builds and 147/160 fields only with the full cumulative scaffold: structure, vocabulary, policy, and container-image guidance. The test still omitted ScenarioForge’s downstream challenge-resource generation, dependency/flag sequencing, deployment, and live validation. The artifacts support operationally rich, reproducible exercises for training and evaluation.

I 
Introduction

Cyber ranges reproduce networked systems, vulnerabilities, and adversarial activity for controlled training and evaluation. Effective exercises require more than a narrative: authors must coordinate infrastructure, vulnerable components, network dependencies, participant actions, and success conditions. This combination of domain expertise and detailed configuration makes scenario construction expensive; automation can improve diversity and adaptability

The unresolved problem is the boundary between description and execution. An LLM may propose unavailable implementations, incompatible versions, invalid configurations, or unsupported objectives. Assessments of soundness, realism, creativity, or usability do not establish that a scenario can be instantiated and operated

We present ScenarioForge (Fig. 
1
), a framework that treats LLM output as a proposal rather than a finished environment. A typed, persistent representation separates generative authorship from deterministic grounding and execution: the model proposes scenario intent and relationships, while backend components decide which installed assets can realize them and record the resulting bindings. The resolved specification is then instantiated in CORE and judged using observations from the deployed environment. This division establishes an explicit trust boundary—plausible generated content is never accepted as evidence of an available resource, an operational service, or a supported objective.

Our contributions are:

•

Automated, resource-grounded construction:
 ScenarioForge grounds topology, services, segmentation, traffic, artifacts, and challenge chains in deployable resources. It supports Vulhub environments and user-developed vulnerabilities, services, and artifacts, with built-in automated resource construction using local or cloud LLMs.

•

Execution-based validation and infrastructure:
 Catalog preflight, preview, sequencing, deployment, runtime validation, and live artifact checks produce operational evidence. Our evaluation harness and hardened CORE 9.2 VM/fork make these checks repeatable for container-backed ranges.

•

A matched AI-authoring benchmark:
 Across 50 separate benchmark prompts, both ScenarioForge+Qwen3.8 and ScenarioForge+GPT5.6 achieved 50/50 (100%) topology/build success and 160/160 requested intent fields. Thus, the relatively small, open-weight Qwen3.8 model matched the frontier model inside ScenarioForge, and the framework-assisted conditions outperformed direct frontier generation, which peaked at 41/50 builds and 147/160 fields despite cumulative scaffolding. We release the frozen prompts and expectations, retained outputs, scoring records, and analysis scripts needed to reproduce the comparison.

•

A reproducible execution dataset:
 We release the ScenarioForge Execution Dataset: 226 fixed scenarios covering 294 Vulhub images, all 85 custom-built challenge-resource generators, and 867 challenge steps across varied topologies, services, policies, traffic, artifacts, and dependencies.

Together, the matched benchmark isolates description-to-buildable-definition conversion, while the Execution Dataset follows fixed, grounded definitions through sequencing, deployment, and live evidence. This proposal-to-evidence design makes the contribution more than a prompt or generator. It localizes failures to authoring, resolution, compilation, deployment, runtime state, or artifact verification; recorded phase timings characterize reproduction cost in the stated environment.

III 
Methodology

III-A
 
System Overview

As Fig. 
1
 shows, ScenarioForge combines LLM-based synthesis with deterministic grounding, execution, and validation. Inputs may range from a broad objective to explicit topology, service, vulnerability, routing, segmentation, access, and challenge constraints.

The canonical scenario XML persists throughout the workflow. Grounding maps network elements to CORE configurations, vulnerable services to Docker-backed resources, and CTF objectives to installed challenge-resource generators. The resolved scenario runs natively or in a CORE 9.2 VM controlled through gRPC and SSH; a participant may attach through hardware-in-the-loop networking. Runtime checks cover nodes, containers, services, ports, injects, segmentation, traffic, and artifacts. A Solutions Script traverses the challenge sequence and records pass, fail, inconclusive, or skip. XML, logs, summaries, and reports preserve the evidence.

III-B
 
Scenario Specification and Intermediate Representation

ScenarioForge uses one XML document across authoring, preview, sequencing, and execution; when invoked, the LLM refines it but never invokes CORE, Docker, or remote shells. The representation records infrastructure, node and resource configuration, challenge state and dependencies, participant connectivity, and execution metadata. Partially specified scenarios preserve user constraints while the model fills missing fields, and every generated value must resolve to a catalog entry or backend capability. Embedded 
PlanPreview
 and sequencing state make the previewed topology and challenge structure the deployment inputs rather than a separate plan.

III-C
 
LLM-Guided Scenario Synthesis

The LLM extracts constraints, decomposes the request into infrastructure and exercise components, completes unspecified roles and relationships, and serializes the result into constrained XML. Explicit constraints remain authoritative, so a fixed vulnerability, node count, or challenge order is preserved while surrounding topology and services may be generated.

The WebUI combines model-guided authoring with a topology-intent compiler that preserves explicit requests. Its built-in AI-assisted Generator Builder uses local or cloud LLMs to scaffold or refine user-developed vulnerability and challenge resources as 
flag-generator
 or 
flag-node-generator
 implementations, contract-test them, and repair failures before installation. This workflow produced the 85 custom-built challenge-resource generators evaluated here; installed generators execute deterministically.

Because services, versions, generators, or attack steps may be unavailable or incompatible, initial output remains a proposal. Deterministic resolution establishes which concrete assets and parameters can realize it, preserving flexible authoring without delegating deployment decisions to the model.

This hybrid path is important when requests mix exact and open-ended requirements. The intent compiler converts explicit counts and named services into typed constraints, while model calls fill relational structure and unspecified values. Resolution can add the hosts required to place selected containers, reconcile identifiers after topology edits, and reject catalog choices whose architecture or runtime prerequisites do not match the execution endpoint. Validation and repair operate on the persisted representation, so a corrected draft is rechecked against the same constraints rather than regenerated from an unconstrained conversation.

III-D
 
Grounding in Executable Cybersecurity Resources

Grounding retains an element only when it maps to an executable asset or supported deterministic configuration. Vulnerable services resolve to cataloged Docker Compose environments, ports, startup procedures, and inject plans, including user-developed resources and reproducible Vulhub environments 
[
16
]
. Challenge-resource generators produce artifacts, flags, deployment instructions, and solutions; network and participant resources compile to CORE, HITL, or Proxmox-supported configurations. Thus a service name alone is insufficient without a compatible version and runtime definition, and an objective is accepted only when its installed generator supplies both an artifact and a verification procedure. Static preflight rejects invalid catalog or inject wiring before execution.

The active challenge-resource generator catalog covers binary artifacts such as firmware and packet captures; credentials and signatures; JSON, YAML, and INI configurations; encoded payloads; and textual evidence such as chat exports, tickets, operations notes, and backups. Each entry records its producer, placement, expected output, and solution procedure. This makes catalog state, rather than a model’s recollection, the authority for what the range can deploy and verify.

Three invariants connect this algorithm to the implementation. First, every selected resource is identified by a catalog key and copied into the resolved specification, making model output insufficient as a binding. Second, all dependency endpoints must name persisted steps or typed artifacts before topological ordering. Third, deployment and validation consume the same resolved XML and stable node identifiers used by preview. These invariants prevent a plausible but unavailable component, an orphaned prerequisite, or a preview-only repair from silently entering execution. Failures therefore have typed locations: grounding, dependency analysis, preflight, deployment, runtime validation, or objective verification.

III-E
 
Topology and Exercise Construction

After grounding, ScenarioForge compiles hosts, routers, links, addressing, routes, service placement, traffic, segmentation, and artifacts into CORE operations. Construction follows dependency order: networks precede services, targets precede injects, and artifacts follow active destinations. A preview from the execution XML exposes the exact topology, roles, vulnerabilities, and dependencies before deployment.

III-F
 
Dependency-Aware Challenge Construction

ScenarioForge represents each exercise as a dependency graph. A 
step
 is one verifiable challenge objective bound to a grounded service, file, credential, or challenge node and an expected result. The framework connects requirements to predecessor outputs, rejects unresolved or cyclic graphs, and records targets, flags, and solution actions. 
Sequence
 constructs a valid order and 
Resolve
 selects resources and parameters; both update the same XML.

Sequencing persists more than an ordinal list. Each assignment records the target node and service, the resource that implements the objective, prerequisite access facts, typed inputs produced by earlier steps, expected outputs, and the verification strategy. Topological ordering then yields a deployable Flow in which generated files, credentials, endpoints, and access capabilities become explicit edges rather than instructor-only assumptions. The persisted sequencing JSON and updated XML are consumed by execution, the Solutions Script, and artifact validation, so all three observe the same resolved dependency graph.

After deployment, the Solutions Script traverses the graph and records pass, fail, inconclusive, or skip. Supported HTTP, SSH, and TCP challenge-node strategies are automatic; vulnerability-host and artifact-only steps preserve flags, hints, solutions, and interfaces for participant or exploit-harness interaction. Intermediate outputs reach only dependent steps, while strict validation establishes deployment and artifact readiness across the resolved graph.

III-G
 
Segmentation and Pivot Constraints

Pivoting is a dependency edge from provider 
 to target 
: a predecessor produces access to 
, and the dependent step requires it before targeting 
. Providers may be vulnerable hosts, generated challenge nodes, or supported SSH fallbacks.

The graph must link requirement to provider; policy must permit the service from 
 and deny unauthorized sources; probes require success from 
 and failure from representative prohibited sources. This positive-and-negative test avoids treating allow-list membership as proof of isolation.

Each dependent step carries its provider–target pair, so multiple or non-adjacent pivots do not overwrite global context. Paths are probed independently and reported through the common verification record.

III-H
 
Human-in-the-Loop Scenario Refinement

Users may provide a broad objective or detailed requirements, or bypass AI entirely and construct the scenario in the WebUI. The shared XML exposes a resolved preview before deployment. Edits trigger re-resolution, allowing unsupported resources, placement, sequence, or connectivity to change without restarting authoring. When used, the model proposes intent; grounding restricts implementation, and the user retains final control.

III-I
 
Cyber-Range Instantiation

After topology, resources, and dependencies are fixed, ScenarioForge executes natively or separates the application, CORE 9.2, and participant roles across hosts. CORE gRPC manages session topology and lifecycle; SSH handles placement, Docker, services, injects, and inspection. Execution creates the topology and policy, deploys services, applies injects and artifacts, then activates traffic and HITL attachments. Web and CLI workflows share this backend, and request identifiers let retried clients reconnect to long operations.

III-J
 
Multi-Stage Validation and Verification

Validation separates three failure classes. Catalog preflight checks deployment metadata, services, and inject wiring. Runtime validation inspects the active CORE session for expected nodes, containers, ports, injects, outputs, traffic, and policy behavior. Challenge verification traverses the resolved sequence, automatically exercises supported strategies, and records pass, fail, inconclusive, or skip for each step. Web and 
check-artifacts
 CLI paths return a structured 
validation_summary
; strict success requires its 
ok
 field and all relevant issue counters to be clear. Passing one stage does not imply success at a later one.

In particular, strict validation requires zero missing nodes, stopped Docker services, missing injects, missing generator outputs, and missing generator injects. Positive and negative reachability probes check segmentation, while artifact checks inspect the deployed environment rather than merely the generated plan. Per-issue logs retain the observation that caused a failure, preserving the boundary among catalog qualification, composed runtime state, and objective completion.

IV 
ScenarioForge Execution Dataset

IV-A
 
Construction and Intended Configuration

The ScenarioForge Execution Dataset contains 226 fixed, materialized specifications, each preserving its authoring input, seed, catalog choices, authoritative XML, preview, challenge order, execution evidence, and derived composition. For every case, ScenarioForge generated and persisted Flow order, challenge dependencies, and flag placement from the resolved challenge graph; all 226 then traversed deployment, strict runtime validation, and live artifact checks. Intended configuration is preserved separately from execution logs, so success does not define intended content. The collection uses one validated catalog snapshot covering 294 enabled, self-contained Vulhub images and the complete enabled set of 85 custom-built challenge-resource generators; ten imported entries requiring external online services were excluded. This dataset is distinct from the 50-prompt Matched AI-Authoring Benchmark in Section V.

Thirty source specifications define vulnerability-focused, artifact-focused, mixed, scale, and segmented families. The evaluation harness expands their iteration counts and seeds into 226 resolved specifications before execution, freezing bounded topology choices, resource assignments, policy and traffic settings, and validation requirements. This two-level organization preserves both the reusable family definition and the exact case that ran. It also allows a future catalog or backend revision to replay identical resolved cases or deliberately rematerialize a family and report that distinction.

IV-C
 
Intended Uses and Coverage

Fixed inputs and bindings support regression testing, controlled agent or participant comparisons, and composition analysis without reconstructing intent from logs. The dataset’s deliberate composition combines complete enabled-catalog coverage with varied family sizes, chain lengths, topology scales, access patterns, segmentation policies, and reproducible traffic profiles.

Each record provides three aligned views of the same exercise: source and resolved specifications capture intended configuration; preview and sequencing outputs capture compiled topology and dependency structure; and runtime logs, structured validation, and artifact reports capture observed execution. Stable scenario, node, and challenge identifiers connect these views. This organization supports separate measurement of intent preservation, topology construction, deployment, policy behavior, and objective evidence while retaining the exact configuration needed to reproduce or extend a case.

V 
Experimental Evaluation

V-A
 
Experimental Questions and Evidence Boundary

The experiments use separate inputs. 
EQ1
 asks whether all 226 fixed Execution Dataset scenarios traverse XML construction, preview, sequencing, deployment, strict runtime validation, and live artifact checks. 
EQ2
 asks whether resource-grounded authoring produces CORE-buildable topologies and preserves requested intent better than direct frontier generation across an independent 50-prompt benchmark. EQ2 measures the topology-facing items controlled by the AI Generator through safe CORE construction. ScenarioForge’s downstream sequencing stage derives Flow order, challenge dependencies, and flag sequence from the grounded topology and catalog metadata; EQ1 evaluates these stages. A separate no-Flow audit starts the framework-assisted outputs.

V-B
 
Evaluation Infrastructure and Full-Pipeline Study

We built 
scenarioforge-eval
 to exercise each fixed specification once. It parses YAML, persists seed and XML, invokes preview, sequencing, execution, and artifact checks, serializes phases sharing a CORE VM, and retains inputs, logs, metrics, and reproduction packages. Strict success requires normal process exit, 
validation_summary.ok
, zero issue counters, and observed expected containers, services, ports, injects, policies, traffic, and access paths. Its WebUI controls runs and indexes outcomes, timings, failure locations, topology, composition, and evidence.

The harness exposes separate Execute and Analysis views. Execute selects the specification set, ScenarioForge checkout, output directory, phase boundary, and replay mode; it streams bounded logs and supports stopping, resuming, or rerunning selected phases. Analysis indexes result trees and filters by run metadata before presenting topology, composition, timing, phase outcomes, artifacts, and validation evidence. This interface supported live diagnosis during the study, while the retained JSON and CSV records remain the source for reported measurements.

The evaluation VM packages our customized CORE 9.2 fork. We fixed container-initialization races, Docker/Podman node and interface persistence across gRPC/XML, stale interfaces, Compose project and session isolation that could cause collisions or empty volumes, and container shell/environment discovery. These are repeatability and runtime-infrastructure contributions, not AI Generator outputs.

The batch used a 16-core, 48 GB Apple M4 Max host and CORE 9.2 in a local 8 GB VMware VM. We report the 194 runs whose images were already cached separately from the 32 runs in which image pulls were observed, exposing cache state alongside each timing range.

Under this infrastructure, all 226 executions completed XML construction, preview, sequencing, deployment, strict validation, and live artifact checks with no phase failures. The specifications establish intended membership; retained batch records establish outcomes and localize future failures by phase.

V-C
 
Matched AI-Authoring Benchmark

The Matched AI-Authoring Benchmark contains 50 arm-neutral requests across five complexity tiers, progressing from routed networks through services, segmentation, vulnerable containers, and combined stress cases. We used 
gpt-5.6-sol
 to generate the initial suite with this instruction: 
“Generate 50 concise, arm-neutral cyber-range requests in five increasing-complexity tiers containing 12, 12, 10, 10, and 6 cases. Progress from routed host networks to services, firewall/NAT segmentation, vulnerable containers, and combined stress cases. Vary router and host counts; do not mention ScenarioForge, its schema, catalog names, or tools. For each request, return machine-readable expected router, host, service, segmentation, vulnerability, and traffic fields.”
 The resulting requests and expectations were frozen before any evaluated condition ran.

Scorers parse each condition’s native output; missing or unreadable output remains in the denominator. Seven conditions over 50 prompts yield 350 retained native authoring artifacts—100 framework-assisted ScenarioForge definitions and 250 direct response/XML records—and 1,120 intent-field decisions (160 per condition). The framework runner permits one retry only after provider timeout; reported token and timing values use the retained successful attempt.

Across the frozen suite, each condition receives requests totaling 462 topology-node instances: 128 routers (mean 2.56; range 1–5) and 334 hosts (mean 6.68; range 2–15). Every prompt specifies both counts. Twenty-four prompts request 38 named services (15 SSH, 15 HTTP, and 8 HTTPS); 18 request 24 segmentation instances (14 Firewall and 10 NAT); 15 request 29 vulnerable-service placements; and three request background traffic. Tier-specific feature totals progress from none in T1 to 19 services in T2; 3 services and 12 policies in T3; 3 services, 2 policies, and 18 vulnerabilities in T4; and 13 services, 10 policies, 11 vulnerabilities, and all three traffic requests in T5. These six field types produce the 160 intent decisions per condition. Per-request expectations and detailed tier composition are released under 
frontier-comparison/specifications/
 in the dataset repository.

The framework-assisted arm sends only the description through ScenarioForge’s AI Generator. We denote the open-weight 
Qwen3.8-27B-NVFP4
 condition 
ScenarioForge+Qwen3.8
 and the frontier 
gpt-5.6-sol
 condition 
ScenarioForge+GPT5.6
; both receive the same schema, compiler, catalogs, validation, and repair path. The direct arm sends the same description to 
gpt-5.6-sol
 without ScenarioForge. Its five cumulative scaffold levels, or rungs, progress from a CORE XML request (R1), to a structural example (R2), node/service vocabulary (R3), segmentation vocabulary (R4), and container-image guidance (R5). The cumulative design measures how much representation knowledge direct generation requires before it approaches the framework-assisted conditions, while the two AI Generator conditions expose the effect of changing the underlying model without changing the framework.

Every rung retains the same user request and all prior instructions, so differences across R1–R5 reflect added representation guidance rather than changing task content. R1 asks for a loadable CORE XML artifact. R2 demonstrates document structure, R3 enumerates permitted node and service terms, R4 adds the policy vocabulary needed for firewall and NAT requests, and R5 supplies container-image conventions for vulnerable services. The direct model receives no ScenarioForge catalog lookup, compiler, validation tool, or repair loop. Conversely, the framework conditions receive no rung-specific direct scaffold: they use the ordinary AI Generator path under test.

EQ2 excludes Flow/challenge-chain outcomes because downstream sequencing, not the AI Generator, derives order, dependencies, and flag sequence. Scoring them would misattribute framework decisions. Created with ScenarioForge’s AI-assisted authoring/scaffolding tools, the 226 fixed EQ1 scenarios collectively exercise chains, challenge resources, artifact dependencies, access-controlled pivots, segmentation, traffic, deployment, and live validation.

Intent accuracy
 is the fraction of requested fields matched after parsing each native representation. Host counts use the same topology-level definition in both arms: all materialized non-router hosts, including hosts that carry vulnerable services. 
Buildability
 is successful CORE 9.2 construction of a nonempty session from the condition’s native artifact. The scorer calls 
open_xml(start=False)
, causing CORE to parse and instantiate the topology definition while leaving nodes, containers, and physical interfaces inactive. Per-case and summary records are retained. The companion robustness audit starts the framework-assisted outputs, runs post-execution validation, and invokes live artifact checks with Flow disabled.

The scorer first extracts each condition’s native XML, then derives observed topology and resource fields without normalizing the artifact through ScenarioForge. This avoids silently repairing direct output before grading. Build and intent are reported separately: a structurally valid session can omit requested content, while a textually plausible artifact can preserve counts yet fail CORE construction. Keeping missing outputs and absent fields in their denominators prevents selective reporting of parseable cases.

V-D
 
Matched-Benchmark Results

Table 
III
 answers EQ2. Both framework conditions reached the buildability ceiling, whereas direct frontier generation improved across rungs but remained nine builds short at R5. Failure inspection adds detail beyond the aggregates: at R1, 35 artifacts loaded with no nodes and 10 were rejected; all nine R5 failures were CORE rejections. Qwen and GPT tied inside the framework, indicating that model scale was not decisive under the shared schema, catalog, compiler, validation, and repair path.

Intent accuracy followed the same pattern: both framework conditions reached the ceiling, while direct R5 remained 13 fields short and satisfied every field in 38 of 50 requests. Table 
III
 also reports the cost and latency difference between the tied framework outcomes, separating authoring quality from provider effort.

The rung trajectory in Table 
III
 shows that the structural example produced the largest gain; node/service vocabulary primarily improved buildability, segmentation vocabulary improved intent without changing buildability, and image guidance yielded a smaller final gain. Increasingly explicit syntax and vocabulary therefore narrowed but did not close the gap. The framework conditions reached both ceilings without exposing this cumulative representation scaffold to the user.

All 100 framework-generated scenarios (50 per model) ran on the ARM CORE VM with Flow challenge sequencing disabled; after 60 seconds, runtime and live-artifact checks were applied. With original resources, ScenarioForge+Qwen3.8 passed 46/50 and ScenarioForge+GPT5.6 passed 47/50. Diagnostic replays recovered remaining cases: one GPT case passed unchanged after a transient CORE failure, while two GPT and four Qwen passed after documented substitutions with prior successful execution on the same ARM endpoint. The qualified replays reached 50/50 for each framework condition; first-pass and remediation outcomes remain separately reported.

The gap between 100% construction and lower first-pass runtime/artifact success identifies the remaining failure layer: a CORE topology can construct before an architecture-incompatible container or transient daemon event interrupts startup. Reporting original resource selections and remediated replays independently distinguishes topology quality from endpoint compatibility and transient infrastructure behavior.

V-E
 
Interpretation and Measurement Scope

As Table 
III
 shows, the same frontier model reached both measured ceilings only inside ScenarioForge; ScenarioForge+Qwen3.8 reached them as well. Schema, catalogs, compilation, validation, and repair therefore materially change whether generated intent becomes a usable CORE definition. EQ1 and EQ2 test complementary sides of the trust boundary—description-to-definition and fixed-definition-to-validated-exercise, respectively—and neither substitutes for the other.

We used 
gpt-5.6-sol
 to generate the arm-neutral request suite and froze all requests and expected fields before evaluation. Intent scoring covers six observable topology and resource field types, while provider-reported token counts and latency characterize the retained authoring calls. Together with the per-case native artifacts and CORE outcomes, these measurements expose both semantic preservation and executable-structure quality.

Reproduction supports interactive and collection workflows. For individual authoring, a user works through the AI Generator or directly in the WebUI, reviews or edits the grounded preview, and launches sequencing, execution, validation, and export from the same interface. For collections, a 
.spec.yaml
 file defines a reusable scenario family through iteration count, seed policy, fixed or bounded topology and resource choices, Flow settings, segmentation, traffic, access, and validation policy. Materialization freezes one resolved specification per iteration, letting users regenerate a family to explore diversity or replay an authoritative XML and seed to reproduce the recorded case exactly.

The command 
scenarioforge-eval --sf-path SPEC_PATH --execute --out OUTPUT_PATH
 reproduces specification collections. Replay may begin from a specification and seed or authoritative XML, bypassing model variation. The reproduction ZIP carries authoritative XML, a hashed manifest, and materialized artifact sources; WebUI import reconstructs the recorded configuration and artifact state for replay, or regenerates artifacts deterministically from saved resolved inputs. It must be protected because it may contain credentials, flags, and generated secrets. Solutions Script, HITL, and exploit-harness interfaces support participant and automated exercise interaction.

Within the dataset repository, source specifications capture reusable families, resolved specifications freeze randomized choices, and result directories preserve the authoritative XML and observed run evidence. The comparison subtree likewise retains each native response and grading record rather than only aggregate percentages. A manifest supplies file hashes, and the included analysis scripts recompute the paper-facing table from per-case records. Connection credentials and provider secrets are redacted; reproductions must supply their own CORE connection settings outside the archive.

The release supports aggregate, per-case, and raw-evidence audits. Specifications establish EQ1’s intended composition; native artifacts and request identifiers trace EQ2 outcomes. Raw XML, logs, and reports preserve negative and intermediate evidence alongside successful examples.

VIII 
Conclusion

ScenarioForge separates proposals from grounded, deployed, and validated exercises. All 226 Execution Dataset scenarios completed the full pipeline. In the independent 50-prompt benchmark, both framework conditions achieved 50/50 topology/build success and 160/160 intent fields; direct frontier generation peaked at 41/50 and 147/160. The smaller open-weight model matched the frontier model inside ScenarioForge. These complementary studies evaluate topology authoring and full-pipeline execution; released artifacts support reproduction and future cross-model, participant, and agent studies.

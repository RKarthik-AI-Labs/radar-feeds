Abstract.

Large language model (LLM) agents have demonstrated strong capabilities in automating complex information-seeking and transactional tasks across the open web. However, their real-world reliability remains limited by access barriers, such as dead links, authentication walls, rate limits, and anti-scraping defenses. These barriers block the primary execution path, causing agents to abort prematurely, loop indefinitely, or quietly compromise the user’s original constraints. Existing evaluations either implicitly assume that target resources are freely accessible or focus on transient perturbations that simple retries can resolve, failing to capture scenarios where agents must actively reroute around impassable bottlenecks. To bridge this gap, this paper introduces 
AgentHeal
, a comprehensive benchmark evaluating 
goal-preserving resource recovery
, defined as the ability of web agents to detect non-transient access barriers, pivot to legitimate alternative sources, and fulfill the user’s exact original objective. Spanning nine barrier families across a counterfactual sandbox and real-website replay, our experiments on leading LLM backbones show that average task success drops sharply from 89.4% to 43.7% under access barriers. Further analysis reveals that agents frequently fall for deceptive look-alikes, fail to escalate to users when necessary, violate access policies, or waste computation on futile retries, providing actionable diagnostics for developing resilient web agents. Code and Dataset are available at 
https://github.com/Lzzzzzzzzzzy/AgentHeal
.

1. 
Introduction

Recent advancements in LLMs have catalyzed a paradigm shift from passive text generators to autonomous agents capable of complex reasoning, planning, and tool use 
(
Yao et al., 2022b
; 
Schick et al., 2023
)
. Within this landscape, web agents have emerged as a particularly promising frontier. By automating multi-step digital workflows, such as open-ended information retrieval, cross-platform navigation, and online decision-making, these systems are increasingly deployed across the open web as autonomous research assistants, browser extensions, and command-line tools 
(
Nakano et al., 2021
; 
Zhou et al., 2024
; 
Drouin et al., 2024
)
.

To systematically evaluate and advance these capabilities, a progression of foundational benchmarks has emerged. Early efforts such as WebShop 
(
Yao et al., 2022a
)
 established a simulated e-commerce environment for grounding language instructions into web actions. Subsequent benchmarks including Mind2Web 
(
Deng et al., 2023
)
, WebArena 
(
Zhou et al., 2024
)
, and VisualWebArena 
(
Koh et al., 2024a
)
 expanded evaluation to realistic, multi-step tasks across diverse, functional websites. Most recently, BrowseComp 
(
Wei et al., 2025
; 
Chen et al., 2026
)
 and BEARCUBS 
(
Song et al., 2025
)
 challenge agents to locate hard-to-find information directly on the live web, substantially raising the bar for generalist web navigation.

Despite their increasing realism, existing benchmarks largely operate under an implicit 
Idealized Reachability Assumption
–the premise that the target webpage or primary information source required to fulfill a user’s instruction is perpetually accessible and functional. In the wild, however, the open web is inherently dynamic, fragmented, and adversarial, which makes the assumption unrealistic. Autonomous agents frequently encounter hard access barriers, including subscription paywalls, anti-bot CAPTCHAs, geographic restrictions, rate-limiting blocks (e.g., HTTP 403/429), and link rot (e.g., HTTP 404 or deprecated pages). While recent stress-testing and robustness studies have examined agent resilience against UI layout perturbations, pop-up distractions, or adversarial prompt injections 
(
Kara et al., 2025
; 
Wang et al., 2026
; 
Bai et al., 2026
)
, they fundamentally overlook scenarios where the primary resource itself becomes completely unreachable, simply retrying is infeasible and the agent must instead identify an alternative route that still satisfies the user’s original request.

Consequently, we argue that a fundamental gap exists between static, controlled benchmark evaluations and the unpredictable reality of open-world web deployment. Even on the live web, existing benchmarks usually assume “happy-path” execution where target pages remain intact and accessible.
In practice, humans rarely abandon a task when they encounter a broken link or a paywalled article. Instead, they naturally adapt by reformulating search queries, consulting cached archives, or cross-referencing alternative repositories. Current web agents, by contrast, often become trapped in repetitive action loops or hallucinate unverified answers when their primary trajectory is blocked. We formalize this missing competency as 
Autonomous Self-Healing
: the ability of an agent to diagnose terminal access failures, dynamically reroute to legitimate alternative sources, rigorously audit cross-source factual consistency, and abstain honestly when no viable substitute exists. Bridging this gap necessitates a dedicated evaluation paradigm that moves beyond idealized “happy-path” execution.

To this end, this work investigates how modern web agents behave under resource-blocked conditions by addressing three core research questions:

(1)

Capability:
 When obstructed by unexpected access barriers on a primary target, can an agent autonomously pivot and discover alternative resources to successfully complete the task?

(2)

Reliability:
 Can the agent critically audit the authenticity and relevance of candidate alternative resources (e.g., avoiding outdated or spurious mirrors), and honestly report task infeasibility rather than hallucinating when no valid alternatives exist?

(3)

Efficiency:
 How many interaction steps does an agent require to either successfully recover via an alternative path or decisively terminate an unsolvable task without wasting computation in futile retries?

However, constructing a rigorous benchmark to evaluate these dimensions presents three non-trivial challenges:
(i) 
Controllable and Reproducible Obstruction:
 Real-world web blocks (e.g., dynamic CAPTCHAs or temporary server outages) are stochastic and ephemeral, making it difficult to ensure deterministic, reproducible evaluation across different models and time periods.
(ii) 
Multi-Path Ground-Truth Verification:
 Once an agent deviates from the blocked primary source, the space of valid trajectories expands exponentially. Verifying whether an open-ended alternative source genuinely satisfies the original user intent requires fine-grained semantic and factual auditing.
(iii) 
Calibrating Deceptive Dead-Ends:
 Assessing reliability requires carefully curated scenarios featuring both noisy/outdated look-alike pages and true dead-ends (where information is exclusively held behind an impenetrable barrier), so as to distinguish genuine verification from lucky guessing or hallucination.

To address this challenges, we introduce 
AgentHeal
, a comprehensive benchmark evaluating the self-healing capabilities of web agents under realistic access failures, as shown in Fig. 
1
. 
AgentHeal
 organizes access barriers into nine families across four categories within two complementary environments: a counterfactual sandbox and a real-website replay suite. In the sandbox, each request is evaluated across three controlled variants: a barrier-free baseline, a recoverable barrier with valid alternatives, and an unrecoverable barrier containing only deceptive look-alikes. This counterfactual design prevents any static strategy, such as always guessing or abstaining, from succeeding across both failure modes. Moreover, valid alternatives are never exposed alongside the blocked page, requiring agents to actively discover them via web archives or scholarly indexes. For rigorous evaluation, each candidate source embeds a unique answer value, enabling deterministic source attribution to audit LLM judge verdicts while gating success on access policy compliance.

We conduct extensive experiments across several LLM backbones and find that existing agents struggle substantially on 
AgentHeal
, with average task success dropping sharply from 89.4% on barrier-free baselines to 43.7% under access barriers. Beyond raw performance drops, our analysis reveals severe reliability flaws. Agents frequently fall for deceptive look-alikes with matching titles and topics, rarely escalate to the user even when human intervention is strictly required to unblock a resource, and routinely violate access policies. These failure modes indicate that current web agents struggle to balance task completion with operational reliability. Furthermore, when confronted with truly unrecoverable barriers, agents fail to terminate gracefully, exhausting computational budgets on futile retries.

The main contributions of this work are as follows:

•

Problem Formulation.
 We formalize autonomous self-healing for web agents under persistent access barriers, distinguish equivalent copies, other versions, and look-alikes of a requested resource, and define a compliance-gated criterion that specifies the goal-preserving response under every availability condition.

•

Benchmark Design.
 We construct 
AgentHeal
, which combines a counterfactual sandbox with known solvability and calibrated discovery routes with a replay of real-world access barriers, presents every task as an unadorned user request.

•

Extensive Evaluation.
 We evaluate several leading LLM backbones and observe a sharp performance drop from under access barriers, exposing critical reliability bottlenecks including susceptibility to look-alikes, failure to seek human intervention, policy violations, and futile retries.

2.1. 
LLM-based Web Agents

The advancement of LLMs has catalyzed a paradigm shift in autonomous agents, enabling them to interact directly with digital environments

LLM-based web agents perceive pages through the DOM, the accessibility tree or screenshots, and act by clicking, typing, and navigating.
Benchmarks in this line evaluate whether agents complete tasks on reachable resources. WebArena

, AndroidWorld 
(

: the target webpage or predetermined golden trajectory is assumed to be continuously accessible. In fact, recent cross-site benchmarks such as CAP 
(
Xu et al., 2026

2.2. 
Agent Robustness under Noisy Environment

Deploying web agents in real-world environments exposes them to pervasive environmental noise and operational obstacles. A growing body of work has examined agent robustness under imperfect environments such as intra-page perturbations, adversarial prompt injections and policy violations 
(
Ma et al., 2024
; 
Xu et al., 2024
; 
Levy et al., 2026
)
. WAREX 
(

 injects network errors, server errors, script delays, and adversarial pop-ups through a proxy. StressWeb 
(

(
Wang et al., 2026

 injects user and tool noise under a solvability constraint and reports consistent degradation across models. However, such work usually assums that the noise is transient and locally recoverable: the target resource remains reachable, so retrying, waiting, or dismissing the distraction eventually restores progress along the original path. Real-world web access, in contrast, is often obstructed by hard access barriers, such as login walls, CAPTCHAs, paywalls, regional restrictions, or removed pages, which cannot be overcome by merely persisting on the original path. 2.3. 
Self-Correction and Failure Recovery in LLM Agents

Reflection and self-refinement allow agents to repair their own errors when encountering execution failures. Early frameworks such as Reflexion 
(
Shinn et al., 2023
)
 and Self-Refine 
(
Madaan et al., 2023
)
 leverage verbal feedback for iterative refinement, while tree-search algorithms like LATS 
(
Zhou et al., 2023
)
 and web-based tree search 
(
Koh et al., 2024b
)
 enable backtracking over action trajectories. However, migrating from action-level backtracking to open-web resource recovery introduces two critical challenges that remain unaddressed by prior work:
First, 
Alternative Resource Auditing
: Finding an alternative webpage does not guarantee task success. In open-ended web environments, substitute sources often suffer from stale data, partial coverage, or unverified claims. Existing recovery frameworks lack explicit auditing mechanisms to verify whether a candidate resource is semantically equivalent and trustworthy before committing to it, making agents prone to silent hallucinations 
(
Liu et al., 2026a
)
. Second, 
Honest Abstention under Unrecoverable States
: When a blocked resource is strictly unique and no valid alternative exists across the web, forcing task completion leads to fabricated answers or unsafe workarounds. While recent works like AgentAbstain 
(
Liu et al., 2026b
)
 and Agentic-abstention 
(
Luo et al., 2026
)
 investigate when agents should refrain from acting due to ambiguous instructions or missing tools, they do not address dynamic infeasibility triggered by runtime web access barriers and exhausted alternatives. 
AgentHeal
 bridges these gaps by jointly evaluating autonomous alternative discovery, rigorous correctness auditing of substitute resources, recovery latency, and honest reporting when recovery is provably infeasible. Tab.

 positions 
AgentHeal
 against representative benchmarks.

4.1. 
Taxonomy of Access Barriers

Real-world web navigation is frequently disrupted by administrative, structural, and technical barriers that render the primary resource unreachable (
). To support systematic evaluation, we organize these barriers into nine distinct families across four broad categories based on their root causes and observable manifestations.

Temporal and structural shifts (
).

Because the open web is dynamic, content continually drifts or relocates over time, spanning three barrier families. 
Gone & moved
 (i.e., link rot) refers to pages that have been permanently deleted or relocated, producing HTTP 404/410 errors or redirect loops because the canonical target no longer exists at its original URI. 
Version change
 arises when URL structures or API endpoints are revised and legacy versions are retired in favor of updated schemas. 
Deprecation
 covers pages that remain reachable in form but whose underlying data has been superseded or stripped of attributes required by the user’s request.

User-gated access (
).

Many platforms place content behind identity or interaction checks to filter automated traffic and enforce access control, comprising two families. 
Human verification
 includes interactive challenges such as CAPTCHAs, image grids, and puzzle-based friction that demand human intervention rather than unauthorized automated bypass. 
Authentication
 covers mandatory login walls, multi-factor authentication (MFA), and session expiration, all of which block unauthenticated browsing unless the user supplies valid credentials.

Access protection (
).

Commercial and institutional platforms deploy policy-level defenses to protect proprietary content across three families. 
Paywalls
 require payment or institutional subscriptions to unlock full text or data tables, appearing in either hard or soft forms. 
Bot policies
 enforce anti-scraping rules through mechanisms such as Cloudflare challenge pages, WAF blocks, and IP-based restrictions, which typically return HTTP 403 Forbidden or follow 
robots.txt
 disallow rules. 
Legal restrictions
 include regional geoblocking and compliance walls that prohibit access from specific jurisdictions or networks.

Persistent service unavailability (
).

Infrastructure-level failures and quota limits can sever access to a host even when a resource nominally exists. We group 
throttling and server outages
 into a ninth family representing severe operational unavailability. Throttling occurs when request quotas are exhausted and the server rejects traffic with HTTP 429 Too Many Requests, while server outages involve infrastructure crashes and gateway timeouts (HTTP 502/503/504). In 
AgentHeal
, these failures persist across the entire episode on the primary host, rendering naive retries futile and requiring the agent to pivot toward external mirrors, caches, or archives.

4.3. 
Real-Web Barrier Replay Suite

Because synthetic pages cannot fully capture the noisy formatting and heterogeneous policies of live websites, we complement the sandbox with a real-web barrier replay suite. Specifically, we curate 180 real-world access barriers across 121 hosts (20 per barrier family), drawing from public sources such as dead links flagged in Wikipedia citations, paywalled articles with open-access preprints, and repositories disabled by takedown notices. Each instance is annotated with verified real-world alternatives backed by recorded archival evidence, and ground-truth answers are validated directly against these captured sources. During evaluation, recorded HTTP responses are deterministically replayed under their original URLs alongside the hosts’ native 
robots.txt
 rules and an emulated web archive.

5. 
Evaluation

We conduct a comprehensive empirical evaluation on 
AgentHeal
 to investigate how state-of-the-art web agents respond to real-world access barriers. Specifically, our experiments are structured around four core research questions:

•

RQ1 (Healing Capability):
 When obstructed on a primary target resource, can agents autonomously pivot and locate valid alternative sources to fulfill the user task?

•

RQ2 (Auditing Reliability):
 Can agents critically audit candidate alternatives to reject deceptive decoys, and honestly report task infeasibility rather than hallucinating when no valid substitutes exist?

•

RQ3 (Recovery Efficiency):
 How many interaction steps and redundant retries do agents expend before successfully recovering via an alternative path or decisively terminating an unsolvable task?

•

RQ4 (Impact of Explicit Guidance & Agentic Harnesses):
 To what extent can explicit self-healing interventions—ranging from zero-shot system prompt hints to modern execution harnesses—unlock or enhance an agent’s self-healing performance?

5.2. 
Analysis

Table 
2
 summarizes the performance of thirteen foundation models across the counterfactual sandbox and the real-web barrier replay suite. Overall, while modern web agents excel under unobstructed conditions, persistent access barriers trigger a severe degradation in task success and expose a fundamental tension between recovery capability and operational reliability.

Performance degradation and healing capability (RQ1).

On barrier-free baselines (
), most evaluated backbones achieve near-ceiling performance, averaging 89.4% across all models. However, introducing access barriers causes average task success to plummet by more than half to 43.7% in the counterfactual sandbox and 63.4% in the real-web replay suite. Strong unobstructed performance does not guarantee effective self-healing and in terms of pure discovery capability, DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash and GLM-5.3-Flash achieve the highest sandbox Recovery Rate, followed closely by GPT-6.1-Sol, demonstrating that capable backbones can actively pivot toward web archives and scholarly indexes when primary links fail. Across open-weight Qwen models, scaling parameter size from 8B to 27B substantially strengthens sandbox recovery capability from 11.1% to 45.8%, rivaling several commercial API models.

The capability-reliability trade-off (RQ2).

The reliability metrics reveals two opposing failure modes across model families. On one end of the spectrum, cautious models such as Claude-Haiku-5.5 exhibit exceptional operational discipline, achieving the highest Honest Reporting Rate alongside the lowest sandbox Violation Rate. This conservative disposition allows Claude-Haiku-5.5 to reach 56.9% overall sandbox TS despite a modest Recovery Rate of 27.6%, as it reliably abstains and escalates to the user rather than guessing when blocked. On the other end, aggressive task-completers relentlessly pursue answers at the expense of source validity and policy compliance. Most notably, MiniMax-3.1-Flash achieves a 42.7% sandbox Recovery Rate, yet its Audit Accuracy drops to 19.4%, its Honest Reporting Rate falls to 19.1%, and its Violation Rate reaches 28.7% in the sandbox and 42.1% in real-web replay. A similar pattern emerges as open-weight models scale up. While Qwen3.8-27B quadruples the recovery rate of Qwen3-8B, its Honest Reporting Rate declines from 61.8% to 54.2% and its Violation Rate nearly doubles from 7.3% to 13.6%. Such phenomenon confirms that agents frequently adopt high-ranking look-alikes or fail to disclose version discrepancies when exact copies are unavailable.

Comparison between the sandbox and real-web replay.

Comparing the two evaluation environments highlights their complementary diagnostic value. Average Recovery Rate rises from 35.2% in the counterfactual sandbox to 70.1% in the real-web replay suite, with GPT-6.1-Sol reaching 95.5% recovery on real-world barriers. This gap arises because the sandbox deliberately populates general search results with adversarial look-alikes and enforces multi-step discovery routes, whereas real-web barriers occasionally leave canonical titles or DOIs easily searchable via standard queries. Conversely, the real-web replay suite elicits substantially higher policy Violation Rates (17.7% on average vs. 11.1% in the sandbox).
When confronted with authentic paywalls, anti-bot challenges, etc., models are far more prone to attempting unauthorized bypasses than in synthetic environments.
Together, the two suites demonstrate that while agents can often locate real-world mirrors, doing so safely and without falling for deceptive look-alikes remains a major open challenge.

6. 
Conclusion

In this paper, we challenge the idealized reachability assumption underlying existing web-agent benchmarks and formalize autonomous self-healing, defined as the ability of an agent to recover from persistent access barriers while preserving the user’s original goal. To evaluate this crucial ability, we introduce 
AgentHeal
, a comprehensive benchmark designed to test goal-preserving resource recovery under realistic Access Barriers across both a counterfactual sandbox and a real-web replay suite. Our extensive evaluation on leading LLM backbones reveals a severe performance drop when agents are confronted with access obstacles, exposing the widespread failures in auditing source provenance, respecting policies, and avoiding futile retries. By bridging the gap between the mostly adopted reachable assumptions and the unpredictability of the open web, 
AgentHeal
 provide both a rigorous testing suite and diagnostic insights to inspire the development of resilient, self-healing web agents, serving as a catalyst for future research to prioritize agent safety, transparency, and adaptive reasoning in the wild.

Appendix A 
Implementation Details

Tools.

Every configuration acts through the tools in Table 
4
. The ReAct loop sees them in its system prompt and calls them in a text format; production harnesses reach the same tools through a Model Context Protocol server, with their built-in web tools disabled and their shell traffic routed through the logging proxy.

Real-Web Barrier Collection.

For each barrier family, we draw candidates from public sources where the access obstacle is documented independently of our benchmark. Specifically, our collection covers dead links flagged in Wikipedia citations (gone and moved), articles pairing a paywalled version of record with an open arXiv preprint (versioned works), gated models and credentialed datasets (authentication), subscription articles with and without open copies (paywalls), publisher and community pages displaying verification interstitials (human verification), pages disallowed by 
robots.txt
 rules (automated-access policies), repositories disabled by takedown notices in GitHub’s public notice repository (legal restrictions), rate-limited or under-maintenance public APIs (throttling and outages), and retired endpoints listed in official notices (deprecation). Every target page was requested and captured once in October 2026 to make sure its correctness.

Outcomes and Failure Modes.

Each episode is assigned a fine-grained outcome that determines its corresponding failure mode. Our evaluation procedure checks the following four conditions sequentially and terminates at the first step that assigns an outcome. The first two conditions are evaluated directly from the network request log. In the fourth step, answer provenance is determined deterministically by matching the submitted answer against embedded source values. Given this deterministic provenance, the execution trace, the ground truth, and the final report, the LLM judge then determines whether the report provides a substantive answer, properly discloses any source substitution, and accurately diagnoses the barrier.

(1)

Policy check.
 Does any request in the trace match a prohibited action pattern? If so, the episode is classified as a 
policy violation
.

(2)

Budget check.
 Was a final report submitted within the step budget? If not, the episode results in a 
retry loop
.

(3)

Abstention check.
 Does the report abstain from providing an answer? If so, the outcome depends on whether an acceptable alternative was reachable. Abstaining when a valid copy or version exists constitutes a 
premature abort
. Conversely, when no acceptable source is reachable, the outcome is an 
honest report
, or a 
user request
 if only the user can unlock the resource and the agent explicitly asks for assistance.

(4)

Provenance attribution.
 Otherwise, the submitted answer 
 is attributed to the visited resources carrying its value, 
. Deriving the answer from the requested version yields a 
faithful recovery
. Deriving it from an alternative version yields a 
disclosed version use
 if the report explicitly identifies the version discrepancy, and a 
silent version use
 otherwise. Finally, adopting a value from a look-alike source constitutes 
look-alike adoption
, while reporting a value absent from all visited resources constitutes 
fabrication
.

Appendix B 
Limitations

While 
AgentHeal
 provides a rigorous testbed for evaluating autonomous self-healing, our work has several limitations. First, to ensure strict reproducibility and deterministic source attribution, both our counterfactual sandbox and real-web replay suite operate in snapshot-based environments. Although this design prevents benchmark decay, it does not capture the continuously evolving dynamics of the live web, where search engine rankings and archival snapshots shift in real time. Second, in tasks requiring human intervention to unlock user-gated barriers, our protocol evaluates whether the agent accurately diagnoses the barrier and escalates to the user at termination, rather than modeling multi-turn collaborative dialogues with a human in the loop. Finally, our evaluation primarily focuses on text-centric and DOM-based web navigation, leaving complex multimodal interactions, such as interpreting visual-only layouts or dynamic canvas elements, for future exploration.

Appendix C 
Ethical Considerations

A potential risk of studying web access barriers is that recovery techniques could be misinterpreted or misused to bypass legitimate website protections. 
AgentHeal
 explicitly mitigates this concern by placing strict access policy compliance at the core of its evaluation criterion. Our benchmark penalizes any trajectory that attempts to defeat security controls, such as solving CAPTCHAs on the user’s behalf, extracting text concealed behind paywalls, spoofing user-agent identities, or violating 
robots.txt
 rules. Rather than encouraging adversarial circumvention, our work promotes responsible self-healing through legitimate alternative channels, including open-access repositories and public web archives, alongside honest abstention when no compliant path exists. Furthermore, all experiments are conducted within isolated local environments using either synthetic hosts or recorded HTTP replays, ensuring zero traffic load or operational disruption to live websites, and all curated replay data is stripped of personally identifiable information.

Appendix D 
LLM Usage

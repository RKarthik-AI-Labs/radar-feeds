Abstract.

The shift from traditional to generative search has fundamentally transformed online product discovery.
Because conversational recommendations synthesize choices into a few direct answers, subtle systemic preferences toward specific providers can exert profound commercial impacts.
In this work, we audit this phenomenon through two distinct dimensions: (i) 
brand popularity bias
, the systematic preference for market-dominant entities over long-tail alternatives, and (ii) 
brand familiarity bias
, the ranking advantage an entity encoded in an LLM’s parametric memory holds over an unfamiliar competitor.
First, evaluating commercial queries across major engines, we observe persistent brand popularity bias: dominant brands in the top quintile are cited 
 to 
 more frequently than the average brand within a sample of 1,176 top market players, already present in parametric memory that external web retrieval fails to mitigate.
Second, through controlled in-context experiments holding product attributes constant, we find that substituting authentic brand signatures with unfamiliar ones significantly degrades recommendation rank.
Conversely, fact-preserving content rewrites yield negligible ranking gains.
Taken together, our findings demonstrate that generative search engines reinforce a “rich-get-richer” dynamic by favoring established market leaders and imposing a parametric barrier to entry that standard content optimization cannot overcome.

Keywords: 
generative search engines, generative engine optimization, algorithm audit, provider fairness, entry barriers, buyer personas, large language models

1. 
Introduction

Chatbots and search engines powered by Large Language Models (LLMs) have shifted consumer product discovery from static URL ranking to conversational, fine-grained recommendations with synthesized justifications.
Because users ascribe high credibility to these outputs and increasingly rely on them for everyday decisions 
(
Spatharioti et al., 2025
)
, generative search exerts substantial influence over consumer choice and market exposure.
Auditing the mechanisms governing LLM-driven product recommendations is therefore of paramount importance.

Because these instruments operate at a global scale, minor systematic biases in their generation logic can produce profound market effects, as brands prioritized by generative search engines might gain distinct commercial advantages.
While model preferences may sometimes reflect genuine product superiority, it is critical to disentangle meritocratic selection from systemic model bias.
Trained on massive Web corpora, LLMs inevitably inherit and amplify pre-existing statistical biases 
(
Gallegos et al., 2024
)
.
While recent literature has audited LLMs for sociodemographic biases—including gender 
(
Armstrong et al., 2024
)
, ethnicity 
(
Salinas et al., 2024
)
, and social group 
(
Kondapally et al., 2026
)
—as well as prompting biases like persona 
(
Jack et al., 2026
)
 and cognitive biases 
(
Filandrianos et al., 2025
)
, brand-level economic biases remain underexplored.

In this study, we formalize and audit two distinct dimensions of brand bias:

(1)

Brand Popularity Bias:
 The systematic preference of LLMs toward highly popular, high-market-share brands over long-tail or niche alternatives.
Overrepresented in pre-training corpora, popular brands might enjoy a structural head start in user recommendations, which may create a “rich-get-richer” (or Matthew) effect 
(
Merton, 1968
)
. This could reduce the visibility of local or specialized competitors 
(
Klimashevskaia et al., 2024
; 
Mansoury et al., 2020
)
.

(2)

Brand Familiarity Bias:
 The advantage a brand recognized within an LLM’s parametric memory holds, at equal content, over an unfamiliar or novel entrant.
This dimension evaluates the algorithmic barrier to entry for newly emerging brands that lack pre-existing weight representations in frozen models.

Concurrently, the shift toward generative search has spurred novel visibility-seeking strategies known as Generative Engine Optimization (GEO) 
(
Aggarwal et al., 2024
)
.
Analogous to traditional marketing, GEO aims to alter page representations—adjusting style, tone, and keyword alignment—to maximize the probability of selection by LLM-based chatbots and search engines 
(
Aggarwal et al., 2024
; 
Puerto et al., 2025
)
.
However, the relative weight that LLMs assign to surface-level text optimization versus deeply memorized brand entities remains unclear.
In this work, we contrast the impact of changing brand identity against rewriting content via GEO techniques to test whether LLMs decouple brand name authority from underlying product descriptions.

Notably, the normative relationship between brand bias and recommender fairness is nuanced 
(
Burke, 2017
; 
Wang et al., 2023
)
.
On one hand, fair recommendation engines should evaluate product merit independently of brand prominence, benefiting both niche vendors through equal exposure and consumers through increased diversity.
On the other hand, established brands often correlate with higher consumer trust, quality control, and widespread user satisfaction. Prioritizing well-known brands may therefore align with average consumer expectations.
Nevertheless, LLMs are known to suffer from mode collapse 
(
Zhang et al., 2025
)
; in commercial recommendations, this behavior risks creating digital market oligopolies, suppressing fair competition, and locking users into homogeneous recommendations.
To understand these dynamics, we address two research questions:

•

RQ1:
 How do deployed generative search engines distribute visibility among brands across varying levels of popularity?

•

RQ2:
 How do manipulations of a product page, such as swapping brands/products and using GEO rewriting strategies, affect its recommendation rank?

We evaluate these questions across four distinct commercial sectors in the Italian market: 
Sport
, 
Fashion&Beauty
, 
Insurance
, and 
Travel
.
In 
Study 1
, we quantify brand popularity bias by evaluating brand text mentions and web source citations under both 
search-on
 (RAG-enabled) and 
search-off
 (parametric memory only) conditions.
In 
Study 2
, we evaluate brand familiarity bias and GEO influence by manipulating product descriptions—swapping real and invented brand names and applying GEO stylistic rewrites to test ranking stability.

Our contributions are the following:

•

We propose an experimental methodology to isolate and evaluate brand popularity bias and brand familiarity bias in generative search, accounting for web grounding, buyer personas, brand names, and GEO rewrites. The framework rests on a simple fairness criterion, equal rank at equal content, and in principle applies to any domain and model.

•

We demonstrate that commercial generative search engines exhibit significant favoritism toward popular brands across both parametric generation and web-retrieved citations.

•

We show that the same page ranks lower under an invented brand than under its real brand, at equal content (brand familiarity bias), and that GEO rewrites do not close this gap.

2. 
Related work

Auditing algorithmic systems is a cornerstone of socio-technical inquiry, tracing back to foundational frameworks in traditional search engines 
(
Introna and Nissenbaum, 2000
; 
Sandvig et al., 2014
; 
Metaxa et al., 2021
)
.
However, the paradigm shift from static ranked lists of URLs to synthesized natural language answers has introduced novel systemic risks, prompting urgent calls for updated auditing paradigms suited for generative search 
(
Kirsten et al., 2026
)
.

Auditing Generative Search Engines.

Generative search engines reconfigure the information ecosystem, impacting content creators, end users, model providers, and digital markets simultaneously 
(
Sadeddine et al., 2025
)
.
While generative search engines draw on a wider range of sources compared to traditional web search 
(
Kirsten et al., 2026
)
, their reliability remains deeply problematic.
Grounding evaluations reveal that many generative claims are unsupported by or misattributed to their cited sources 
(
Liu et al., 2023
)
.
Moreover, generative search exhibits persistent socio-technical vulnerabilities, including overconfidence, unannounced source selection heuristics, and high response stochasticity 
(
Narayanan Venkit et al., 2025
; 
Chen and Koudas, 2026
; 
Liu et al., 2023
; 
Schulte et al., 2026
)
.
These dynamics obscure accountability, making it difficult for users to evaluate the factual integrity or commercial neutrality of recommended content.

Economic Fairness and Popularity Bias in Recommender Systems.

Recommender system fairness was a critical area of study well before the advent of LLMs 
(
Biega et al., 2018
)
.
Fairness in recommendation is inherently multi-sided, encompassing both 
consumer fairness
 (ensuring users receive honest, diverse, and unbiased recommendations) and 
producer fairness
 (ensuring items or vendors are not penalized for features unrelated to product merit) 
(
Burke, 2017
; 
Singh and Joachims, 2018
)
.
Focusing on generative models, LLMs inherit statistical distribution from their massive web pre-training corpora 
(
Yu et al., 2023
)
, thus amplifying their bias to end users via authoritative and credible responses in natural language 
(
Sadeddine et al., 2025
)
, thus mining model fairness and accountability.
Algorithmic biases in LLMs span social and attribute dimensions (favoring protected demographic groups), linguistic and knowledge dimensions (disadvantaging specific languages), system and optimization dimensions (driven by interface design), and data or propensity dimensions 
(
Ma et al., 2026
)
.
Among data-driven biases, 
popularity bias
 is particularly prevalent in recommender systems 
(
Klimashevskaia et al., 2024
)
.
LLMs demonstrate a strong structural propensity to recommend head-items overrepresented in their training data 
(
Dong et al., 2026
; 
Chu and Hou, 2026
)
.
Mitigating popularity bias directly enhances producer fairness toward long-tail and underrepresented entities 
(
Deldjoo et al., 2024
; 
Dinnissen and Bauer, 2023
)
.
Under the broader umbrella of 
brand bias
, LLMs systematically prioritize global brands over regional or local alternatives 
(
Kamruzzaman et al., 2024
)
.

Entity-Level, Name, and Geographic Preference Biases.

LLM recommendation outcomes are also heavily modulated by prompt-level conditions and textual entity names.
For instance, conditioning LLMs with distinct buyer personas significantly alters the commercial brands recommended in generated outputs 
(
Jack et al., 2026
)
.
Furthermore, changing only an entity’s name reveals deep-seated model preferences. Audit literature in human audit studies established that audit testing via resume name manipulation reveals systemic racial and ethnic disparities, favoring names associated with White individuals 
(
Bertrand and Mullainathan, 2004
; 
Gaddis, 2017
)
.
Parallel name-based biases persist in LLMs, where decision-support models penalize names tied to women and racial minorities 
(
Salinas et al., 2024
; 
Armstrong et al., 2024
)
.
In product recommendation contexts, an item’s product name and document ordering substantially distort its generated ranking 
(
Pfrommer et al., 2024
)
.
Similarly, cross-cultural audits indicate that open-weight models originating from the US, Europe, and China exhibit geographic skews toward domestic brand origins when ranking shopping items, though explicitly stating the user’s location can recalibrate these rankings 
(
Betello et al., 2026
)
.

Generative Engine Optimization (GEO) and Strategic Manipulation.

The susceptibility of LLMs to textual manipulation introduces new surface vectors for commercial exploitation.
Third parties can intentionally alter product page representations to inflate recommendation probabilities 
(
Filandrianos et al., 2025
)
.
Mirroring human purchasing heuristics, LLMs exhibit vulnerability to embedded cognitive bias triggers—such as social proof, exclusivity, scarcity, discount framing, and authority claims—which significantly increase an item’s generated visibility.
This vulnerability has driven the rise of 
Generative Engine Optimization (GEO)
, wherein web pages are strategically rewritten using statistics, quotes, and authoritative formatting to maximize AI retriever selection 
(
Aggarwal et al., 2024
)
.
While some studies confirm the efficacy of GEO techniques, others observe mixed or neutral impacts 
(
Kumar and Lakkaraju, 2024
; 
Puerto et al., 2025
)
.
Crucially, the proliferation of strategic GEO manipulation creates a classic 
Prisoner’s Dilemma
 for content providers and vendors: individual entities have a strong competitive incentive to adversarially optimize their content for LLMs, regardless of competitor actions, ultimately degrading the overall utility and trustworthiness of the search ecosystem 
(
Nestaas et al., 2025
)
.
Because post-hoc data filtering is inherently complex, current mitigation strategies primarily target output generation via fairness-guided prompting 
(
Ma et al., 2026
)
, logit re-confidencing 
(
Chen et al., 2024a
)
, or in-context learning constraints 
(
Schubert et al., 2024
)
.

Position of Our Contribution.

Building upon the established literature in recommender fairness and generative search audits, this work disentangles brand bias along two distinct socio-technical axes: 
brand popularity bias
 and 
brand familiarity bias
.
While prior work has recognized general head-item dominance 
(
Chu and Hou, 2026
; 
Klimashevskaia et al., 2024
)
, we systematically measure how popularity bias restricts visibility for long-tail vendors across both inline textual mentions and web source citations.
The same approach could be applied to other entities whose name a model may know, such as news outlets or other sources 
(
Dai et al., 2025
; 
Khan et al., 2026
)
.
By explicitly evaluating models under both Retrieval-Augmented Generation (
search-on
) and non-retrieval (
search-off
) settings, we isolate the inherent bias of parametric model memory from grounding retrieval mechanisms.

Furthermore, our study extends current auditing paradigms for RAG systems 
(
Liu et al., 2023
; 
Kirsten et al., 2026
)
 by examining the initial search queries generated by LLMs.

Chen and Koudas (2026)
 observe that brands the model already knows are written into the search queries; we measure how this changes with brand popularity.
In some contexts, we reveal that popular brands secure a structural advantage early in the retrieval pipeline, shaping the initial web queries before final synthesis occurs.
Finally, whereas existing entity-manipulation studies focus primarily on isolated name swaps or stylistic rewrites 
(
Aggarwal et al., 2024
; 
Pfrommer et al., 2024
)
, our findings suggest that LLMs may evaluate brand identity in tandem with product-description coherence.
We show that unfamiliar brands face a notable entry barrier even when paired with identical product specifications, while mismatched brand-product associations appear to incur an additional coherence penalty.

3.1. 
Study 1: the audit

We study brand popularity bias, by interrogating the LLMs under different settings, investigating the mention and citation shares of brands, paired with brand popularity.

Data collection.

The prompts are recommendation questions derived from what real users search for.

We collected keyword data from DataForSEO for four sectors, 
Insurance
, 
Travel
, 
Fashion&Beauty
, and 
Sport

In each sector, we drew keywords at random across levels of search volume, with at most two per sub-topic (for example, 
car insurance

“Quale assicurazione RC moto scegliere?”

Each prompt is asked without persona, as a baseline, and preceded by each of three buyer personas of its sector.
A persona consists of three sentences describing life stage, experience with the category, and attitude to price.
The Baseline persona (A) wants to spend as little as possible; the Price variant (B) changes only the price sentence, and the Profile variant (C) changes life stage or experience while keeping the same price sentence of A.

They should be considered as controlled purchase contexts, not demographic segments.

Metrics.

A brand is 
mentioned
 when it appears in the text of an answer, and 
cited
 when its official site is among the answer’s sources.
We define 
mention rate
 of brand 
, the proportion of answers that mention

We define

 of

, limiting on the brands for which we know the official domain (683 out of 1,176 brands).
To account for the different prevalence of mentions and citations, we also normalize each rate by its average rate within the same sector and engine.
We compute brand’s popularity by

 is the mean monthly number of Google searches for its name in Italy over twelve months, obtained from DataForSEO

We measure brand popularity bias as the slope of the mention rate (or citation rate) on popularity, estimated by Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) with one point per brand. If popularity bias equals 1, an increase of Google searches by 10 times implies an increase of 1 percentage point in the mention rate, i.e. one more answer out of 100.
The 
effect of Web search
 on brand 
 is its mention rate with 
search-on
 minus its mention rate with 
search-off
.

In Study 1, we also estimate how citation, mentions, and text vary with the same prompts and different personas.
We measure 
query similarity
 as symmetric best-match cosine between their search queries, embedded with BGE-M3 
(
Chen et al., 2024b
)
, a multilingual model, since the search queries are both in Italian and English. Two answers can issue a different number of search queries, so each search query is matched with the most similar one of the other answer, in both directions.
On the answer side, we measure the similarity between the set of retrieved brands (or sources) with Jaccard index.
Answer text similarity is measured with cosine similarity between their embeddings.

With 
search-on
, we can obtain both mentions and citations.
A brand that is mentioned is 
found
 if its name appears in the text of at least one returned source, it has 
no match
 if it does not appear in sources and sources are available, and it 
cannot be verified
 if at least one source cannot be read.

When using GPT, we can also inspect the order of actions deployed by the engine.
In particular, with 
search-on
, we measure how often a brand appears in the query of first search.
Since GPT writes its first search query before receiving any web result, this behavior indicates that the engine drives the search biased by model’s own knowledge, favouring the brand that appears in the first query.

A separate model (
gemini-2.5-flash
, temperature 0) lists the brands mentioned in each answer, and we merge by hand the spelling and product-line variants of the same brand; a manual check on 30 answers found 96% of the extracted names correct (Appendix 
B
).
A brand enters the analysis of a sector if at least one engine mentions it 5 or more times. We then compute its rates for all three engines. This gives 1,176 brands, 1,159 of them with a popularity value.
As a robustness check, we repeat the analyses with a separate set of brands for each engine and with a threshold of 10 (see Appendix Table 
C.1
).
The source check uses the mentions of brands that reach the threshold in the engine itself.

In our analyses, the prompt is the sampled unit.
Intervals are 95% intervals from 1,000 bootstrap draws of the prompts within each sector, each draw keeping all the answers to the drawn prompts. Differences use the same draws.
We compare the slopes with 
search-off
 and 
search-on

C.2

Correlations across brands come with their standard 
-values.
We correct families of tests with Benjamini–Hochberg

3.2. 
Study 2: the controlled experiment

In Study 2 we address RQ2 in a controlled experiment. An audit cannot tell whether an engine prefers a brand for its name or for its content, so here we fix the pages and change, on one of them, the name that signs it and the way it is written.
We include 5 product pages in the prompt, close to the mean number of sources per answer in Study 1 (on average 4.6 for GPT and 5.3 for Gemini; Perplexity returns all the results it retrieves, 9.0 on average), and ask the LLMs to rank them (with 
search-off
), with different interventions.

Outcome.

In each call a model receives a question and five pages in a given order, answers the question citing the pages and ends with a ranking of all five (Appendix 
D
 gives the full message).
One of the five is the 
target
, the page we change. An intervention effect is the number of positions gained by the target with the intervention.
For example, if the target is third under its real brand and fourth under an invented brand, the effect is 
.

We took a subsample of 24 prompts of Study 1, six per sector (Appendix Table 
D.1
).
These prompts ask the user to choose a product and have at least seven candidate brands. Brands that are mentioned in at least 5 of the 240 Study 1 answers to the prompt, with a verified official site, that sell the product under their own name.
Among the most mentioned candidates, the most popular is set aside as the 
Better known
 signature (see below), so it never supplies a page. The five most mentioned of the remaining candidates supply one product page each from their official site.

We use the full text of each page, without menus, reviews or related products.
Before the experiment, a calibration run asked the four models to rank the five real pages.
The target is the page whose mean rank in this run is closest to 3, so that it has room to move both up and down. The other four pages never change.

We change the target in two ways.
The 
signature
 is the set of names on the page: the brand, the product and its technologies, codes and web address.
The 
text
 is the way the same facts are written.
To intervene on the signature, we turn the text of the target into a template in which every name is a placeholder, and we fill the placeholders in five ways.

Original brand Real product
 uses the true brand and product, and 
Original brand Invented product
 the true brand with an invented product.

Better known Invented product
 and 
Less known Invented product
 use, respectively, a more popular and a less popular real brand, with the same invented product. 
Invented brand
 uses an invented brand, whose 
.it
 domain does not exist, with the invented product. The five versions are identical word for word except for the names.

Better known
 and 
Less known
 brands are, respectively, the most popular among the brands most mentioned for the prompt, and the least popular brand mentioned for it; the target must be at least twice as popular as the less-known brand and at most half as popular as the better-known one.

Invented brand
 names share no distinctive part with the real ones.
Appendix 
D
 gives the full rules.

In 
Better known Invented product
 and 
Less known Invented product
, a real brand with an invented product may itself look odd to a model.
So, when the 
Better known
 or 
Less known
 brand sells a product of the same category as the target under a name of its own, we add 
Better known Real product
 and 
Less known Real product
, which sign the page with that brand and that real product.
This is possible for both 
Better known
 and 
Less known
 brands in 15 of the 24 questions (Appendix Table 
D.2
).
The product is the one of that brand most mentioned in the Study 1 answers to the prompt or, if there is none, the first suitable one on its official category page.
In the other questions the target page mentions no product, the brand sells none with a name of its own, or, in travel, the product is a holiday tied to a place.

To intervene on the text, three rewrites keep every fact of the original and change only how it is written.

Structure
 puts the decisive information first and uses headings and lists, while 
Fluency
 and 
Authoritative
 follow two methods of 
Aggarwal et al. (2024)
.

gpt-5.5
 writes the rewrites on the template, so every signature carries the same rewrite.
We keep a rewrite only if the placeholders and numbers are unchanged, its length is within 15% of the original, so that a rewrite cannot gain or lose rank by adding or cutting text, and a different model (
deepseek-v3.2
) finds no fact added or removed (Appendix 
D
).

The placeholders remove the names, but the text may still reveal the brand, for example through a typical formula or a distinctive feature.
To measure how often this happens, each model reads the target page with all names replaced, and we ask it which real brand the page belongs to (24 questions, 4 models, 3 repetitions).
The question invites a guess that the ranking task does not, so the share of correct answers is an upper bound, as in the leak test of 
Chen and Koudas (2026)
.
In the rankings, we also count how often the answer mentions the original brand of the target when the page carries another signature.

For each question, we collect 5 signatures, with 4 texts, with 10 balanced orders of the five pages, each repeated twice, on 4 models.
In addition to the three engines of Study 1 with 
search-off
, we include the open-weight 
deepseek-v3.2
 (Table 
1
).
We balance the orders so that each page appears in each position equally often.
This gives 
 rankings. The real-product versions add 9,600 rankings with the same texts, orders, repetitions and models.

Each effect is a difference in rank between two versions of the target in calls that are otherwise identical.
We average these differences in three steps, first within each model and question, then over the four models, then over the 24 questions, so that every model and every question has the same weight.
Because every signature is collected with every text, an effect of the signature is averaged over the four texts, and an effect of the text over the five signatures.
This average summarises each factor fairly only if the two do not interact, so we also estimate the effect of rewriting under each signature (Figure 
7
b and Appendix 
E
).
The 24 questions are the sampled unit: we resample them 2,000 times to obtain 95% intervals, and we test each hypothesis with a one-sided 
 test on the 24 question means, adjusting the 
-values with the Holm method 
(
Holm, 1979
)
.

H1: Generative search engines are affected by brand popularity bias.

If generative search engines are affected by brand popularity bias, the correlation between brand popularity and mention share should be positive (
H1a
), and so should the correlation between brand popularity and citation share (
H1b
). With 
search-off
 the engine can rely only on what it learned in training, so we also expect the correlation with mention share to be stronger with 
search-off
 than with 
search-on
 (
H1c
).

Figure

search-off
 and 
search-on

H2: Search queries shape the sources of an answer, but not the brands it mentions.

To see where the mentioned brands come from, we compare pairs of answers to the same prompt. If 
H2
 holds, answers whose search queries are more similar should also be more similar in their sources and text (
H2a
), while the similarity of the brands they mention should not grow with query similarity, since brands come mostly from what the model already knows (
H2b

Figure

On all three engines, the more similar the search queries, the more similar the sources, the mentioned brands and the text, and the slope is largest for sources, then brands, then text (on GPT 4.10, 3.57 and 2.30, on a scale where 0 means as alike as answers to different prompts and 1 as alike as two repeats).
The relation holds also when we remove brand names from the search queries before measuring their similarity (Appendix

H2a is supported.

When two answers have more similar search queries, the overlap between their mentioned brands also increase, almost as much as their sources (on GPT, slope 3.57 for brands against 4.10 for sources). This is hard to reconcile with brands coming only from the model’s memory. 
H2b is not supported.

5. 
Discussion

In this work, we proposed an empirical framework for provider fairness in generative search in product recommendation.
Appendix Table 
F.1
 summarizes the outcome of each audited hypothesis.

First, regarding 
RQ1
, we find persistent 
brand popularity bias
 across almost all evaluated engines and market sectors.
Generative search engines disproportionately recommend market-dominant brands, a bias already present in their parametric memory, as shown by 
search-off
.
Crucially, enabling Retrieval-Augmented Generation (
search-on
) does not mitigate this head-item favoritism.
In fact, (i) popularity bias remains unchanged between 
search-on
 and 
search-off
 conditions, and (ii) retrieval mechanisms often introduce popular brands in search queries before fetching external pages.
Consequently, external retrieval does not counteract the model’s parametric brand bias.
Buyer personas are the only factor in our audit that weakens the brand popularity bias. A plausible explanation is that a persona turns a generic request into a constrained one: the engine has to match brands to a stated budget and profile, rather than list the best-known options in the category.
Part of this may happen through retrieval, since with 
search-on
 the persona changes the search queries, which often add price terms, and the sources the engine retrieves.

Second, regarding 
RQ2
, our controlled experiments demonstrate a multi-faceted 
brand familiarity bias
.
Holding product descriptions, specifications, and layout strictly constant, authentic real-world brands achieve significantly higher recommendation ranks than synthetic brand controls.
Our findings suggest that generative search engines may penalize unfamiliar entities that lack pre-existing weight representations in training data, creating a steep algorithmic barrier to entry.

Third, our experiments do not observe significant ranking gains from Generative Engine Optimization (GEO) rewrites.
While prior work suggests that authority-style marketing can overcome brand dominance 
(
Chu and Hou, 2026
)
, those manipulations incorporated unverified or fabricated clinical claims.
When GEO transformations are strictly constrained to be fact-preserving, surface-level textual optimization appears insufficient to overcome built-in parametric brand preferences.

Taken together, these results highlight key structural socio-economic risks in generative search.
By over-representing dominant market players, generative search engines could feed a loop reminiscent of the Matthew effect (“rich-get-richer”) 
(
Klimashevskaia et al., 2024
; 
Mansoury et al., 2020
)
.
Because generative search engines synthesize choices into a few top recommendations rather than a paginated list of URLs, long-tail and local competitors may face near-total erasure.
At a global scale, exposing millions of daily users to similar or even identical, mode-collapsed recommendations risks creating digital market oligopolies and suppressing consumer choice diversity.

This systemic bias introduces a fundamental issue of market contestability.
The observed 
brand familiarity bias
 directly mirrors the item cold-start problem in traditional recommender systems 
(
Schein et al., 2002
)
.
However, unlike traditional systems where explicit product features can bridge the cold-start gap, generative search engines rank an unknown name lower, as a novel entrant would have, even when complete product descriptions are provided in-context.
Consequently, newly established brands or sustainable startups may be disadvantaged simply because they were founded after model pre-training.

Broader implications.

Our experimental pipeline offers a low-cost audit that developers of generative search engines could run before deployment, comparing the rank variations across controlled identity swaps.
The design is not specific to brands: in principle it applies to any entity whose name a model may know, such as news outlets, public bodies or authors, and prior work already finds that the name of a source can matter more than its content 
(
Dai et al., 2025
; 
Khan et al., 2026
)
.
For product vendors and marketers, our findings set boundaries on the efficacy of Generative Engine Optimization (GEO).
While marketing agencies advocate stylistic rewriting for LLM visibility, fact-preserving GEO yields marginal returns, and a new provider may gain more from becoming known in the web sources that models learn from and retrieve.
For regulators, focusing solely on citation transparency overlooks how visibility is allocated:
auditing frameworks must therefore evaluate inline entity mentions alongside source links to safeguard market contestability and antitrust oversight.
Finally, consumers relying on generative search for product discovery may be exposed to a narrower, head-biased subset of the market.
Awareness of these built-in parametric priors may help users seeking specialized, local or emerging alternatives.

Limitations.

Our empirical findings should be contextualized within several deliberate methodological trade-offs.
First, outputs from black-box LLMs inherently fluctuate across time and geography due to continuous background updates and localized indexing 
(
Chen and Koudas, 2026
; 
Schulte et al., 2026
)
.
To control for these intrinsic auditing dynamics, we fixed all prompts to a single temporal snapshot and location (Italian queries from Rome), leaving cross-cultural or regional brand disparities 
(
Betello et al., 2026
)
 outside our scope.
Second, in Study 2, we explicitly executed experiments with live web retrieval disabled (
search-off
).
While this setup omits live search reranking in the wild, this choice allows us to exert total experimental control over the in-context page, thus isolating brand bias.
Third, our persona conditioning relied on three holistic multi-sentence templates rather than an exhaustive factorial grid.
While this captures only a subset of possible user profiles, the three personas lower the brand popularity bias in the same direction, significantly for two of them.
Fourth, model audits were conducted through vendor API endpoints.
While APIs provide reproducible, programmatic access, they may omit proprietary, client-side system prompts or interface heuristics present in consumer-facing chat interfaces.
Fifth, our GEO rewrites strictly operationalized established optimization strategies 
(
Aggarwal et al., 2024
)
 under an explicit fact-preserving constraint.
We deliberately excluded deceptive or fabricated claims (e.g., fake statistics) to reflect legitimate on-page optimization, leaving whether adversarial copy can bypass parametric priors to future work.
Sixth, our evaluation focuses exclusively on generative search, precluding a direct empirical baseline against traditional search engines.
Finally, we quantified brand visibility via mention and citation frequencies without performing sentiment analysis.
While negative portrayals are theoretically plausible, manual inspection of the generated outputs revealed no instances of unfavorable brand mentions or citations; recommendations were uniformly positive or neutral.

Future Work.

Future research should extend this audit methodology along three main avenues.
First, conducting 
relevance-conditional audits
 would compare generated brand recommendations against an independent, ground-truth inventory of market offers matched for price and specification.
Second, testing real-world brand entities founded strictly after the model’s training cutoff date under 
search-on
 conditions would provide a sharper delineation between real-world brand emergence and synthetic entity penalties. Finally, expanding this framework to open-weight architectures and multi-agent retrieval pipelines could pinpoint the exact layer dynamics where parametric brand priors override retrieved in-context specifications.

Endmatter

Ethical considerations and adverse impacts

The studies query deployed commercial systems through their official APIs and involve no human participants and no personal data. Source pages were read after the calls, to check whether the names of mentioned brands appear in them (Study 1) and to build the product pages of Study 2. Each page was first requested with a declared user agent; pages that blocked this request were retrieved through a commercial unblocking service (Bright Data Web Unlocker) or from Internet Archive snapshots, and the few still unreadable were saved manually. Invented brand names were checked to have no existing or registered web domain.
Showing what moves a page in a generated ranking has a dual use: it can inform providers and regulators, and it can guide manipulation; rewriting moves pages very little, but the identity result suggests that signing a page with a known brand works, which is impersonation and is not something we recommend or tested in the wild.

Abstract

Language model performance depends strongly on the mixture of domains in the pretraining data corpus.
Popular data mixing methods select these domain weights by training many small proxy models on random mixes and then fitting a regression model to predict performance from the mix.
We recognize this practice as a domain attribution problem, where the regression measures each domain’s contribution to the target metric.
Through this lens, the standard Dirichlet sampling of proxy data mixes is a poor regression design that requires tuning and cannot support a linear fit for hundreds of domains.
Building on classical optimal experimental design, we introduce 
LinMix
, which samples mixes at the vertices of the capped simplex so that each domain is at its cap or absent.
This hyperparameter-free vertex sampling decorrelates domain effects, enabling an interpretable linear model whose coefficients are the domain attributions.
The optimal mix given by 
LinMix
 provably keeps only domains with positive attribution values and requires no search over candidate mixes.
We further establish the optimality of vertex sampling, and prove that Dirichlet sampling inflates the attribution error when its mixes concentrate around the prior data mix.
Pretraining experiments with 1B-parameter OLMo2 and 4B Qwen3.5 language models show that 
LinMix
 outperforms Dirichlet-based data mixing across reasoning, code, and math benchmarks, with relative gains of 46% on MBPP and 14% on GSM8K.
At 288 fine-grained domains, only vertex sampling supports a linear fit, and 
LinMix
 improves benchmark accuracy by 11% on average over the best nonlinear baseline.

Introduction

The composition of a pretraining dataset, drawn from an ever-growing data corpus, is one of the most decisive factors governing the downstream capabilities of large foundation models 
(
Gadre et al., 2023
; 
Team Olmo, 2025
)
.
Given the impressive gains in model training efficiency and benchmark performance obtained by curating an optimal dataset, 
data mixing
 has emerged as a well-founded optimization problem.
Naturally, a source dataset can be split into the so-called 
domain
 subsets based on their web origin 
(
Fan et al., 2024
, e.g., GitHub, arXiv,)
, semantic type 
(
Li et al., 2024
, e.g., code, math,)
, or statistical profile 
(
Diao et al., 2025
, e.g., by clustering data representations,)
.
Data mixing is then commonly formulated as a domain-weighting problem, where each domain receives a fraction of the target model’s token budget and the model is pretrained on the resulting mixture 
(
Xie et al., 2023
; 
Li et al., 2024
; 
Thudi et al., 2025
; 
Xie et al., 2025
; 
Tan et al., 2026
)
.

In this work, we study 
regression
-based data mixing 
(
Liu et al., 2025
; 
Wettig et al., 2025
; 
Ye et al., 2025
; 
Chen et al., 2026
)
, which consists of two stages:
(i) training a swarm of small 
proxy models
 on randomly sampled mixes, and
(ii) selecting the target mix by optimizing a regression model fit to predict performance from the mix.
This procedure improves both the benchmark performance and the training efficiency of language models, yet two of its design choices remain poorly understood and are addressed ad hoc in practice.
First, it is unclear from which distribution the proxy mixes should be drawn, a question we frame as the classic problem of 
regression design
 
(
Kiefer and Wolfowitz, 1959
)
, where the sampled mixes govern how much a fixed budget of proxy runs reveals about each domain’s effect.
Second, we show that popular methods do not scale to hundreds of fine-grained domains, where their Dirichlet sampling and optimization hyperparameters lack working defaults and require per-setup heuristic tuning, which becomes increasingly difficult as the number of domains increases.

Domain attribution.

In parallel, data attribution quantifies how much a model’s behavior depends on its training data 
(
Koh and Liang, 2017
; 
Ghorbani and Zou, 2019
)
.
We formalize regression-based data mixing as a form of data attribution, in which domains replace individual training samples.
A linear fit to the proxy runs thus yields a domain-level datamodel 
(
Ilyas et al., 2022
)
, whose coefficients measure each domain’s marginal contribution to the target outcome.
Consequently, a reliable regression design identifies these interpretable domain weights, an objective this work addresses from first principles.

Contributions.

We introduce 
LinMix
 as a simple and effective data mixing method whose vertex proxy sampling scheme enables selection of the optimal mix for language model pretraining via an interpretable linear model (§
3
).
Figure 
1
 illustrates its design.
Pretraining experiments with 1B-parameter OLMo2 and 4B Qwen3.5 language models demonstrate that 
LinMix
 outperforms data mixing methods relying on Dirichlet sampling across reasoning, code, and math benchmarks (§
4
).
Furthermore, our theoretical analysis reveals fundamental limitations of Dirichlet-based mixing (§
5
).
Beyond requiring heuristic hyperparameter tuning, it severely inflates attribution error whenever proxy mixes concentrate around the prior or when the prior becomes skewed (Theorem 
1
).
Finally, we formally establish the theoretical optimality of vertex sampling in 
LinMix
 (Theorem 
2
).

Experiments

Data.

We use the 200B-token WebOrganizer corpus 
(
Wettig et al., 2025
)
 built from CommonCrawl web data and cleaned for language model pretraining.
Each document is assigned one of 24 topics (e.g., science, politics) and one of 24 formats (e.g., academic writing, news), and we split the corpus into 2048-token sequences, each drawn from a single domain.
We study two mixing granularities: 24 coarse domains given by the topics alone, and 288 fine-grained domains formed by the larger half of the 
 topic–format pairs, covering 94% of the corpus (cf. App. Figure 
5
).
Removing the practically empty tail from the prior data mix reduces noise when estimating domain weights.

Language models.

Following 
Chen et al. (2026)
, we build on the open source OLMo 2 implementation 
(
Team OLMo et al., 2025
)
 to train 30M and 60M proxy models and 1B target models.
All models are trained past the 
 tokens-per-parameter Chinchilla ratio 
(
Hoffmann et al., 2022
)
 to avoid undertraining 
(
Chen et al., 2025b
)
, on 1B, 2B, and 32B tokens, respectively.
This setup is comparable to prior work that pairs 27M 
(
Liu et al., 2025
)
 and 50M 
(
Wettig et al., 2025
)
 proxies with 1B targets.
Following 
Wettig et al. (2025)
, we set the up-sampling cap to

Hyperparameters and further training details are given in Appendix 
C
.

Target benchmarks.

We optimize data mixes for three targets: ARC 
(
Clark et al., 2018
, easy and challenge,)
 for scientific question answering; HellaSwag 
(
Zellers et al., 2019
)
 for commonsense reasoning; and HellaSwag combined with PIQA 
(

In each case, we optimize the mix against proxy-model bits-per-byte 
(
Gao et al., 2021

We also optimize for BPB validation loss on the Common Pile

Kandpal et al., 2025

Theoretical Analysis

We formalize the domain attribution view of data mixing from first principles in Appendix 
E
 and summarize these results here.
Starting from linear datamodels 
(
Ilyas et al., 2022
)
, we show that the expected outcome of a proxy run is linear in its domain mix, with coefficients that average sample-level attribution scores (Proposition 
2
, Corollary 
2
).
Domain attribution thus interpolates between data attribution and a single-domain corpus, preserving the linear structure in between.
We further relate it to the Shapley and Banzhaf data values of cooperative game theory in Remark 
1
.

With a finite budget of proxy runs, estimating the domain attribution is a linear regression whose error is governed by the design 
 (Lemma 
1
).
Dirichlet sampling inflates this error as the concentration 
 grows and keeps its mixes close to the prior (Theorem 
1
).
With many domains, Dirichlet mixes cannot stay far from the prior because sparse mixes at a small 
 violate the per-domain caps and are rejected.
We therefore tune 
 empirically to a near-prior range (Section 
4.1
), for which the expected error of Dirichlet sampling is theoretically several times the lowest error possible with equal caps (Appendix 
E.3
).
This matches Figure 
3
, where both samplers lead to reliable fits at 24 domains, but no surrogate fits the Dirichlet design well at 288 domains.
In theory, vertex sampling without caps trains each proxy model on a single domain and thus estimates every domain attribution independently and equally well, which no other design can improve on (Proposition 
3
).
In practice, the caps force every mix to spread over several domains and reveal less about each of them, but vertex sampling remains the best possible design in expectation for equal caps (Theorem 
2
).

Under these idealizations, Theorems 
1
 and 
2
 help explain why the linear fit of 
LinMix
 can be more reliable than one based on Dirichlet sampling.
Dirichlet mixes concentrate around the prior, while vertices carry as much evenly spread information as the caps allow.

6 
Conclusion

We demonstrate that an interpretable linear model suffices for data mixing under vertex sampling and validate it across language model pretraining benchmarks.
We further develop the theoretical foundations of 
LinMix
 and relate data mixing to the attribution literature.
While this paper contributes to the interpretability of data mixing, our results suggest that optimizing a vertex-sampled matrix with a nonlinear model could yield even better target model performance, which we leave for future work.
We discuss limitations of our work in Appendix 
F
, most importantly the cost of training a proxy swarm and the single training run per target model.

Data mixing.

Data mixing methods differ in how they estimate domain value and spend compute to choose a mix 
(
Liu et al., 2026
)
.
Online methods adapt the mixture during training.
DoReMi 
(
Xie et al., 2023
)
 learns weights during proxy training through group distributionally robust optimization, DoGE 
(
Fan et al., 2024
)
 and 
Tan et al. (2026)
 use gradient-based updates guided by generalization or validation performance, and PiKE 
(
Li et al., 2025
)
 re-weights data sources from their gradients.
MixMin 
(
Thudi et al., 2025
)
 optimizes a weighted ensemble of proxy predictions, while Chameleon 
(
Xie et al., 2025

Data Mixing Laws 
(
Ye et al., 2025
)
, AutoScale 
(
Kang et al., 2025

Shukor et al. (2025)

Mixture optimization also extends to tokenizers 
(
Won et al., 2026
)
 and multimodal midtraining 
(
Wen et al., 2026
)
.
Adaptive search allocates new proxy runs to promising mixtures through iterative predictor fitting 
(
Diao et al., 2025
)

(
Chen et al., 2025a
; 
Yen et al., 2025
)
.

LinMix
 instead fixes its proxy mixtures before observing their outcomes, using vertex sampling to estimate domain effects across the feasible region.
This design enables a useful linear fit across hundreds of domains; combining it with adaptive search is a direction for future work.

Theory of optimal regression-based data mixing.

Regression-based data mixing methods select the target mix by minimizing a fitted surrogate model, offering little theoretical insight into the optimal mix itself

Data attribution.

Our work relates to the parallel literature on data attribution, which typically studies the influence of individual training examples rather than entire domains 
(
Ghorbani and Zou, 2019
; 
Feldman and Zhang, 2020
; 
Wang and Jia, 2023

Li and Yu, 2023

We identify a connection between regression-based data mixing and datamodels

With a limited number of runs

Data selection and filtering.

Data quality and filtering methods improve training corpora by selecting or weighting individual examples.
DataComp

Gadre et al., 2023

Methods that combine selection criteria include Meta-rater

During training, selection can adapt by coordinating multiple criteria

Ling et al. (2025)

(
Moon et al., 2025

Filtering and mixing can be combined by curating the corpus before optimizing its domain mix, as in our experiments.

Abstract

Skill optimizers adapt an agent to long-horizon tasks, such as repairing a terminal environment, by rewriting its skill, a natural-language procedure in its context, and keeping the edits that raise success on a small dev set. On such tasks success is noisy, so a gain on a small dev set does not show that an edit helps, and a success score does not show what the edit costs. We introduce BiPA, which replays an optimizer’s acceptance rule on candidates identical to its seed to measure what selection alone reports, and records each episode as a bipartite graph of turns and the text they read to trace extra input tokens to the behavior behind them. Across three optimizers, three agentic benchmarks and two models, the learned skills succeed about as often as their hand-written seed, 0.1 points higher on average. Their dev gains vanish on test: keeping the best of several candidates on at most 30 tasks reports gains this large even when none changed. They read about 16% more input tokens: the optimizers almost always add rules, half to re-check work and some to act one call at a time, and the agent follows them as extra turns, each re-sending the whole conversation. Four principles follow: accept an edit only if its gain exceeds what selection alone reports; charge it for the turns it adds, not for its length; let the editor delete and merge rules; show it what earlier rules cost. Code is available at 
https://github.com/valleysprings/BiPA.git

Introduction

On one AutomationBench task, SkillOpt’s skill passes like the seed but reads 1.42 times its input tokens, through extra turns.

A 
harness
, such as OpenCode or SWE-agent, is the agentic scaffold that runs a language model as an agent: it gives the model its prompt and tools and runs its loop until the task is done 
(
OpenCode contributors, 2025
; 
Yang et al., 2024b
)
. Built for software engineering, these harnesses now run agents on other long-horizon tasks, such as repairing a terminal environment, serving a customer or running a business workflow. An agent is adapted to a family of such tasks with a 
skill
, a natural-language procedure that the harness places in the model’s context at the start of every task. Skill optimizers automate this, starting from a 
seed
 skill 
(
Agrawal et al., 2026
; 
Yang et al., 2026
; 
Li et al., 2026
)
: an 
editor
 model reads the 
trajectories
, the logs of the agent’s runs on training tasks, and turns their successes and failures into rules, and a 
gate
 keeps an edit if the agent with the edited skill solves more tasks of a small development set, the dev set. These optimizers report gains, mostly on short, cheap tasks such as question answering, and controlled studies find the gains sparse and fragile 
(
Liu et al., 2026
; 
Wang et al., 2026
)
.

Long-horizon tasks expose three gaps. First, selection is noisy. Long tasks keep dev sets small and success is stochastic, so one run can rank two skills either way 
(
Miller, 2024
; 
Kapoor et al., 2025
)
, and keeping the best of several comparisons reports a gain even when nothing changed, a winner’s curse 
(
Smith and Winkler, 2006
)
: a reported gain does not show that a skill helps. Second, success hides cost. Each turn, one request to the model, re-sends the whole history, so extra turns raise the input tokens read over a task, and a success score does not show them. Third, rules go uncredited. The editor writes several rules per edit and the gate scores the whole edit with one number, so no rule is tested alone and it stays unknown which rules change the agent.

We propose BiPA (Bipartite Attribution; Figure 
1
), which measures these gaps from the agent’s logs without changing the optimizer. For the first gap, it replays the optimizer’s acceptance rule on candidates identical to the seed, so any gain the rule reports comes from selection alone (Section 
4.1
). For the second, it records each attempt at a task as a bipartite graph between the agent’s turns and the text each turn reads (Section 
4.2
) and splits a rise in input tokens into more tool calls, the same calls over more turns, more text per turn and longer skill text (Sections 
4.3
 and 
4.4
). For the third, we sort each added rule into one of eight categories by what it asks the agent to do and compare the categories with the behavior the graph records (Section 
5.3
).

We apply BiPA to GEPA, SkillOpt and SkillAdam on Terminal-Bench 2.1 (TB2), 
-bench
 and AutomationBench (AutoBench) with DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash and GPT-6-Luna, and test transfer by running some of DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash’s skills under GPT-6-Luna or on another benchmark. All start from one seed, a generic hand-written procedure from a public repository that asks for a fresh, complete check before any claim of completion 
(
Vincent, 2025
)
.

Learned skills succeed about as often as the seed: over all sixteen, test success is 0.1 points higher. The dev gains the optimizers report do not hold on test: the gate keeps the best of several candidates on a dev set of at most 30 tasks, where unchanged candidates would report gains of the same size. The skills gain about as many tasks as they lose, and read about 16% more input tokens than the seed, over more turns. The edits grow the skill: 91 of 97 scored edits lengthen it, about half of the rules they add tell the agent to re-check its work and none to read less, and the agent carries these rules out as extra turns, each re-sending the whole conversation. The skills do not carry over to another model or benchmark, and most of their cost goes with them.

Our contributions are:

•

We formulate BiPA, which dissects skill optimization without changing the optimizer: a selection replay gives the dev gain that selection alone reports, and a bipartite graph of turns and context traces each extra input token to the behavior behind it (Sections 
4.1
 to 
4.4
).

•

Based on BiPA, we show why learned skills do not pay off: selection alone can produce their dev gains, and their rules add turns that each re-send the whole conversation, a cost that, unlike the gain, travels with the skill to other models and benchmarks.

•

Based on this mechanism, we propose four design principles for skill optimizers (Section 
6
): accept an edit only if its gain exceeds what selection alone reports; charge it for the turns it adds, not for its length; let the editor delete and merge rules; and show it what its earlier rules cost.

2.2 
Agent and Harness Evaluation

Evaluating optimizer gains.

Controlled studies find that skill evolution is sparse and filtered by validation, with validation and test sometimes favoring different feedback 
(
Liu et al., 2026
)
; that on Terminal-Bench 2.1 harness evolution gains little on held-out tasks and does not beat simply running the agent more times at the same budget, while on long-horizon games it beats a search baseline 
(
Wang et al., 2026
)
; and that writing a harness update differs from benefiting from it 
(
Lin et al., 2026
)
. Each measures success; BiPA also measures what a skill changes in the agent.

Reruns and selection.

Repeated selection on a fixed holdout overfits it 
(
Dwork et al., 2015
; 
Cawley and Talbot, 2010
)
, and the best of several noisy estimates is biased upward, the optimizer’s curse, the winner’s curse of Section 
1
 
(
Smith and Winkler, 2006
)
. Agent evaluations thus call for repeated trials, error bars and a statement of the harness used 
(
Yao et al., 2025
; 
Miller, 2024
; 
Kapoor et al., 2025
; 
Kapoor et al., 2026
; 
Zhang et al., 2026b
)
.

Cost accounting.

Agents of similar accuracy differ widely in cost 
(
Kapoor et al., 2025
; 
Kapoor et al., 2026
)
. In agentic coding, input tokens drive the cost and vary from run to run 
(
Bai et al., 2026a
)
, and cutting tokens need not cut cost, because it changes the trajectory 
(
Weinberger and Hozez, 2026
)
. Attribution methods trace cost or failures to steps 
(
Liu and Li, 2026
; 
Shah, 2026
)
. We trace the cost of a skill to the turns its rules add, each of which re-sends the history.

We run three published general skill optimizers (Appendix

(

Finding 2 (RQ1).

Finding 3 (RQ2).
 Learned skills sharpen the agent’s behavior; they do not expand it.

An agent 
covers
 a task if one of its three test runs solves it; a skill 
expands

sharpens

Over the six model–benchmark pairs with learned skills, the seed covers 36 tasks that the no-skill agent does not and misses 13 that it covers, while the learned skills, summed over the three optimizers, cover 57 tasks that the seed does not and lose 62 that it covers. Where they gain, a task’s three episodes agree more: tool sequences more alike than under the seed for 68% of the tasks whose success rose, against 49% of the unchanged (Appendix 
F.2
).

Finding 4 (RQ2).
 Every skill learned under DeepSeek costs more than the seed.

All nine read 1.08 to 1.43 times the seed’s input tokens per task and take 1.03 to 1.22 times its turns (Table 
1
). For eight of the nine, every test run reads more input tokens than any run of the seed, and the increase holds on the tasks that both skills solve in every test run (1.08 to 1.29), so it does not come from solving different tasks (Appendix 
D.1
). Averaged over the nine skills’ test tasks, the input-token ratio 
 is 1.18; over the tasks of the seven learned under Luna it is 1.14, and over all sixteen 1.16.

Finding 5.
 Learned skills do not transfer across model or benchmark; most of their cost does.

We run Luna on the three 
-bench
 skills learned under DeepSeek, and each DeepSeek TB2 or AutoBench skill once on the other benchmark. One of the nine is above the seed, by 1.9 points: under Luna the three score 3.6 to 6.2 points below it, as the skills learned under Luna do, and on the other benchmark five of the six score 2.5 to 9.3 points below it. The cost moves with the skill: under Luna the skills learned under DeepSeek read 1.08 to 1.38 times the seed’s input tokens and those learned under Luna 1.01 to 1.05, and across benchmarks TB2’s skills read 1.24 to 1.49 times the seed’s on AutoBench and AutoBench’s 1.00 to 1.22 on TB2 (Appendix 
F.1
).

5.3 
Additional Analysis

One chain, from the editor through the agent to the harness, produces the extra cost. The gate scores a whole edit on at most 30 tasks and cannot credit a single rule, so the rules the editor writes accumulate.

Finding 6 (RQ3).
 The editor adds rules, and none asks the agent to read less.

A 
rule
 is one added bullet, numbered item, table row or example line; we sort each into one of eight categories by what it asks the agent to do, such as

Of the 59 DeepSeek edits scored on dev tasks whose parent the logs identify (38 under Luna), 58 lengthen the skill (33 under Luna); of the 337 rules they add (81 under Luna), none asks the agent to read less, half ask it to verify and 18 serialize (Appendix

). Nor does the gate filter cost: kept and rejected edits raise input tokens over their parents by about 10 and 14% on average.

Finding 7 (RQ3).
 The agent carries the rules out as extra turns.

The rules change the behavior they name. On AutoBench under DeepSeek, 56% of the seed’s writes, the calls that change state, share a turn with another write, against at most 0.3% under each learned skill, whose writes go one per turn while reads stay grouped; these serialized writes are most of the turn-splitting there. 6 
Design Principles for Skill Optimizers

The chain of Section 
5.3
 shows where an optimizer can intervene: what its gate accepts, what it charges for, what its editor writes, and what the editor sees. One design principle follows for each (Figure 
10
, Appendix 
E
).

Principle 1.
 Let the gate keep the parent unless the candidate gains more than selection alone reports. When success cannot separate the two, let cost decide, but only when the token difference exceeds what two runs of one skill differ by.

A repeated win approximates this: in simulation, requiring a dev win to repeat on a second dev run accepts 6 to 17% of unchanged candidates instead of 24 to 41%, and keeps 55 to 94% of edits that truly solve two more dev tasks (Figure 
10
a). On test, every DeepSeek learned skill reads 1.08 to 1.43 times the seed’s input tokens, while seven of the nine differ from the seed in success by less than the seed’s runs do (Appendices 
C.2
 and 
10
).

Principle 2.
 Charge each edit for the turns it adds, not for its length: count its extra turns against what it gains on dev.

Over the 59 DeepSeek edits whose parent the logs identify, the change in skill length explains 10% of the variance in an edit’s change in input tokens and the change in turns 93% (Figure 
10
b), and shorter skills can cost more 
(
Xing et al., 2026
)
. Loading the skill on demand instead of in every turn’s prefix does not remove the cost: when the prompt says to load it, SkillOpt’s 
-bench
 skill reads 1.23 times the seed’s input tokens either way (Appendix 
F.3
).

Principle 3.
 Let the editor delete and merge rules.

The three DeepSeek editors shorten the skill in 1 of 59 edits. Deleting the rules that split calls across turns would save less than it seems: recomputed with the learned skills at the seed’s calls per turn, AutoBench’s 
 would halve, from 0.17 to 0.08 (Figure 
10
c), but most of that comes from writes the rules serialize so that two writes in one turn cannot overwrite each other (Appendix 
E.2
). The editor also needs to write rules aimed at the costs that serialized writes do not explain: batch the independent reads that remain sequential, worth about one turn per episode, and return from each call only what the next turn needs.

Principle 4.
 Show the editor what its earlier rules cost: how many extra turns and input tokens they caused.

Kept and rejected edits cost alike: 73 and 76% of them raise input tokens over their parent (Figure 
10
d). Only 5 of the 59 keep dev success and add no tokens, so a gate that required both would reject almost every edit; the cost has to be avoided as the edit is written.

7 
Conclusion

Measured with BiPA, the skills learned by GEPA, SkillOpt and SkillAdam sharpen the agent’s behavior; they do not expand it. Over all sixteen learned skills of both models, test success is 0.1 points above the seed’s; the skills cover about as many new tasks as they lose, make a task’s episodes agree more where they gain, and read 1.16 times the seed’s input tokens, over more turns. Selection alone can produce the dev gains the optimizers report, and the skills do not carry over to another model or benchmark, while most of their cost does. One causal chain explains the extra cost: on small dev sets the gate cannot credit single rules, so the rules the editor writes accumulate; the agent carries their checks out as extra turns; and input tokens grow faster than turns, because the harness re-sends the history. Four design principles follow (Section 
6
); and to expand what an agent solves, an optimizer would need to reward coverage, the tasks solved in at least one of several episodes.

Limitations

BiPA is a measurement instrument. We apply it to three published skill optimizers from one shared seed, so that their edits can be compared line by line, with two models in one harness on three benchmarks of long-horizon tasks; it applies unchanged to other optimizers, seeds, models, harnesses and task families. Three limits concern the instrument and one the optimizers it measures.

Post-hoc measurement.

BiPA measures what a learned skill changed once its episodes are recorded: it attributes a change in input tokens to the behaviors that carry it, and a change in success to what selection alone reports, under the assumptions stated in Appendix 
C.1
. It does not predict which rule will add turns, and it does not explain why the model chooses a behavior; the link from a rule to the behavior it causes rests here on the edits the optimizers made and on matching rules to the behavior they name, such as serialized writes. Controlled rewrites of single rules would test this link causally, and a model of it would let an optimizer estimate an edit’s cost before running it.

Harnesses that compact their history.

The turn elasticity is derived for a harness that re-sends its history, as OpenCode does in our runs. For harnesses that compact or summarize their history, or bill cached history at a discount, the bipartite graph and the four-term split still apply, but the turn elasticity has to be measured from their episodes; our re-billing of the recorded episodes under a compacted history, with the agent’s turns held fixed, is a first step.

Decomposing success.

BiPA decomposes cost because input tokens and tool calls add up over turns and context components. Success does not: it is scored once, at the end of an episode, and splitting it over turns, components or rules needs a value function over the agent’s states. Behind a black-box model API the model’s state is not observable, so no such value function can be defined, let alone decomposed. This is the credit a gate would need to judge single rules, and the most important extension of BiPA.

Static optimizers and recursive self-improvement.

What separates these optimizers from recursive self-improvement (RSI) is not which artifact they optimize, a skill, a harness or a model, but whether the optimizer itself improves. An external optimizer does not solve tasks; its job is to make the agent better. Improving it continually means making “improve the agent, starting from a given agent” its training task, rather than training on ordinary task scores. Today’s skill, harness and model optimizers are static propose-verify-accept loops: a fixed procedure proposes a change, scores it on tasks and keeps or discards it, and optimizing several artifacts together is still such a loop. BiPA measures these loops and extends to harness and model optimizers, but they are not RSI. RSI needs every link of the chain to keep improving: the agent, the optimizer that improves it, and whatever improves that optimizer.

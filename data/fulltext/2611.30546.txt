Abstract.

AI search agents form a new and fast-growing class of web traffic.
Like a browser, they fetch pages on demand for one user; like a transcoding proxy, they rewrite what they fetch; and like a shared cache, they answer other users’ questions from the stored copy.
The rules publishers rely on, such as 
robots.txt
, 
Retry-After
, and 
Cache-Control
, were written for more familiar clients, so the agent’s vendor decides which of them to follow.
We present a black-box measurement study of how these agents fetch, read, and reuse pages, covering the search APIs of OpenAI, Anthropic, xAI, and Google and the consumer apps of ten services.
We find that OpenAI and xAI do not honor 
robots.txt
, and no service acts on 
Retry-After
.
Services silently read less than the origin served: OpenAI drops some formats, no service reads JavaScript-only content, and xAI reads only the first 0.5 MB of a page.
OpenAI, Anthropic, and xAI share fetched pages across users and largely ignore 
Cache-Control
, so answers can reflect outdated pages for 30 minutes to a day or more.
Publishers therefore lack effective control: directives are advisory and followed selectively, describe the served page rather than the rewritten copy, and bound how long a copy is reused but not how many answers it serves.
We release our code and data.

1. 
Introduction

People increasingly get information from answers written by AI, whether from an assistant such as ChatGPT or from the summaries that search engines now place above their results.
A study of ChatGPT usage found that seeking information, a close substitute for web search, grew from 14% to 24% of messages in a single year 
(
OpenAI, 2025b
)
, and Google reports strong growth in queries to its AI Mode 
(
Kopack, 2026
; 
Lang et al., 2026
)
.
With such an answer on the screen, few people go on to the sources.
In a Pew Research Center study, Google users clicked a link inside a AI summary in only 1% 
(
Chapekis and Lieb, 2025
)
.

The pages behind these answers are still read, but increasingly by the AI instead of the person.
Fetches triggered by questions to AI assistants grew more than 15-fold on Cloudflare’s network over 2025 
(
Belson, 2025
)
, and by mid-2025 such fetchers already made up about a fifth of the AI bot traffic Fastly observed 
(
Mathur et al., 2025
)
.
Publishers, meanwhile, receive few visitors in return, as AI platforms request far more pages than they refer visits back 
(
Cloudflare, 2026a
; 
Tomé, 2025
; 
Belson and Rhea, 2025
)
.
A growing share of the web is thus read not by people but by AI assistants that search and fetch pages on their behalf, forming a new class of web traffic. We refer to these as 
AI search agents
.

How these agents behave matters because the web runs on a loose contract between servers and clients: a server states its rules, and clients largely follow them.
A site lists in 
robots.txt
 what crawlers may read, uses 
Cache-Control
 to say how long a copy of a page may be reused, and sends 
Retry-After
 to ask clients to back off.
Each rule was written with a familiar kind of client in mind.
A browser fetches a page to show it to one user, a crawler fetches many pages for a search index or a training corpus, and a shared cache stores copies to serve many users.

Of these clients, crawlers have drawn the most scrutiny.
AI training has made crawling more costly: Wikimedia reports that AI crawlers favor rarely read pages, which miss its regional caches and fall through to its origin datacenters 
(
Wikimedia Foundation, 2025
)
, and prior work measures whether crawlers follow publishers’ rules 
(
Kim et al., 2025
; 
Cui et al., 2025
; 
Liu et al., 2025
)
.
Far less is known about AI search agents, which fit none of these roles cleanly. Instead, they combine three roles: they fetch pages on demand for one user (a browser), rewrite what they fetch (a transcoding proxy), and answer other users’ questions from the stored copy (a shared cache).
We therefore model an agent as three stages (fetch, read, and reuse) and ask which existing rules govern each:

Fetch. Like a browser, it fetches a page on demand for one user. Unlike a browser, it hands the page to software rather than to the user, which arguably brings it under 
robots.txt
. Like any client, it should back off when a server sends 
Retry-After
.

Read. Like a transcoding proxy, it rewrites the page before use, here into a form the model can consume. No HTTP rule governs what it may drop along the way or what it must tell the user.

, which restricts what an intermediary may change, not what the client that consumes the page does with it or tells its user.

Reuse. Like a shared cache, it may answer other users’ questions from a stored copy, which is what

An agent only partly fits each role, so its vendor can decide which of that role’s rules apply, and no rule yet says what the agent as a whole should do.
Vendors already disagree, starting with 
robots.txt
.
OpenAI states that because 
ChatGPT-User
 is initiated by a human and not used for automatic crawling, “
robots.txt
 rules may not apply” 
(
OpenAI, 2026c
)
.
Anthropic says its bots respect 
robots.txt
, with no exemption for user-initiated fetches 
(
Anthropic, 2026
)
, and xAI’s documentation does not say either way 
(
xAI, 2026
)
.

The cost of these choices falls on parties with no say in them.
Publishers face bursts of fetches with no reliable way to govern how their pages are accessed 
(
Wikimedia Foundation, 2025
)
, and the CDNs and hosting infrastructure behind them absorb the same bursts 
(
Mathur et al., 2025
)
.
Once a page is fetched, the publisher also loses control of it: the copy may be rewritten, stored, and used to answer other users, so a publisher that charges per fetch 
(
Teixeira et al., 2026
)
 cannot tell how many answers a single fetch served.
Users cannot tell whether an answer came from an outdated copy or, when a cited answer is wrong, whether the fault lies with the page, the retrieval, or the model.
Citations offer little help, as generative search engines often fail to support their statements with them 
(
Liu et al., 2023
; 
Jaźwińska and Chandrasekar, 2025
)
.
Neither publishers nor users can see what the agent did with the page.

We expose this behavior from the outside through a black-box measurement study of how commercial AI search agents fetch, read, and reuse web pages.
We test the search APIs of OpenAI, Anthropic, xAI, and Google, and the consumer apps of ten services, used through free, logged-in browser accounts: ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Grok, DeepSeek, Le Chat, Qwen, Meta AI, Perplexity, and Copilot.
We serve test pages from origins we control and log every request.
Each page carries a unique code, which lets us tie every answer to the request that fetched its source and tell a fresh fetch from a reused copy.
We validate the results on public pages and release our test pages, probe code, and measurement data (
Appendix G
).

Taken together, these results point to three gaps in publishers’ control over this traffic.
Existing directives are advisory, and vendors follow them selectively.
A directive describes the page the origin served, not the rewritten copy a service keeps.
And 
Cache-Control
 bounds how long a copy may be reused, not how many answers it may serve.
We discuss which of these gaps existing mechanisms can close and where new ones are needed (

We measure how AI search agents fetch and reuse web pages.
To test various AI services (OpenAI, Anthropic, xAI, or Google), we create a test suite, each consisting of a URL and a question about it; our results are based on the answers to each question.
Specifically, there are two kinds of test:

Controlled tests
 run against two origins we operate.
Because we control them, we can change a page, inject failures, set response headers, plant codes in a page, and read every request in the access log, so each behavior can be tied to a controlled change.

Public checks
 repeat selected tests on third-party sites.
We cannot read their logs or change their pages, so a public check can observe only the answer.
Of our findings, only two can be checked this way: file-type handling and answer staleness.

The rest of this section follows the path of a test: the services we ask (
§ 2.1
), the origins and sites we point them at (
§ 2.2
), the pages and questions we send (
§ 2.3
), and how we run and repeat the tests (
§ 2.4
).

Public sites.

Since we do not control content nor HTTP serving, here we aim to measure what file types AI search handles and whether fresh content is consistently used.
Public tests use three sets of third-party targets.
For file types, we use

For staleness, we use two sets.

Public files
 test file types: sitemaps, feeds and 
llms.txt
 files announced by popular sites.
The other two sets try to answer the same question: does a service fetch the latest page, or answer from an old copy?

Changing pages
 are homepages of popular sites that change often.
They cover many sites, but we can only tell whether the answer matches the current page or an older one.

Counter endpoints
 are public endpoints that return one value that only rises, such as a blockchain’s block height.
They cover fewer sites, but the value in the answer tells us exactly how old the copy is.
How we choose them is in

2.3. 
Probe Design

This section describes the controlled tests.
Public checks use third-party pages we cannot change, so none of it applies to them (
§ C.3
).

Pages and codes.

Our default test page is a 2 KB HTML document generated from a line-based source of frozen prose.
The same source can be rendered as plain text, Markdown, JSON, XML, CSV, PDF or DOCX (
§ C.4
).
Each experiment varies one thing about the page, such as its size (up to 2 MB), media type or response status, and holds everything else fixed.
To attribute each answer to the page, run, and service that produced it, and to show that the service read the page and did not guess or recall it, we embed unguessable codes in each page, as earlier cache-deception studies plant markers to see where content ends up 
(
Mirheidari et al., 2020
)
.
The codes are derived from the page ID and a secret known only to the origin and the measurement client.
Some experiments place five codes at known depths of a longer page, or deliver them through scripts and data blocks (
Fig. 5
).
We elaborate on how we construct these pages in 
§ C.4
.

Questions.

Each test sends a URL and one question.
Unless an experiment needs a different answer, the question asks for the page’s ID and the code at a given depth, quoted exactly (
§ C.1
).

Attribution.

Every page has a fresh ID, and its URL carries a service tag.
6
6

6

              
              
              
            In the format 
https://<origin>/<page-id>.html?<service-tag>

We match the client’s questions to origin requests by URL and time, so attribution does not depend on a fetcher’s User-Agent or source IP.
The services treat URLs with different tags as different pages (
§ E.1
), so one page can be asked about under several tags without interference.

Reuse.

To tell reuse of a page from reuse of an earlier answer, our reuse tests ask successive questions about different codes on the same page.
The later code is absent from earlier answers, so a correct answer with no new origin request shows that the page itself, not the earlier answer, was reused.

2.4. 
Procedure

Runs.

Unless otherwise stated, we test each condition in at least three independent runs, each with fresh page IDs.
We started runs between 15 September and 10 November 2026, mostly between 06:00 and 20:00 UTC, and about a quarter on a weekend.The ethical considerations are in 
Appendix A
.

Timeouts.

Each API call has a five-minute timeout; a call that exceeds it is recorded as failed.

Where we measure from.

The measurement client runs on a server in Saudi Arabia, except for our own downloads of public pages, which ran from a server in the United States.

3. 
Results

We organize the results by the three phases of a retrieval, which are also the three actions of the title: 
fetch
, how a service requests a page from the origin (
§ 3.1
); 
read

3.1. 
Fetch: Identity and Failure Handling

Servers set access rules for the clients that fetch from them, and these rules can depend on the client’s role.
Here we test how the services meet them: who they say they are, how many requests each question generates, and how they respond to 
robots.txt
 and to failures.

3.1.1. 
Fetcher Identity and 
robots.txt

Earlier work studies whether crawlers follow 
robots.txt
 
(
Liu et al., 2025
; 
Kim et al., 2025
; 
Cui et al., 2025
)
.
We ask whether the same holds for the fetches AI search agents make for a user.
A server first has to know who is fetching.
The IETF Crawler Best Practices draft recommends that crawlers identify themselves in the User-Agent header 
(
Illyes et al., 2026
)
, but the header is self-declared and cannot be verified 
(
Kaiserman, 2025
)
; Cloudflare’s Web Bot Auth uses signatures instead 
(
Conroy, 2026
)
.
Prior reports describe Grok’s fetcher as impersonating browsers and repeating requests 
(
Stackfox, 2026
; 
DataDome, 2025
)
, so we cannot rely on the declared identity.
Instead, we put a service-specific tag in every URL we send, so every origin request can be attributed to a service (
§ 2.3
).

3.2. 
Read: Content Handling and Cost

A fetch that reaches our origin does not mean the service uses the page in its answer.
We test which formats, script-delivered text, and page sizes survive the fetch, and what each costs in input tokens.

3.3. 
Reuse: Caching and Sharing

We ask whether information fetched for one query is reused later: by whom, for how long, and is this despite what the origin says.
Each test has three steps.
First, a warm-up question makes the service fetch a page we serve.
Then, after a gap, the origin keeps the page, changes it, or makes it fail.
Finally, a repeat question asks for a code that only a new fetch could return, and the origin log shows whether one happened (
§ C.6
).

3.3.4. 
Heal from Negative Caching

Negative caching is established in DNS: resolvers can retain a failed lookup and answer later queries without asking the authoritative server 
(
Andrews, 1998
)
.
We ask whether services do the same with a failed page fetch, after the origin has recovered.
Each trial starts with a fresh URL that returns an error (
503
, 
404
, or 
403
) throughout the first query; once that query ends, we switch the URL to a marked 
200
 page and ask again after a delay.
A service that remembers the failure answers with an error and sends no request; one that does not fetches the page and returns the marker.
A page that was available from the start, asked at the same age, shows whether the service reuses ordinary copies at that delay.

Failures are remembered, but differently by each service.

Anthropic did not fetch a recovered page again for any of the three errors in the whole window, up to about 11 hours, and its answers kept saying the page was broken.
OpenAI sent no request for about the first 25 minutes; after that it fetched the recovered page again, but 70% of such trials its answer still quoted the original error.
xAI read the fix promptly for 
503
 and 
403
, but for 
404
 behaved like Anthropic, with no new request at any delay (
Fig. 3
b).

4. 
Discussion

4.1. 
HTTP Mechanisms in the AI Era

Our findings show that AI search agents do not follow the assumptions that HTTP makes of browsers and crawlers.
We discuss which existing mechanisms still apply and what new ones the retrieval stack may need.

Control signals work only when a vendor chooses to follow them.

Cache directives remain the right way for a publisher to say how its pages may be reused, and one vendor already follows part of them: from mid-October 2026, Anthropic honors 
no-store
 by fetching such pages again on every question (
§ 3.3.2
).
Within a single role, such as reuse, an existing directive still applies, and following it takes a policy change by the vendor, not a new protocol.
But compliance is partial.
Anthropic follows 
no-store
 on our pages but not consistently on a public endpoint (
§ E.7
), and it still reuses pages marked 
no-cache
; OpenAI and xAI follow none of the directives.
The other signals we tested are no better: OpenAI and xAI ignored 
Retry-After
, and only Anthropic requested 
robots.txt
 (
§ 3.1.2
, 
§ 3.1.1
).
For 
robots.txt
, the standard defines rules for crawlers, which it calls automated clients, and says nothing about fetches that a user triggers 
(
Koster et al., 2022
)
.
Nothing in these specifications requires a vendor to follow the advisory signal, so a publisher gives up control over how its pages are fetched and reused to the vendors.

Directives describe the origin’s page, not the service’s copy.

HTTP’s reuse rules assume that a cache holds the object the origin served: 
ETag
 and 
Last-Modified
 check whether it changed, and 
Cache-Control
 says how long it stays valid.
Services process pages before the model reads them: none read text that a page supplies only through scripts (
§ 3.2.2
), and Anthropic’s dynamic filtering lets the model run code on the fetched page (
§ 3.2.4
).
A 
max-age=600
 on a news article governs only the original HTML, not the text a service extracts from it, and no fetcher sent a conditional request (
§ 3.3.2
).
We cannot see what a service stores, so whether a processed copy is what it reuses remains open.

Publishers cannot tell who is fetching.

An origin identifies a client by its address and its User-Agent, and both are self-declared.
OpenAI and Anthropic name their fetchers, while xAI’s documentation does not 
(
xAI, 2026
)
 and its requests carry browser-like User-Agents from many source addresses across over a thousand networks (
§ 3.1.1
, 
§ D.1
).
An operator cannot single such a fetcher out by address or identity, and log studies that attribute traffic by declared User-Agent 
(
Kim et al., 2025
)
 would miss it.
Emerging IETF work proposes to bind an agent’s identity to a cryptographic signature instead of an address 
(
Meunier and Major, 2026
)
, which would let an origin apply a policy per agent.
Any mechanism that enforces control above needs this first.

What new mechanisms may be needed.

Two gaps remain that no existing HTTP mechanism covers.

A request cannot say how much the answer depends on it.

AI search agents multiply the requests an origin receives, since one user question can trigger many fetches 
(
Sayyid-Ali et al., 2026
)
.
Some of these requests are exploratory, collecting context by following links, while others fetch the page the answer uses.

To the origin they look the same, so when it is overloaded it cannot serve the needed requests first and slow the rest.
A lightweight header that marks a request as essential or exploratory would allow this.

HTTP caches whole pages, but retrieval reuses pieces of text.

An HTTP cache stores a response under its URL and treats it as stale as a whole.
A retrieval system splits pages into chunks and reuses the chunks that are relevant to a question 
(
Jin et al., 2026
)
, which may come from pages on several origins.
Such a system may answer from a stored chunk without requesting the page, and one changed sentence makes the whole URL stale under HTTP.
How freshness rules should apply to chunks, when one answer mixes chunks from several origins, is an open problem.

4.2. 
Vendors See the Fetch, Users and Publishers Do Not

Vendors see what a fetch retrieved, when, and at what cost.
Users and publishers see little of it, and cannot respond to what they cannot see.

Users cannot see what a fetch costs.

Input cost depends on the service, not only on the page (
§ 3.2.3
).
OpenAI’s stays within a band up to 2 MB, xAI bounds it by reading only the first 0.5 MB and dropping the rest without saying so, and Anthropic’s grows with the page, to about 760 K tokens for a 2 MB page.
Anthropic offers a size limit 
(
Anthropic, 2025
)
, but a user cannot predict in advance what size is proper for their question.
A failed fetch costs too: for a 2 KB page, a call whose fetch failed used about as many input tokens as one whose fetch succeeded (
§ D.7
), so it adds to the bill without adding anything to the answer.

Users cannot see how old an answer is.

Nothing in an answer shows whether it comes from a live fetch or a stored copy.
An update reached answers after 30 minutes (OpenAI) to a day or more (Anthropic, xAI) (
§ 3.3.3
), and OpenAI sometimes answered from the old version although it had fetched the new one (
§ E.8
).
Only Anthropic’s API reports the retrieval time and lets a developer ask for a fresh fetch 
(
Anthropic, 2025
)
; we found neither for OpenAI or xAI, and none of the services shows this to the end user.
From outside, we observe only how long a copy lasts, not whether a fixed time limit or eviction ends it; either way, the answer carries no sign of its age.

Publishers cannot see how their page is used.

A fetch shows the publisher one request, not how many answers it will serve: a stored copy is shared across accounts (
§ 3.3.1
), and each later use leaves no request at all.
The origin’s log therefore counts fetches and not uses, so anything that depends on counting, such as pay-per-fetch, sees only part of the use 
(
Teixeira et al., 2026
)
.

Publishers do not know what format an AI agent wants.

There is no agreed format for web content that is friendly to AI agents.
An agent has two options.
If it wants the human view, it should run scripts and browse interactively.
If it wants the content, it should read the file directly, in a form like Markdown.
The services do neither fully.
Some retrieval stacks browse a page like a person 
(
OpenAI, 2025a
)
, yet no service runs JavaScript (
§ 3.2.2
), and OpenAI refuses Markdown (
§ 3.2.1
), the format commonly recommended for LLM input 
(
Microsoft, 2024
; 
Auer et al., 2024
)
.
A publisher therefore cannot tell which form to optimize for.

4.3. 
Limitations

Cost-constrained parameter space.

Our experiments incurred approximately $400 in API costs, which constrained the parameter space we could explore.
In particular, object sizes beyond 
, where the token costs are highest, were tested only sparsely.
Real-world HTML pages frequently exceed this threshold, so our cost estimates are conservative for the tail of the size distribution.

Two controlled origins.

Some experiments need a controlled origin: planting codes, setting arbitrary 
Cache-Control
 directives, and varying response size are not possible on third-party sites.
We use two, one behind a CDN with caching bypassed and one served directly, and their results agree, but both are our own new domains.
Agents may apply different policies to established sites, depending on domain reputation, location, or hosting provider, which two origins cannot exercise.
We complement the controlled measurements with observations on public pages and endpoints.

Public-page validation.

On third-party pages we see only the answer, not the process behind it: there is no request log and no extracted text.
A stale answer therefore shows that an old version was used, but not where it came from.
The fetcher may also receive a different edition of a page than our polling server does (by region, language, or bot rules), or an older copy from the site’s own cache.
Services differ in how they turn a page into text and in whether they quote at all, so we score each answer by outcome and report read rates per service.
The sites are popular pages that allow all four fetchers, and a stale answer is a lower bound on reuse.
We therefore use these pages to validate the controlled results, not to estimate rates.

Vendor coverage and black-box view.

We study three commercial API vendors (Anthropic, OpenAI, xAI), plus Google on public pages and several consumer apps in the appendix; other vendors may behave differently.
Behavior also changes over time: Anthropic’s mid-study shift to fetching 
no-store
 pages again on our origins shows that findings can become outdated within the measurement period, so our results are a snapshot.
We see the request log at the origin and the API reply at the client, but not what happens in between.
The dynamic-filtering result is inferred from token counts and tool traces, internal steps such as chunking, storing, and deduplication cannot be observed, and our explanation for old answers after a new fetch is a guess (
§ E.8
).
For the same reason, we cannot say why Anthropic’s fetch tool reused an old copy of the NIST beacon, which sends 
no-store
, but fetched our own 
no-store
 pages again (
§ E.7
).
We also measure how long a service keeps a page and whom it shares it with, but not how often reuse happens, which depends on how popular pages are among users (for web pages, popularity is classically skewed 
(
Breslau et al., 1999
)
) and is visible only to the vendors.

Content we did not test.

Our pages are plain text, and a question asks for a code that a plain string match can find, so the check does not depend on how well the model understands the page.
Images and video would need richer pages, and checking an answer about an image or a video would depend on whether the model can read it, which adds a variance of its own.
We also did not test how services handle ads, which is a natural next direction.
It would mean serving ads to the fetchers, and since services keep stored copies and share them across accounts, those ads could reach other users’ answers.

5. 
Related Work

Bot and crawler measurement.

Kim et al. find that scrapers comply less as 
robots.txt
 directives get stricter, and that AI search crawlers rarely check the file at all 
(
Kim et al., 2025
)
.
Cui et al. study how publishers write 
robots.txt
 rules for LLM bots and report cases where a bot did not follow them 
(
Cui et al., 2025
)
.
Sayyid et al. audit what ChatGPT and Claude can do with a page and the traffic this creates: one question can trigger 10–40 fetches, and fetched pages are reused for about 10–15 minutes from the same address 
(
Sayyid-Ali et al., 2026
)
.
Unlike studies of periodic crawlers and the controls publishers have against them 
(
Kim et al., 2025
; 
Cui et al., 2025
; 
Liu et al., 2025
)
, or of what answer engines can do with a page 
(
Sayyid-Ali et al., 2026
)
, we study the on-demand fetch itself, beyond whether it honors 
robots.txt
.
We measure it at the origin server, in request logs: what a fetcher requests, how long it keeps and shares what it fetched, how it handles failures, and what each fetch costs.

AI traffic impact on web infrastructure.

Zhang et al. inject controlled AI workloads into a Varnish deployment and find that cache miss rates double when AI traffic reaches 25% of requests and triple at 100% 
(
Zhang et al., 2025
)
.
The Wikimedia Foundation reports that AI bots account for roughly 35% of pageviews but over 65% of resource-intensive requests, with a preference for low-popularity pages that bypasses regional caches and reaches origin datacenters 
(
Wikimedia Foundation, 2025
)
.
Recent proposals have agents retrieve passages that publishers expose under their own policies, in place of whole pages 
(
Chen et al., 2025
; 
Hua and Xiao, 2026
)
.
These works establish the strain and one remedy, but do not explain which agent behaviors drive the load.
We provide that missing layer: passive server-side measurement that attributes origin pressure to specific retrieval properties.

Web proxies.

Web proxies have long been a major research area.
Caching proxies shared by many users cut latency and load 
(
Fielding et al., 2022a
)
, and transcoding or data-saving proxies compress and modify pages to save users’ data 
(
Tahir et al., 2020
; 
Agababov et al., 2015
; 
Li et al., 2016
)
.
They also raised concerns: measurements found that they removed content and ads and weakened security, and that Web Light applied by default, so publishers could opt out only with a header 
(
Tahir et al., 2020
; 
Kondracki et al., 2020
)
.
Shared caches have also leaked one user’s private page to another, by treating different URLs as the same page 
(
Mirheidari et al., 2020
)
; we test this for AI search agents with personalised links and find no leak.
AI search agents combine both roles, but differ in three ways: the user receives an answer and not the page, so neither the user nor the publisher can see what was changed; the processed page is reused across accounts; and the behavior depends on an non-deterministic model, so it can vary between runs.
We measure whether the controls publishers rely on, such as 
Cache-Control
, are respected, observed at the origin server.

Caching in the retrieval stack.

Most retrieval-augmented systems search a pre-indexed, static corpus, and the caches built for them decide reuse by embedding similarity rather than by URL 
(
Lewis et al., 2020
; 
Bergman et al., 2025
; 
Jin et al., 2026
)
.
Work aimed at the open web adds freshness: it reuses a stored answer or page only while it is likely still current, or re-fetches pages and re-indexes only what changed 
(
Mansoor et al., 2026
; 
Zerhoudi et al., 2026
)
.
Systems that retrieve from the live web at inference time focus on task completion and answer quality from the agent side 
(
Nakano et al., 2021
)
.
These works design or propose caches for agents.
We measure the caches that commercial services already run: whether an answer comes from a fetch or from a stored copy, how long that copy is kept, and which 
Cache-Control
 signals the service respects, observed at the origin server.

6. 
Conclusion

We measured how AI search agents retrieve the web, using controlled origins and public pages.
Services share fetched pages across accounts and mostly ignore the origin’s cache rules, and their fetchers differ in whether they honor retry delays, request 
robots.txt
, or read certain formats, scripts, and long pages.
A publisher therefore cannot predict when a change reaches users, and a user cannot see how old or how complete an answer is.
AI retrieval is a new traffic class: it fetches on demand like a browser, rewrites pages like a transcoding proxy, and reuses them like a shared cache, and the controls HTTP gives publishers do not yet govern it.
Our study covers how these systems behave, not their security; a natural next step is to test the fetchers’ security properties, such as TLS validation
(
Kondracki et al., 2020
)
, with the same controlled origins.

Abstract

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is vulnerable to knowledge poisoning, where adversaries manipulate retrieved evidence to influence model outputs.
Existing defenses are primarily evaluated using attack success rate (ASR), which obscures defense-induced failures and the mechanisms behind apparent attack prevention.
To address this issue, this work introduces 
Aligned RAG Evaluation
, a matched-query protocol that jointly examines clean and poisoned responses, with and without defense.
By combining matched conditions analysis, LLM-judge endorsement, and retrieval dependence controls, the proposed framework distinguishes prevented from introduced failures, target mentions from LLM endorsement, and single-passage influence from evidence composition.
Experiments on HotpotQA and Natural Questions reveal that existing defenses can introduce unexpected target-positive answers, although they can prevent some attacks, while evaluation criteria can also change defense rankings.
Our findings expose critical blind spots in ASR-based evaluation and motivate a more comprehensive assessment of RAG poisoning defenses.
The code and the evaluation artifact are available at 
https://github.com/chxhana/aligned-rag-eval
.

Introduction

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) allows a language model to generate output using evidence retrieved from an external corpus 
Lewis et al. (2020)
, which, although effective, also makes the unreliable external corpus part of the model’s input.
A RAG poisoning attacker can add crafted passages to the corpus, and those passages can be retrieved at the inference stage and steer the model toward an attacker-chosen answer 
Zhong et al. (2023)
; 
Zou et al. (2025)
.
As a countermeasure, defense mechanisms should therefore reduce the malicious influence introduced by the attacker without degrading the system’s performance on clean/benign questions.
However, most defenses of RAG poisoning evaluate whether a defense can 
prevent
 an attack from causing the model to answer desired targets 
Zou et al. (2025)
; 
Xiang et al. (2026)
; 
Zhou et al. (2025)
; 
Zhang et al. (2025)
, yet this approach is not a comprehensive evaluation.
A defense mechanism can change the system’s behavior on many queries, including malicious and clean, 
by blocking some poisoned answers, introducing new target-positive answers on other poisoned queries, or making the model abstain
.
Thus, a leaderboard that looks at only prevented attacks of a defense mechanism may not reflect the overall safety of the defended system.

To address this issue, this paper introduces 
Aligned RAG Evaluation
, a low-cost yet effective mechanism that uses matched records to evaluate the impact of defense mechanisms.
Particularly, we save four answers for each query to check the prevented and newly introduced attacks: clean retrieval without a defense, clean retrieval with a defense, poisoned retrieval without a defense, and poisoned retrieval with a defense, with all other variables fixed, including query, target, victim model, and decoding policy.
In addition, we also save the retrieved passages and rerun answers after removing these passages, which allows us to check whether the model just mentioned the target, actually endorsed it, or needed both poisoned and legitimate evidence to produce it.
This evaluation protocol is applied to PoisonedRAG passages 
Zou et al. (2025)
 on HotpotQA 
Yang et al. (2018)
 and Natural Questions (NQ) 
Kwiatkowski et al. (2019)
.
Other than the basic evaluation, we also introduce

split-knowledge
, a probe where the target answers appear only when the injected passage is combined with existing legitimate evidence in the corpus.
The enhanced protocol lets us compare two mechanisms: evidence displacement with PoisonedRAG and evidence composition with split-knowledge.

The main results of this work demonstrate the claim: 
prevented is not protected
 in RAG defenses from different perspectives.
(i) preventing attacks does not always lower risk.
Under string labels, a defense can block poisoned target outputs while introducing new wrong answers.
(ii) the choice of evaluation metric can substantially change the conclusions about defense.
On the same saved outputs, a defense that appears ineffective under string matching becomes the strongest under LLL-judged endorsement.
(iii) the retrieval score detectors fail when the retrieved context is mostly poisoned, e.g., we found that the detector intercepts 59 of 60 target-positive outputs when there is one poison in the context, but intercepts none with five poisoned.
Together, these findings argue that RAG defenses should be evaluated as interventions on the whole system.
A comprehensive defense report should show what the defense prevented, what it introduced, how it affected clean queries, and whether target strings were actually endorsed.

2.2 
Retrieval and Passage Dependence

The four-condition record shows us how a target answer is changed, and the passage dependence tells us why it changes by testing which retrieved passages the answer depends on.
To evaluate the dependence, we keep every selected query, including queries where no poison is retrieved.
For each query, we record the retrieved passages, the ratio of poisoned contexts, whether legitimate supporting evidence was present, and the model’s answers under passage-removal controls.

A target answer is defined as 
newly induced
 if the clean answer is target-negative but the poisoned answer is target-positive.
When removing those poisoned passages, we keep the remaining passages in the same order and do not retrieve any replacements.
Then, an answer is 
injection-dependent
 if the target-positive label disappears after we remove the poisoned passages from the context.
Besides, we also run two smaller control experiments:
A poison-only control that keeps only the poison passages and an individual-poison control that keeps one poison passage at a time.
These control experiments show whether the poison works only as a group, or whether one injected passage is enough by itself.

Experimental Setup

Attacks and Models.

We evaluate PoisonedRAG 
Zou et al. (2025)
 on 100 queries each from HotpotQA 
Yang et al. (2018)
 and Natural Questions (NQ) 
Kwiatkowski et al. (2019)
, using the corresponding BEIR corpora 
Thakur et al. (2021)
.
We additionally evaluate a split-knowledge attack on HotpotQA, where the injected passage induces the target answer only when combined with legitimate retrieved evidence.
For retrieval, we use Contriever 
Izacard et al. (2021)
 to retrieve the top-
 passages in the main experiments.
The split-knowledge experiments additionally evaluate BGE-base English (
BAAI/bge-base-en-v1.5
) 
Xiao et al. (2024)
.
We use Llama-3-8B-Instruct 
Llama Team (2024)
 as the primary answering model.
To assess whether our findings generalize across model sizes and families, Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct-AWQ 
Lin et al. (2024)
 using vLLM and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 
Jiang et al. (2023)
 are also evaluated.

Defenses.

We evaluate two categories of defenses: detection-based
and pipeline-based defenses.
Detection-based defenses analyze the retrieved context using graph-based features or retrieval-score statistics.
If the resulting detection score exceeds a calibrated threshold, the system abstains from answering.
We evaluate three pipeline-based defenses:
KeywordAgg, adapted from released RobustRAG 
Xiang et al. (2026)
,
TrustRAG filtering with conflict resolution 
Zhou et al. (2025)
 and our proposed prompt control baseline, which adds an instruction asking the model to be cautious about unreliable evidence.

We preserve their core defense mechanisms while standardizing context inputs, model calls, decoding, and scoring, which allows all defenses to be evaluated on the same saved retrieval contexts.
We evaluate pipeline-based defenses with the Llama
models, while the Mistral experiments are limited
to detection-based defenses because the pipeline adapters were built for the Llama-family victim.

Metrics and Statistics.

We evaluate attack outcomes using two scoring methods.
First, we report target-string matching rates for comparability with prior RAG poisoning studies.
Second, we use 
gpt-4o-mini
 as an LLM judge  
Zheng et al. (2023)
 to reassess the outputs from the main four-condition experiments.
Unlike string matching, the LLM judge distinguishes between merely mentioning the attacker’s target and endorsing it as an answer.
It also evaluates answer correctness against the reference and identifies abstentions.
To assess the LLM judge’s reliability, we conduct a blinded human audit and sample additional cases involving label disagreements, defense-induced transitions, or uncertain judgments

4 
Experimental Results

We structure our evaluation around four research questions (RQs) that examine the limitations of conventional attack success rate (ASR) in evaluating RAG security.

RQ1
: Defense Effectiveness.
Does a defense reduce overall attack success and improve system security?

RQ2
: Evaluation Reliability.
How do different attack success criteria affect conclusions about defense effectiveness?

RQ3
: Detection Robustness.
How does the number of injected passages affect the effectiveness of poisoning detectors?

RQ4
: Evidence Dependence.
Does a successful poisoning attack rely on a single injected passage, or on combining poisoned and legitimate evidence?

Together, these questions examine what conventional
ASR-based evaluation overlooks underlying successful attacks.

4.2 
Evaluation Reliability

Although TrustRAG prevents 20 target-positive outputs and introduces 20 others under string matching (in HotpotQA, 
=1), these balanced transitions do not necessarily reflect equivalent changes in actual attack success.
Target-string matching identifies the presence of the attacker’s target but cannot distinguish its endorsement or rejection.
To examine this distinction, we manually reviewed all 40 HotpotQA one-poison TrustRAG query pairs whose target-string labels changed.
Among the 20 preventions, 16 were confirmed as genuine preventions, where the undefended answer endorsed the attacker target but the defended answer did not.
In contrast, only 6 of the 20 introductions were confirmed as genuine introductions.
In the remaining 14 cases, the defended answer mentioned the attacker target while questioning or rejecting the poisoned evidence, without endorsing it.
Consequently, the audited transitions reveal 16 genuine preventions and 6 genuine introductions, compared with the balanced 20/20 transitions reported by string matching.
These findings demonstrate that target-string matching can substantially overestimate defense-induced attack success by treating mentions or rejections of poisoned evidence as successful attacks, which undermines the evaluation reliability.

In this section, we score the same saved answers with an independent LLM judge to check whether evaluation methods change the defense ranking.
Figure 
3
 shows that target-string matching and LLM judge endorsement give different results for the same answers.
On HotpotQA with one poison, the TrustRAG adapter and the undefended system both have 60 target-string positives.
Under LLM-judged endorsement, TrustRAG has 11 positives compared with 35 without defense.
On NQ with one poison, this target-positive of TrustRAG is higher than KeywordAgg (46 vs. 42) but the LLM-judged endorsement gives a lower score for TrustRAG than KeywordAgg (7 vs. 20).
The results from Fig. 
3
 show that the scoring rule changes defense methods ranking.

Remark.

The LLM-judged endorsement positive rate includes only answers explicitly labeled as positive by the LLM judge, while uncertain judgments are reported separately.
For example, on HotpotQA with one poisoned passage, the judge assigns uncertain labels to 9 undefended answers and 10 TrustRAG-defended answers as shown in Appendix 
F
 (Table 
10
).
Thus, lower judged endorsement rates should not be interpreted as evidence that all remaining answers are safe.
Unlike TrustRAG, which substantially reduces judged target endorsement, the prompt-only control increases endorsement from 35% to 61% on HotpotQA and from 42% to 55% on NQ with one poisoned passage.
On HotpotQA, string matching also detects an increase, but its magnitude is smaller (12 vs. 26).
This difference highlights the sensitivity of defense evaluation to the scoring rule.
In particular, string matching treats any mention of the attacker target as a positive outcome, even when the answer explicitly rejects it.
Judged endorsement instead evaluates whether the generated answer semantically supports the attacker’s target.

Agreement with Human Judgments.

We additionally assess the reliability of the LLM judge through a blinded human audit of 100 outputs in Table 
11
.
Human annotators provide binary yes/no labels, whereas the judge may additionally return 
uncertain
.
For target endorsement, five outputs have no defined attacker target and are therefore labeled 
not applicable (N/A)
 by both evaluators.
Among the remaining 95 outputs, the judge agrees with human annotations on 64, yielding an overall agreement of 69/100 when including the 5 N/A cases and treating uncertain labels as disagreements.

The results show that agreement is substantially higher in decisive yes/no judgments: 64/69 for target endorsement and 67/71 for abstention.
When uncertain judgments are counted as disagreements, the overall agreement is 50/98 for answer correctness and 67/99 for abstention.
These findings support the use of judged endorsement as a complementary evaluation metric, while highlighting the need to report uncertain cases separately.

Reduced Endorsement Does Not Imply Preserved Utility.

A defense may reduce target endorsement by abstaining or generating vague responses rather than providing correct answers.
To examine this trade-off, we evaluate clean-answer correctness on 200 held-out queries per dataset (Details are reported in Appendix 
C
).
The LLM judge returns uncertain correctness labels for 147 of 200 TrustRAG responses on HotpotQA and 139 of 200 on NQ.
These high uncertainty rates prevent us from drawing reliable conclusions about clean-answer utility.
Thus, although TrustRAG substantially reduces judged target endorsement, whether it preserves answer quality on benign queries remains unresolved.

4.3 
Detection Robustness

In this section, we investigate how the effectiveness of detection-based defenses changes as the number of injected passages increases.
These defenses analyze the retrieved context before answer generation and trigger abstention when the detection score exceeds a threshold calibrated on clean retrieval contexts.
We evaluate graph-based and retrieval-score-based detectors, with particular emphasis on score-gap and score-dispersion statistics.

Figure 
4
 reveals a substantial decline in detection effectiveness as the number of injected passages increases on both datasets.
On HotpotQA, both the score-gap and score-dispersion detectors intercept 59 of 60 target-positive outputs with one injected passage (
), at a 1% false-positive rate.
However, when the number of injected passages increases to five (
), both detectors fail to intercept any of the 96 target-positive outputs under the same calibrated thresholds.
A similar pattern emerges on NQ, where the score-gap and score-dispersion detectors intercept 32/64 and 36/64 target-positive outputs at 
, respectively, but only 1/92 each at 
.
These results demonstrate that detection effectiveness can deteriorate sharply as poisoned passages increasingly dominate the retrieved context.

To examine whether this degradation arises from retrieval encoding artifacts, we apply the same 512-token limit to both legitimate and injected passages.
We additionally compare dot-product scoring with cosine similarity to control for differences in embedding norms.
The detection collapse persists at 
 under these settings, suggesting that unequal token limits and embedding norms do not explain the observed failure.

The Generalization of Detection Failure.

To examine whether the observed detection failure is specific to the victim model, we repeat the experiments using Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 (Appendix 
B
, Figure 
5
).
From the results, the attack remains effective, with target-positive rates increasing from 19% on clean HotpotQA queries to 80% and 100% under one and five injected passages, respectively.
On NQ, the corresponding rates increase from 5% to 72% and 98%.
Despite the high attack success rates, the score-gap detector’s interception rate drops sharply as the poison budget increases.
At a 1% false-positive rate, interception decreases from 78/80 to 0/100 on HotpotQA and from 38/72 to 1/98 on NQ.
These results indicate that the degradation of score-gap-based detection under higher poisoning budgets is not specific to Llama-3-8B-Instruct, but also generalizes to Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3.

4.4 
Evidence Dependence

In this section, we investigate whether successful poisoning attacks depend on multiple injected passages or can be triggered by a single passage, since high attack success rates alone cannot distinguish these mechanisms.
To examine this root cause, we conduct removal controls on poisoned target-positive cases: we regenerate responses after removing all injected passages and then with each injected passage retained individually.

Single-Passage Sufficiency.

Table 
4
 shows that a single injected passage is sufficient to reproduce the target-positive label in most PoisonedRAG cases.
On NQ with 5 injected passages, 90/100 queries change from target-negative under clean retrieval to target-positive under poisoned retrieval.
Among these newly induced cases, 87 revert to target-negative when all injected passages are removed.
Moreover, among all 92 poisoned target-positive cases, at least one injected passage independently produces a target-positive response in 91 cases.
This pattern also holds across the additional victim models: a single passage suffices for all 95 target-positive cases on HotpotQA with Llama-3.1-70B (Table 
5
) and for at least 94% of target-positive cases across the evaluated Mistral-7B settings (Appendix 
B
).
These findings suggest that most newly induced target-positive outcomes depend on injected evidence, but rarely require multiple injected passages to act jointly.

Split-Knowledge and Evidence Composition.

We further investigate whether attacks can induce responses that require combining legitimate evidence 
 with an injected passage 
.
We defined the 
strict case
 as one in which the target appears when both

Table

Contriever and Llama-3-8B produce target-positive responses for 27 of 311 split-knowledge candidates, of which 23 are newly induced.
However, only 7 satisfy the strict composition criterion, whereas 17 of the 27 target-positive cases can be triggered by an injected passage alone.
We identify two strict cases with BGE cosine retrieval and none with Llama-3.1-70B.
Although the larger model remains vulnerable to PoisonedRAG, it exhibits no strict split-knowledge successes in our experiments.
Together, these results distinguish attacks driven by individually sufficient poisoned passages from those requiring the combination of legitimate and injected evidence.
Split-knowledge thus serves as a diagnostic for evidence composition rather than evidence of greater attack effectiveness.

5 
Conclusion

In this work, we evaluated the reliability of RAG poisoning defenses other than just looking at the
attack success rates.
In addition, we introduce Aligned RAG Evaluation, which compares clean and poisoned responses with and without defense on matched queries to reveal both prevented and defense-induced failures.
Experiments on HotpotQA and NQ show that defenses can introduce new target-positive answers, prompt-only controls may increase attacker-target endorsement, and retrieval-score detectors can fail when poisoned passages dominate the retrieved context.
Our evaluation framework provides a more comprehensive
assessment of defense effectiveness, reliability, and
evidence dependence beyond aggregate ASR.

Limitations

The PoisonedRAG cells contain 100 queries each, and queries share injected passages in groups of ten.
We account for this by using group-level bootstrap intervals and paired tests, rather than treating every query as fully independent.
The defenses should be read as evaluations of our adapted KeywordAgg, TrustRAG, and prompt-control pipelines, not as exact reproductions of the original papers.
This limitation does not affect the central methodological claim that a defense cannot be judged by prevented attacks alone, because protection also depends on what the defense introduces.

Ethics Statement

This work studies retrieval-corpus poisoning to improve RAG defense evaluation.
We use existing benchmarks and released artifacts, report aggregate results, and do not attack live systems.
We release code and records to support reproducible defense auditing.

Appendix A 
Related Works

Corpus poisoning attacks on RAG.

Retrieval-augmented generation makes the retrieval corpus part of the model input, so an attacker who can add documents to the corpus can influence downstream answers.
Early corpus-poisoning work showed that a small number of adversarial passages can be retrieved for many queries 
Zhong et al. (2023)
.
PoisonedRAG made this threat targeted.
Each injected passage is crafted for a specific query and asserts an attacker-chosen answer 
Zou et al. (2025)
.
Later work studies related variants, including blocker documents that disrupt retrieval 
Shafran et al. (2025)
, single-document attacks 
Chang et al. (2025)
, and poisoning in graph-structured retrieval 
Liang et al. (2026)
.
Indirect prompt injection is a neighboring threat where retrieved content contains instructions rather than false evidence 
Perez and Ribeiro (2022)
; 
Greshake et al. (2023)
.
These papers define the attacks and threat models we evaluate.
But we ask what standard success metrics actually measure once a defense is applied.
Our split-knowledge probe also differs from prior poisoning attacks because the injected passage is designed to be insufficient by itself and to work only when combined with legitimate evidence.

Defenses.

RAG defenses try to identify, isolate, or reduce the influence of untrusted retrieved passages.
RobustRAG answers from isolated passages or passage groups and aggregates the resulting answers and our KeywordAgg adapter follows its keyword-based variant 
Xiang et al. (2026)
.
TrustRAG filters suspicious retrieved content and then uses model self-assessment to resolve inconsistencies between retrieved and parametric knowledge 
Zhou et al. (2025)
.
InstructRAG and related prompting methods ask the model to reason explicitly about which passages to trust 
Wei et al. (2025)
; our prompt-only control is a minimal version of this idea.
Layered systems such as RAGuard combine retriever-side hardening with generation-time counterfactual filtering 
Kumar et al. (2026)
.
These defenses are usually evaluated by counting how many known attacks they stop.
We evaluate the same kind of question, but also ask what the defense introduces, how it changes clean answers, and whether target strings are actually endorsed.

Benchmarks and evaluation protocols.

Recent benchmarks evaluate RAG poisoning attacks and defenses across datasets, retrievers, and models 
Zhang et al. (2025)
.
AgentDojo similarly evaluates security and utility together for prompt-injection defenses in agentic settings 
Debenedetti et al. (2024)
.
These benchmarks report attack success and utility as aggregate rates.
Aligned RAG Evaluation is complementary as it saves the four matched answers for each query, so prevention, introduction, clean-side effects, and passage dependence can be inspected and rescored after generation.
This aligns with dynamic evaluation, where the goal is to examine the full results rather than just look at a single score 
Kiela et al. (2021)
.

Attribution and passage dependence.

ContextCite attributes generations to context spans and includes poisoning detection as an application 
Cohen-Wang et al. (2024)
.
RAGOrigin assigns responsibility to database texts for misleading generations 
Zhang et al. (2026)
, and TRACE detects poisoning through token-influence attribution 
Chen et al. (2026)
.
Our removal, poison-only, and individual-poison controls use the same broad ablation idea, but for a different purpose.
We use simple controls to label outcomes such as whether a target depends on injected passages, whether one injected passage is sufficient, and whether a split-knowledge case truly requires combining poisoned and legitimate evidence.

Appendix D 
Implementation and Annotation Details

Retrieval and budgets.

PoisonedRAG queries are grouped in tens; a budget 
 gives each group 
 injected passages.
The main setting uses top-5 retrieval with Contriever, corpus passages encoded at 128 tokens, and queries/poisons at 512 tokens with dot-product scores.
We also run two encoding controls: all passages at 512 tokens with dot product, and all passages at 512 tokens with cosine similarity.

Detectors.

We evaluate six detector scores: spectral connectivity, conductance, modularity, minimum retrieval score, score gap, and score dispersion.
Each detector is calibrated on 500 held-out clean queries to a nominal false-positive rate, then evaluated on 200 clean held-out queries and the attack cells.
Flagged contexts are hard-abstained.
Thresholds are recalibrated per dataset and encoding arm, but shared across poison budgets within the same arm.

Pipelines.

We adapt released components of RobustRAG and TrustRAG to a common saved-context interface.
RobustRAG uses KeywordAgg and a prompt-only control; TrustRAG uses filtering plus conflict resolution.
The source revisions are pinned: RobustRAG 
9bc35b2
, TrustRAG 
11dcea0
, and PoisonedRAG 
f660d72
.
These are component adaptations under one victim interface, not exact reproductions of every original system detail.

Independent judge and human audit.

Each distinct saved answer is scored by 
gpt-4o-mini-2024-07-18
.
The judge sees only the question, target, reference aliases with provenance, and answer; it does not see the method or condition.
It labels target endorsement, reference correctness, and abstention, with 
uncertain
 allowed for each.
The blinded human audit contains 100 outputs, oversampling label flips, TrustRAG cases, prompt-control worsening, and judge-uncertain examples.
Annotators do not see the model, method, condition, or sampling reason.

Uncertainty.

Paired defense effects use 95% bootstrap intervals over shared injection groups.
Endpoint rates use 95% Wilson intervals.

Appendix J 
Output Fidelity and Utility Diagnostics

Recovered RobustRAG outputs.

An early version of our common victim adapter saved only the first line of KeywordAgg’s final response.
The raw traces still contained the full responses, so we recovered 290 complete final outputs across 500 query/context records without rerunning inference.
This correction changes the HotpotQA KeywordAgg target counts from 23/100 and 26/100 to 60/100 and 91/100 at one and five poisons.
The isolated-passage outputs were unchanged, so the aggregation inputs stayed the same.
All KeywordAgg results in the paper use the recovered outputs.
We do not use KeywordAgg’s whole-response EM on the held-out utility set.
Among its 200 utility records, 86 abstain before aggregation and 90 of the remaining records stored only a colon-terminated preamble.
This makes whole-response EM unreliable for that condition.

Why we do not rank utility by EM.

Whole-response exact match is too brittle for the pipeline outputs.
TrustRAG often gives long explanatory answers (median 170.5 words on held-out utility queries), so exact match against short references can mark useful answers as wrong.
We therefore report judged reference correctness with explicit uncertainty (Table 
7
) instead of treating zero EM as zero capability.

Figure 7: 

Four answer types separated by aligned evaluation.
The reference is the expected correct answer for the query; the target is the attacker-chosen answer, which may differ from the reference.

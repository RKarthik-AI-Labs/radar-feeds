Learning whole-body mobile manipulation requires demonstrations that coordinate bimanual actions with locomotion across extended workspaces.
However, existing interfaces trade off direct robot control, perception and action alignment, and natural operator mobility,
making such demonstrations difficult to collect.
We introduce HALO, a human-aligned data collection system for whole-body mobile manipulation.
The system aligns the operator’s perception and action space with the robot while preserving direct physical feedback and natural locomotion.
Operators guide the robot end-effectors through kinesthetic teaching and move a low-resistance platform without the deployed mobile base while performing bimanual manipulation.
This interface enables more natural whole-body demonstrations, but the close human–robot coupling introduces discrepancies between the demonstration and deployment settings.
We address these discrepancies with deployment-aligned demonstration processing, which calibrates and temporally aligns platform motion with the deployed base response and masks and inpaints demonstrator hands in recorded observations.
Compared with Mobile ALOHA and VR teleoperation, HALO enables up to 
 faster demonstration collection while maintaining comparable or higher success rates.
Policies trained with HALO perform precise manipulation tasks, including zip-tie fastening,
and achieve a 73.3% success rate on a six-stage long-horizon mobile manipulation task involving repeated coordinated base and arm motion.

Introduction

End-to-end robot policies and imitation learning 
Zhao et al. (2023)
; 
Chi et al. (2025)
; 
Kim et al. (2024)
; 
Black et al. (2024)
; 
Li et al. (2026)
; 
Wu et al. (2026)
 have achieved strong performance across a wide range of real-world manipulation tasks. However, most prior work focuses on tabletop settings, where robots are confined to a fixed workspace. Real-world applications instead require robots to extend their workspace through mobility while continuing to manipulate objects, motivating coordinated whole-body bimanual manipulation 
Fu et al. (2024)
. Learning such behaviors requires efficient collection of demonstrations that jointly capture locomotion and manipulation.

Existing approaches to whole-body data collection make different trade-offs in preserving natural human coordination.
Indirect teleoperation methods (e.g., VR-based interfaces 
Zhu et al. (2022)
; 
Rakita et al. (2018)
; 
Honerkamp et al. (2025)
) provide less direct manipulation control and lack force feedback.
Leader–follower systems (e.g., Mobile ALOHA 
Fu et al. (2024)
) provide more direct manipulation through leader arms and kinesthetic teaching, but the spatial separation between the operator’s hands, viewpoint, and robot end-effectors introduces a perception gap and constrains natural operator mobility.

Retargeting-based approaches (e.g., UMI 
Chi et al. (2024)
 and HoMMI 
Xu et al. (2026)
) instead preserve unconstrained human motion by mapping human demonstrations to robot behavior through retargeting or whole-body control.
Taken together, existing approaches balance direct robot control, perception–action alignment, and natural operator mobility in different ways, often emphasizing one property over another.

 faster demonstration collection while maintaining higher success rates. The resulting policies achieve sub-millimeter manipulation precision and a 73.3% success rate on a six-stage long-horizon mobile manipulation task involving repeated coordinated base and arm motion. These results demonstrate that human-aligned data collection supports efficient, dexterous, and coordinated whole-body policy learning. Our contributions are threefold:

•

Human-aligned data collection system.
 We introduce a whole-body bimanual teaching system that aligns the operator’s perception–action space with the robot while preserving natural mobility, enabling efficient collection of coordinated and dexterous mobile manipulation.

•

Deployment-aligned data post-process.
 We identify and mitigate demonstrator-induced artifacts in visual observations and control, enabling demonstrations collected under tight human–robot coupling to be used for autonomous policy learning.

•

Systematic real-world evaluation.
 We evaluate the proposed system in terms of data-collection efficiency and demonstration success, and we further study the effects of different inference strategies as well as the achievable precision and efficiency of the learned policies.

Related Work

2.1 
Robot Demonstration Collection

Traditional teleoperation minimizes the embodiment gap by directly controlling the target robot, but interfaces such as SpaceMouse devices, joysticks, and VR controllers remain indirect 
Zhu et al. (2022)
; 
Rakita et al. (2018)
; 
Honerkamp et al. (2025)
; 
Wong et al. (2022)
. Kinematic replicas, such as ALOHA 
Zhao et al. (2023)
; 
Fu et al. (2024)
, GELLO 
Wu et al. (2024b)
; 
Jiang et al. (2025)
, and exoskeleton-based approaches 
Citron et al. (2026)
; 
Chen et al. (2026)
, provide more intuitive control but typically lack force feedback, although some recent systems incorporate it 
Liang et al. (2026)
; they also become cumbersome in mobile settings. UMI 
Chi et al. (2024)
 enables scalable in-the-wild data collection, while recent extensions 
Shen et al. (2026)
; 
Xu et al. (2026)
; 
Yu et al. (2025)
 incorporate force feedback, egocentric sensing and whole-body controllers for mobile manipulation; however, base motion remains difficult to control precisely, and visual and control gaps persist. Our system instead aligns the operator with the robot, providing direct visual and force feedback.

2.2 
Human-to-Robot Demonstration Transfer

Transferring human demonstrations to robot policy learning requires bridging the visual and action gaps between human embodiment and robot execution. DexUMI 
Xu et al. (2025)
 removes the human hand through inpainting and uses action replay to obtain the corresponding foreground dexterous robot hand.

 Compared with the ALOHA end-effector, we remove the handle-and-scissor design for kinesthetic teaching.  Hardware configuration includes the dual-arm manipulation interface, onboard cameras, differential-drive base, encoders, and computing system. The base is removed during data collection.

EgoBridge 
Punamiya et al. (2025)
 aligns human and robot demonstrations using dynamic time warping. Ego2Robot 
Wang et al. (2026)
 first estimates the human hand pose and then replaces the hand with the robot arm through inpainting. Human2Sim2Robot 
Lum et al. (2025)
 uses a single human demonstration together with reinforcement learning to derive robot-executable actions. In contrast, since our kinesthetic teaching interface directly provides ground-truth robot actions, we only need to mitigate demonstrator-induced artifacts in visual observations and control, without additional action annotation or replay.

2.3 
Learning-Based Mobile Manipulation

Hierarchical models separate task-level manipulation prediction from whole-body execution, using planners or whole-body controllers to coordinate the base and arm 
Zhu et al. (2026)
; 
Ha et al. (2024)
; 
Yuan et al. (2026)
; 
Xu et al. (2026)
; 
Zhao et al. (2025)
. However, many real-world tasks require tight and continuous coordination between mobility and manipulation, making such modular designs less suitable for unified whole-body control and introducing additional system complexity. Unified policies 
Wu et al. (2024a)
; 
Intelligence et al. (2025)
; 
Fu et al. (2024)
, in contrast, directly model coupled base and manipulation actions, which is particularly effective when mobility and manipulation must evolve simultaneously. We find that simply concatenating base actions with arm actions already yields strong whole-body control without additional architectural design.

3.1 
Manipulation Interface

A data collection interface should adapt to the operator rather than require the operator to adapt to the robot. ALOHA’s leader–follower design enables intuitive bimanual control, but the operator’s hands remain spatially separated from the follower end-effectors, and the operator’s egocentric view differs from the robot viewpoint. These gaps weaken perception–action alignment and direct robot control. We therefore use kinesthetic teaching to more closely align the operator’s perception and action space with the robot.

As shown in 
Fig.
 
4
, the original ALOHA end-effector is not designed for this type of physical interaction. Its handle limits the working space, while the scissor mechanism is less suitable for forceful manipulation. These features make stable grasping and force application less convenient in compact or contact-rich tasks.

So we redesign the end-effector for kinesthetic teaching by removing the handle-and-scissor mechanism and adopting a compact, high-contact design. As shown in 
Fig.
 
4
, the redesign reduces the end-effector size by more than half and increases the available pitch angle by about 
, enabling closer and more flexible manipulation while preserving direct visual and force feedback.

This close human–robot coupling makes bimanual demonstrations more natural, but it also causes the operator’s hands to appear in the robot observations. We address this visual discrepancy in the deployment-aligned demonstration processing stage described in 
Section
 
3.3
.

3.2 
Mobility Interface

The mobility interface preserves natural operator locomotion while maintaining the motion constraints of the deployed base. Mobile ALOHA’s powered base occupies the operator’s movement space 
Fu et al. (2024)
. We remove the base during data collection and let natural body motion directly drive a low-resistance demonstration platform, as shown on the left of 
Fig.
 
2
 and 
Fig.
 
6
. This allows the operator to control mobility implicitly while focusing on bimanual manipulation.

The deployment system uses an AgileX Tracer AGV 2 differential-drive base, whereas the collection platform uses passive wheels and two fixed-direction wheels placed along the drive-wheel axis. This configuration mechanically constrains the collection platform to the same nonholonomic motion space as the deployed base, preserving kinematic compatibility without post-hoc retargeting.

Figure 7: 
Visual processing.
 Our pipeline removes human hands during rapid motion, bimanual manipulation, and egocentric interaction.

Removing the powered base also substantially reduces the force required for repositioning: moving the deployment system requires 47.3 N of horizontal force and Mobile ALOHA requires 13.0 N 
Fu et al. (2024)
, whereas our collection platform requires only 2.3 N. This reduction allows the operator to walk and reposition naturally, as shown in 
Fig.
 
6
.

Two encoders mounted near the drive-wheel axis measure the motion of the collection platform, as shown in the upper right of 
Fig.
 
4
. Since the collection platform is passive and has different dynamics from the deployed base, the measured motion cannot be used directly as the deployment action. We therefore calibrate the mapping from platform motion to base velocity and estimate the response delay of the deployed base in 
Section
 
3.3.2
.

3.3 
Deployment-Aligned Demonstration Processing

The close human–robot interaction and base-free collection platform make demonstrations more natural, but they also introduce discrepancies between the demonstration and deployment settings. We address these discrepancies through deployment-aligned demonstration processing, covering both visual observations and base actions.

3.3.1 
Visual Processing

The operator’s hands are visible during kinesthetic teaching but absent during autonomous execution. To reduce this visual discrepancy, we process demonstration videos through hand segmentation, temporal mask propagation, and video inpainting, as shown in 
Fig.
 
7
. We divide each video into overlapping temporal segments and sample frames within each segment to serve as keyframes. CaRE-Ego 
Su et al. (2025)
 predicts left- and right-hand masks on these keyframes, which serve as prompts for SAM2 
Ravi et al. (2025)
. Within each segment, we first propagate masks backward from the last keyframe with a valid hand detection, then forward from the first valid keyframe, covering frames before and after the detections. We blend mask probabilities where adjacent segments overlap to smooth boundary transitions, then remove small isolated components, fill holes, and dilate the masks to cover residual hand boundaries. DiffuEraser 
Li et al. (2025)
 then combines optical-flow-guided propagation of visible scene content with diffusion-based inpainting to reconstruct the masked regions. Finally, we blend the repaired regions with the original frames. The resulting observations suppress demonstrator-specific cues while retaining the surrounding scene, reducing the visual discrepancy between demonstration and deployment.

4 
Evaluations

We evaluate our framework along three key dimensions that reflect its ability to preserve human manipulation skills while enabling efficient and coordinated whole-body policy learning:

•

Data collection efficiency.
 How efficiently can users collect successful whole-body demonstrations with our system compared with existing teleoperation interfaces?

•

Dexterity.
 How effectively can demonstrations collected with our system transfer human manipulation dexterity to learned robot policies, particularly for precise and bimanual manipulation?

•

Coordinated whole-body control.
 How well can the learned policy coordinate base motion and manipulation, especially for tasks requiring precise repositioning and long-horizon execution?

For all experimental tasks, we limit the complete data collection process to one hour, including scene reset and object preparation. We impose no constraints on demonstration variability, such as fixed initial base poses or object placements. Operators are instructed to collect demonstrations at a natural pace and to walk naturally during mobile manipulation, allowing us to evaluate whether HALO can capture natural human whole-body motion.

4.1 
Data Collection Efficiency

We compare HALO with VR-based teleoperation and Mobile ALOHA in terms of demonstration success and data collection efficiency, focusing on tasks that require tight coordination between mobility and bimanual manipulation. For a fair comparison, all methods are evaluated by the same operator under the same task settings, collection time budget, and reset protocol. The operator had no prior experience with any of the evaluated interfaces before the study.

4.3 
Coordinated whole-body control

We test HALO on tasks that require both precise whole-body coordination and long-horizon mobile manipulation.

Task description.
 We evaluate two tasks: In 
Disassemble Lego
, the policy must precisely position the base relative to the workspace and coordinate both end-effectors to grasp, separate, and place small interlocked Lego pieces. Varying connection strengths require coordinated bimanual force and motion control together with accurate base positioning, as shown in 
Fig.
 
9
.

For the long-horizon task, the procedure consists of six stages as shown in 
Fig.
 
10
. The policy first retrieves a box from the shelf, then places target objects into the box. It next lifts the loaded box with both arms, navigates to the conveyor, and places the box at the designated location. Finally, the robot returns to the shelf and repeats the procedure. The task requires coordinated bimanual manipulation, accurate base navigation, and reliable long-horizon execution across an extended workspace.

Results.

In 
Disassemble Lego
, the policy succeeds in 30 out of 40 trials (75.0%). For the long-horizon box manipulation task, the policy succeeds in 11 out of 15 trials (73.3%). Despite multiple forward, backward, and rotational base motions throughout the task, the policy consistently returns the mobile base to its initial position, enabling the full procedure to be repeated reliably. For both VR and Mobile ALOHA, policies trained with the same data collection time budget fail on both tasks, due to slow demonstrations with frequent pauses and insufficient valid data resulting from high collection failure rates.

4.4 
Ablation Studies

Inference strategies.
 Since tight whole-body coordination is sensitive to inference pauses, we further study the effect of RTC-specific execution design on policy performance, as shown in 
Tab.
 
4
. Under 
synchronous execution
, inference pauses cannot be fully eliminated, and longer execution horizons yield higher success rates because frequent replanning introduces more pauses and disrupts coordination between the arms and mobile base. 
Asynchronous execution
 achieves higher overall success by mitigating the effect of inference latency. However, more frequent replanning does not always improve performance, indicating a trade-off between reactivity and temporal consistency. Frequent replanning can reduce temporal consistency across action chunks, while longer horizons reduce responsiveness to new observations. Our results show that an intermediate replanning horizon provides the best balance.

5 
Conclusion

In this work, we presented HALO, a human-aligned data collection system for whole-body mobile manipulation. Our key insight is that the robot interface should adapt to human capabilities by aligning perception and manipulation while preserving natural locomotion. This design enables efficient collection of coordinated demonstrations, while demonstrator decoupling mitigates the visual and control shifts introduced by close human–robot coupling. Building on this alignment, future work could incorporate active perception 
Xiong et al. (2025)
; 
Xu et al. (2026)
 to adapt the robot viewpoint during manipulation and more closely reflect human visual behavior. Across rapid mobile manipulation, dexterous tasks, and long-horizon behaviors, HALO demonstrates that human-aligned interfaces provide an effective foundation for efficient data collection and robust whole-body policy learning.

Appendix

Appendix A 
Baseline Implementations

VR teleoperation.

We use XRoboToolkit 
Zhao et al. (2026)
 to receive pose updates from the VR controllers. For each arm, we map the controller’s relative translation and rotation to a relative motion expressed in the robot end-effector frame. We use the resulting target pose to solve inverse kinematics and send the joint commands to the arms.

Mobile ALOHA.

We use the AgileX Robotics setup for our Mobile ALOHA baseline 
Fu et al. (2024)
. During demonstration collection, we power off the mobile base so that the operator can move it manually while teleoperating the arms through the leader arms. External encoders measure the resulting base motion, which can be used to construct the base action labels.

Training uses a global batch size of 128, gradient clipping at a
global norm of 1.0, and an exponential moving average with decay 0.999.
The policy predicts 25 action steps per observation. For RTC training 
Black et al. (2025)
,
we randomly select 0 to 9 initial actions as a clean prefix,
which conditions the remaining predictions and is excluded from the
flow-matching loss. Per-step diffusion-time conditioning distinguishes
the clean prefix from the noisy actions.

State and action representation.

Each action concatenates the left-arm joint targets and gripper
command, the corresponding right-arm commands, and the base linear
and angular velocities, yielding a 16-dimensional vector. The state
uses the same ordering, combining the measured arm joint positions
and gripper states with the previous base velocity command.
The one-step history in Table 
5
 supplies this
previous base command, providing motion context for the mobile base.
Historical actions are removed from the prediction targets, leaving
a 25-step action horizon. This history is separate from the RTC prefix,
which conditions predictions on actions retained from the preceding
action chunk.

Appendix C 
Extra Hardware Detials

Wheel odometry.

Unlike Mobile ALOHA 
Fu et al. (2024)
, which requires a custom wheel odometry assembly with modified DYNAMIXEL motors and 3D-printed components, we adopt an off-the-shelf industrial measuring wheel assembly. As shown in 
Fig.
 
12
, the assembly integrates an OMRON rotary encoder, a measuring wheel, and a spring-loaded mounting mechanism, eliminating the need for custom fabrication and motor modifications. We use a 300-pulse-per-revolution encoder, while higher-resolution variants are available, offering a resolution comparable to or exceeding that of the DYNAMIXEL encoder used in Mobile ALOHA.

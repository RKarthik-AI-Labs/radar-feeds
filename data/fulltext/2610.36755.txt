Abstract

Gradient-based data attribution has become an important tool for high-quality data selection in the instruction fine-tuning of large language models (LLMs), as it estimates the contribution of training examples through their influence on model optimization. However, we find that existing global gradient attribution methods suffer from surface-form bias: minor changes to semantically irrelevant details, such as wording or formatting, can produce highly inconsistent attribution scores even when the underlying content remains unchanged. To address this issue, we propose Content-Invariant Gradient Attribution (CIGA), a robust framework for instruction data selection. CIGA first identifies layers whose gradients are both influential for attribution and stable under semantics-preserving surface-form perturbations, thereby reducing formatting noise in gradient features. It then dynamically fuses these attribution signals with semantic representations of the input, combining optimization-level influence with content-level information. Extensive experiments across multiple benchmarks show that CIGA produces more stable rankings under surface-form perturbations while preserving downstream capabilities and improving average performance. CIGA also reduces attribution cost, offering a robust and efficient approach to data selection for LLM instruction tuning.

Introduction

Figure 1: 
Global-gradient attribution can be sensitive to semantically irrelevant surface details: minor wording or formatting changes produce different gradient representations and low-overlap selected subsets.

Instruction tuning increasingly relies on large, automatically assembled corpora, which often contain redundant, noisy, or weakly informative examples. Recent work shows that models trained on carefully selected subsets can match or even outperform those trained on substantially larger datasets 
(
Albalak et al., 2024
; 
Xia et al., 2024
)
, highlighting target-aware data selection as a key component of efficient LLM post-training.

Targeted gradient-attribution methods rank training examples by the alignment between their parameter updates and those induced by target validation data 
(
Xia et al., 2024
)
. This optimization-aware signal is useful, but global gradients also encode semantically irrelevant surface details such as wording and layout. As illustrated in Figure 
1
, making minor surface-form changes to a validation example while preserving its content can change the selected training subset. We refer to this failure mode as 
surface-form bias
: attribution should respond primarily to the underlying task content rather than incidental forms of expression.

Semantic retrieval offers a complementary content-level signal 
(
Yang et al., 2025
)
, but similarity alone does not indicate whether a candidate induces a useful model update 
(
Jiang et al., 2025
)
. Robust targeted selection therefore requires both optimization-level relevance and reduced sensitivity to semantics-preserving surface variations.

We propose 
Content-Invariant Gradient Attribution (CIGA).
 Motivated by evidence that Transformer layers encode different types of information 
(
Meng et al., 2022
; 
Valeriani et al., 2023
)
, CIGA searches for a layer whose update features are strong yet stable across variants that differ only in semantically irrelevant surface form. It then computes train-validation gradient similarity only at this consistent influential layer. Finally, task-adaptive Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) uses semantic similarity as a calibration signal rather than a replacement for gradient attribution. As summarized in Figure 
2
, the resulting pipeline uses one warm-up checkpoint and one selected layer, improving cross-variant consistency while preserving downstream utility. Importantly, CIGA imposes this invariance only during data attribution; it neither rewrites nor removes the original format diversity of the selected training data.

Figure 2: 
Overview of CIGA: (1) warm-up fine-tuning; (2) consistent influential layer selection across prompt variants; (3) task-adaptive fusion of gradient and semantic rankings; and (4) Top-
 selection and retraining.

The main contributions are as follows:

(i) Characterizing surface-form bias in data attribution:
 We show that minor, semantics-preserving changes to validation examples can produce substantially different global-gradient rankings and selected subsets.

(ii) Consistent influential layer selection:

We introduce a layer score that balances update strength against cross-variant dispersion, yielding gradient features that are less sensitive to semantically irrelevant surface form.

(iii) Robust and effective data selection:
 CIGA calibrates the selected-layer gradient ranking with semantic information. Across five benchmarks, it improves selection consistency while maintaining downstream capabilities and improving average performance, with lower attribution cost than multi-checkpoint gradient selection.

2 
Related Work

2.1 
Data Attribution

Training data attribution quantifies each training example’s contribution to model predictions or performance. Classical Influence Functions (IF) 
(
Koh and Liang, 2017
; 
Barshan et al., 2020
)
 approximate the effect of removing a data point via the inverse Hessian, while recent work develops scalable approximations for LLMs 
(
Schioppa et al., 2022
; 
Park et al., 2023
)
. First-order methods such as TracIn 
(
Pruthi et al., 2020
)
 and LESS 
(
Xia et al., 2024
)
 instead score training examples by their gradient similarity to validation examples, without the Hessian.

In addition to gradient-based approaches, another crucial direction is introducing Data Shapley based on cooperative game theory 
(
Ghorbani and Zou, 2019
; 
Fotouhi et al., 2024
)
 to guarantee theoretical fairness in data value allocation. In-Run Data Shapley 
(
Wang et al., 2025a
)
 utilizes Taylor expansion within a single backward pass to achieve efficient approximate computation.

Regarding evaluation paradigms, the theoretical validity of data attribution algorithms typically requires rigorous verification through Leave-One-Out (LOO) retraining. However, due to the high computational cost of repeatedly retraining LLMs, current research also relies on utility evaluations based on downstream tasks. Prior work evaluates attribution scores in applications such as hallucination detection, fact tracing, and high-quality data selection 
(
Chen et al., 2024
; 
Wu et al., 2024
)
.

2.2 
Data Selection

For instruction tuning, data selection aims to identify a compact subset that maximizes performance on the target task. Gradient-based methods rank candidates using optimization signals. Recent work improves scalability through influence distillation 
(
Nikdan et al., 2025
)
 and early-stage gradient clustering 
(
Wang et al., 2025b
)
.
Beyond computational efficiency, inherent noise similarly constrains the performance of gradient methods. G-SNR 
(
Yuan et al., 2026
)
 uses gradient decay and variance during fine-tuning to estimate learning value and uncertainty, respectively.
However, they generally rely only on global gradient features and struggle to capture the semantic relationships within the data, causing the selection results to lack robustness against variations in expression forms.

Complementary methods use semantic similarity, model-internal signals, or information-theoretic criteria for data selection 
(
Yang et al., 2025
; 
Chen et al., 2026
; 
Duan et al., 2025
; 
Dong et al., 2026
)
. T-SHIRT 
(
Fu et al., 2025
)
 exclusively focuses on highly informative key tokens within the response sequence and introduces neighborhood perturbations to filter out redundant samples that rely on spurious lexical features. Meanwhile, methods based on the ICL single-shot loss difference 
(
Jiang et al., 2025
)
 have also been proven effective in quantifying the value of specific data in bridging model capability gaps.

Overall, global-gradient methods capture update utility but are sensitive to surface-form variations, whereas semantic and internal-signal methods capture content but are less tied to gradient dynamics.

Selection Consistency Across Variants

To evaluate the sensitivity of gradient-based data selection to semantically irrelevant surface-form variations, we perform data selection using five variants of the same validation data and compute the mean pairwise Jaccard similarity among the resulting Top-5% training subsets.

Overall Advantage over Global-Gradient Baselines.
 As shown in Table 
1
, CIGA achieves average overlap rates of 58.33% and 48.68% on LLaMA-2-7B and Qwen3-4B-Base, respectively. Compared with the best-performing global-gradient baseline on each backbone, these results represent improvements of 9.27 and 8.65 percentage points. The gains span diverse tasks, including MMLU, BBH, GSM8K, and HellaSwag, showing that CIGA produces more consistent selections across model architectures and tasks.

Downstream Performance Results

To evaluate the downstream utility of the selected data, we fine-tune the base models on the Top-5% training subsets selected by different methods and evaluate them on five target tasks. In Table 
2
, “Full” denotes fine-tuning on the complete dataset.

Overall Performance.
 CIGA achieves the highest macro-average among all data selection methods on both backbones, exceeding Random by 2.11 and 1.04 percentage points on LLaMA-2-7B and Qwen3-4B-Base, respectively. Paired tests on the run-level macro-averages show significant gains against all evaluated selection baselines on LLaMA-2-7B and against Random, Cos, and SelectIT on Qwen3-4B-Base (
). Under the challenging Top-5% setting, these gains are comparable to those reported in recent data selection studies 
(
Wang et al., 2025b
; 
Chen et al., 2026
; 
Jiang et al., 2025

, while the consistency results above show that CIGA improves robustness without sacrificing downstream utility.

Complementarity of Semantic and Gradient Signals.
 The strong performance of Cos on GSM8K demonstrates the value of semantic similarity for mathematical word problems with prominent structural and semantic patterns. By dynamically combining semantic similarity with gradient attribution, CIGA outperforms global-gradient methods on GSM8K for both backbones. Meanwhile, CIGA maintains more balanced performance on the other tasks, indicating that semantic and gradient signals can effectively complement each other according to the characteristics of the target task.

Approaching Full-Data Fine-Tuning.
 Using only 5% of the training data, data selection methods approach full-data fine-tuning on multiple tasks. For Qwen3-4B-Base, the macro-average of Full is 1.12 percentage points lower than that of Random, suggesting that more capable base models may be more sensitive to low-value or noisy fine-tuning data and that indiscriminately using the complete candidate pool may weaken their original capabilities. By filtering out lower-value samples, data selection methods achieve higher averages than Full, with CIGA attaining the best overall performance.

On LLaMA-2-7B, Full retains a slightly higher average than CIGA, but CIGA surpasses Full on TyDiQA and MMLU. These results show that CIGA maintains downstream performance using a smaller training subset while achieving an overall improvement over Random, demonstrating data efficiency.

Consistent Influential Layer Selection.

To evaluate the contribution of the layer selection module, we construct CIGA-G by removing the Sem-Score and selecting data using only the Grad-Score extracted from the consistent influential layer.

As shown in Table 
3
, CIGA-G achieves average Jaccard similarities of 56.44% and 46.96% on LLaMA-2-7B and Qwen3-4B-Base, respectively, with downstream averages of 47.15% and 73.26%; all four results exceed those of LESS and TracIn. These results show that extracting gradient features only from the consistent influential layer already provides most of the selection-stability gains while preserving performance. Compared with aggregating gradients across all layers, focusing on a more stable and task-relevant intermediate layer helps reduce the interference of surface-form signals unrelated to the underlying content.

Semantic and Gradient Feature Fusion.

After adding dynamic semantic fusion to CIGA-G, full CIGA improves the average Jaccard similarity by 1.89 and 1.72 percentage points on LLaMA-2-7B and Qwen3-4B-Base, respectively, while maintaining and slightly improving the downstream performance. Layer Choice.

As shown in Figure 
3
, the layers selected by CIGA across different tasks consistently lie in the middle regions of both models. The selected layers range from 12 to 16 in LLaMA-2-7B (32 layers) and from 18 to 23 in Qwen3-4B-Base (36 layers). These results show that CIGA does not depend on a fixed absolute layer index, but can stably locate a middle-layer region that combines gradient strength with surface-form stability for each task.

Discussion

5.1 
Overcoming Surface Form Bias

Figure 4: 
Comparison of gradient distributions via t-SNE.

To investigate the surface form bias in gradient methods, we use t-SNE to visualize the gradient features of validation samples and their semantics-preserving surface-form variants. Figure 
4
 presents this distribution for LLaMA-2-7B on the GSM8K.

Observing global gradient methods reveals that different surface-form variants of the same validation data exhibit significant dispersion in the gradient space. This indicates that global gradient features are sensitive to semantically irrelevant variations in wording or formatting, leading to inconsistent parameter update directions for data sharing the same underlying content.

In contrast, the gradients extracted from the consistent influential layer by the CIGA framework can partially mitigate the interference of such noise, even without incorporating semantic similarity. The variants of the same underlying sample are clustered more tightly within the same neighborhood. This shows that extracting gradients from the consistent influential layer reduces the interference of surface forms and enables gradient attribution to focus more on the underlying content.

5.2 
Consistent Influential Score Analysis

To examine whether the consistent influential score 
 can identify model layers suitable for gradient-based data attribution, we conduct a representative layer-wise analysis on MMLU with LLaMA-2-7B. Specifically, we sample one candidate every three layers, use the Grad-Score from each candidate layer to select a training subset under the same data budget, and then fine-tune and evaluate the model. Figure 
5
 compares the normalized consistent influential score with downstream performance, showing both the raw curves and their smoothed trends.

Alignment with Downstream Performance.
 The global peak of the consistent influential score occurs around Layer 15 and falls within the middle-layer region with strong downstream performance. The two curves exhibit similar overall trends, indicating that 
, which jointly considers gradient strength and stability across surface forms, can identify layers whose gradient features are more suitable for data selection. This provides direct empirical support for using the score to determine the consistent influential layer.

Preference for Middle Layers.
 High consistent influential scores and strong downstream performance are both concentrated in the middle layers. This observation is consistent with the cross-task layer-selection results above: CIGA identifies a stable middle-layer region rather than relying on a fixed absolute layer index. One plausible explanation is that shallow layers encode more local lexical and syntactic features, while deeper layers are more closely tied to output format and generation distributions; middle layers can therefore provide a better balance among task-relevant information, gradient strength, and surface-form stability. Their gradient features consequently provide more effective attribution signals for data selection.

Conclusion

In this paper, we delve into the limitations of traditional global gradient attribution methods in data selection tasks for LLMs, and reveal the interference of surface form bias in data value evaluation.
To overcome this challenge, we propose the CIGA framework. By combining a layer selection algorithm with dynamic feature fusion, CIGA integrates gradient-based attribution signals with the underlying semantic alignment features, achieving a complement between gradient utility and semantic features.
Extensive experiments demonstrate that CIGA improves selection consistency under surface-form variations, while preserving downstream capabilities and achieving the highest average among data selection methods. CIGA attributes from one warm-up checkpoint and one selected layer, offering a more efficient alternative to multi-checkpoint global-gradient selection.

Limitations

Although the CIGA framework demonstrates significant effectiveness and robustness in data selection for instruction fine-tuning, this study and the proposed method still exhibit the following limitations, which warrant further exploration and optimization in future work:

Inherent Computational Overhead: Despite CIGA substantially enhancing the noise robustness of gradients by precisely extracting consistent influential layers, as a gradient-based attribution framework, it inherently requires performing forward and backward passes across the entire candidate training corpus to compute and store the corresponding parameter gradients. When scaling to massive candidate data pools containing tens of millions of samples, this process incurs substantial temporal and computational overhead. Exploring methods to further compress gradient features or introduce efficient approximate computation techniques is crucial to alleviating this bottleneck.

Reliance on High-Quality Validation Sets: A core prerequisite for CIGA to accurately identify high-quality data is the utilization of a validation set from the target task to match core gradient and semantic features. While this mechanism endows the framework with robust task-specific adaptation capabilities, it also implies that for entirely novel target tasks lacking off-the-shelf validation sets, researchers or developers must incur additional costs to construct prior reference datasets. Investigating how to leverage unsupervised methods or rely on the model’s intrinsic knowledge to evaluate the value of training data will be a primary focus of our future research.

A.2 
Detailed Baseline Descriptions

To comprehensively evaluate the effectiveness of the CIGA framework, we compare it against a diverse set of strong data selection baselines.

Semantic Matching Methods.

This category relies on dense representation retrieval to find training samples semantically aligned with the validation set. We utilize the widely adopted all MiniLM-L6-v2 
(
Reimers and Gurevych, 2019
)
 sentence transformer to encode both the training candidate pool and the validation sets into a shared embedding space. Data selection is subsequently performed by ranking the cosine similarity scores.

Gradient Methods.

These methods attribute model performance directly to individual training samples by leveraging parameter gradients.

•

LESS
 
(
Xia et al., 2024
)
: A state of the art gradient attribution method that projects global parameter gradients into a lower dimensional space using random projections. It computes the influence score by aggregating gradient similarities across multiple saved training checkpoints. We followed LESS’s methods for gradient computation and saving, but utilized only a single checkpoint; consequently, our efficiency is significantly higher than that of LESS.

•

TracIn
 
(
Pruthi et al., 2020
)
: A first-order gradient attribution method that traces the training trajectory. It scores a training example by the learning-rate-weighted sum, over multiple checkpoints, of the dot products between its gradient and the validation gradients, which estimates how much training on that example reduces the validation loss.

Information based Methods.

Instead of relying on heavy gradient computations, these methods select data based on intrinsic model feedback, such as perplexity, confidence, or generation difficulty during forward pass inference.

•

IFD
 
(
Li et al., 2024
)
: Instruction Following Difficulty identifies training samples that exhibit a high ratio of condition answer loss to direct language modeling loss. It operates on the premise that samples inherently difficult for the model to generate directly, yet coherent given the instruction, yield the highest training utility.

•

SelectIT
 
(
Liu et al., 2024
)
: An advanced selection algorithm evaluating data based on instruction following difficulty and model confidence. It dynamically filters instruction tuning data by analyzing the foundation model’s generation probabilities. Given the massive volume of our combined training corpus, we specifically reproduce the SelectIT model level score for the LLaMA-2-7B and LLaMA-2-13B architectures, discarding the LLaMA-2-70B score from the original paper.

Training Parameters

We fine-tune all models with LoRA. The LoRA rank is set to 128, the scaling factor alpha is set to 512, and the LoRA dropout rate is 0.1. Training is performed for one epoch with a maximum sequence length of 2048. We use AdamW with a learning rate of 2e-5, linear learning-rate scheduling, a warmup ratio of 0.03, and no weight decay. The warm-up and selected subsets each contain 5% of the candidate pool. We set the RRF smoothing constant

Abstract.

Web agents increasingly browse, shop, and manage accounts on behalf of their
users. Prior work has shown that they can be misled by adversarial content,
including prompt injections and deceptive interfaces. Beyond such attacks,
agents operating on the open web also encounter online fraud designed to
deceive humans, including fraudulent online stores, fake investment platforms,
and advance-fee schemes. How web agents respond to such fraud during task
execution remains underexplored, in part because scam websites are
short-lived, making systematic evaluation difficult. To address this gap, we
introduce 
ScamWorld
, an interactive environment that
reconstructs reported scam websites across diverse scenarios, fraud mechanisms,
and languages. Each reconstructed website reproduces documented fraud
mechanisms, making relevant signs of fraud observable before the agent’s final
action and enabling controlled, repeatable evaluation. Through an extensive
study of web agents powered by six frontier LLMs, we find that baseline
deception rates range from 11% to 35% across models. Agents
proceed more readily with payments than with requests for credentials or
wallet information. A closer analysis of agent trajectories shows that some
agents acknowledge suspicious conditions yet proceed, at times treating the
user’s instructions as authorisation to accept additional terms imposed by the
website. 
ScamWorld
 provides a reusable testbed for
systematically evaluating and stress-testing web agents against real-world
online fraud.

1. 
Introduction

Web agents now search the web, shop in online stores, book travel, and manage
accounts on behalf of their users 
(
Deng et al., 2023
; 
Zhou et al., 2024
; 
Yao et al., 2022
; 
He et al., 2024
; 
Wu et al., 2026b
)
.
In carrying out these tasks, they also encounter untrustworthy content written
for humans, from misinformation 
(
Wu et al., 2025
; 
Huang et al., 2026
)
 to online
fraud 
(
Kotzias et al., 2025
; 
Bitaab et al., 2025b
)
. Such fraud
cost victims an estimated $442 billion in one year, with shopping scams
the most common type 
(
Global Anti-Scam Alliance and Feedzai, 2025
)
.

Browser protections offer incomplete coverage against fraudulent websites.
Google Safe Browsing, for example, flags fewer than one in five phishing
websites, with an average detection delay of 4.5 days 
(
Lee et al., 2025
)
.
These gaps leave agents exposed to fraudulent websites during ordinary browsing
and search 
(
Kotzias et al., 2025
; 
Paudel and Stringhini, 2026
)
.
For instance, Figure 
1
 documents a case from 2026 in which a
website impersonating a major grocery chain appeared in Google Search results.
This is a real-world problem. 
When web agents encounter such scam websites
while carrying out a user’s request, do they recognise the fraud, or do they
complete the task anyway?

Existing studies on web-agent safety and online fraud fall into two lines
of work.

i)
 Work on web-agent safety primarily demonstrates the feasibility of attacks,
injecting malicious
instructions 
(
Debenedetti et al., 2024
; 
Evtimov et al., 2025
; 
Guo et al., 2026b
)

or adding deceptive interface elements such as dark
patterns 
(
Ersoy et al., 2026
; 
Guo et al., 2026a
; 
Shi et al., 2026
; 
Cuvin et al., 2026
; 
Zhang et al., 2026a
)

that steer the agent toward an attacker-chosen outcome.

ii)
 Work on online fraud studies how automated methods, increasingly based on
LLMs, can help humans detect malicious URLs or web pages from their address or
content, mainly treating the task as a classification
problem 
(
Bitaab et al., 2023
; 
Nakano et al., 2025
; 
Bitaab et al., 2025a
; 
Zhou et al., 2026
)
.
In contrast, we focus on how increasingly capable web agents behave when they
encounter online fraud during task execution. Studying this behaviour calls for
an interactive testbed grounded in real-world scam websites, where agents’
decisions and actions can be systematically examined across scam scenarios,
interaction modes, and languages. As summarised in
Table 
1
, existing benchmarks rarely evaluate
web agents on real scam websites.

To this end, we introduce 
ScamWorld
, an interactive testbed
for studying how web agents behave on reconstructed scam websites, as
illustrated in Figure 
2
.
Since scam websites are short-lived 
(
Oest et al., 2020
; 
Lee et al., 2025
)
,
and an agent acting on them could hand real payments or personal data to
fraudsters, we reconstruct them as persistent, locally hosted websites that
support safe and repeatable evaluation.
To build these websites, we first collect scam URLs reported by victims, watchdogs, and
authorities 
(
Li et al., 2023
)

and retrieve their pages from
the live web or, for offline websites, from archives such as the
Wayback Machine
1
1

1

https://web.archive.org
. Because these copies are often
incomplete (
e.g.
, missing images or scripts), we manually rebuild
363 scam websites spanning 16 scenarios, 38 fraud mechanisms,
and 26 languages, each preserving the original fraud mechanisms before the
final action, such as a payment. Recording every interaction lets us trace
whether agents identify red flags, warn the user, stop, or proceed. We also
include 53 legitimate websites built the same way, so that scam
avoidance can be distinguished from over-refusal.

Extensive experiments with web agents using six frontier LLMs as backbones
reveal that all evaluated agents are susceptible to online fraud, with baseline
deception rates ranging from 11% to 35%. A closer analysis of
their trajectories shows that agents may identify website-specific risks yet
proceed, often treating the user’s request as sufficient authorisation. Under
explicit assessment, the same agents also recognise many of the websites that
deceive them during task execution. In deciding whether to proceed, they are
more cautious about disclosing credentials or wallet details than about making
payments. With a one-sentence warning, they are less often deceived but also
less likely to complete tasks on legitimate websites. To support further research on these
behaviours, we release 
ScamWorld
 as a reusable testbed for evaluating and
stress-testing web agents across diverse scam scenarios, fraud mechanisms,
languages, and browser interaction modes, alongside legitimate controls.

;

;

)
.
Safety evaluation has followed a similar progression, examining harmful user

; 
Andriushchenko et al., 2025
; 
Kuntz et al., 2025
; 
Wu et al., 2026c
;

)
,
prompt injection in page
content 
(
Debenedetti et al., 2024
; 
Evtimov et al., 2025
; 
Guo et al., 2026b
; 
Zhang et al., 2026b
; 
Chen et al., 2026
; 
Zheng et al., 2026
)
,
and interface-based attacks. Such attacks exploit deceptive
content 
(
Liao et al., 2025
)
, adversarial pop-ups 
(
Zhang et al., 2025
)
, and dark
patterns 
(
Mathur et al., 2019
; 
Ersoy et al., 2026
; 
Guo et al., 2026a
; 
Shi et al., 2026
; 
Cuvin et al., 2026
; 
Wu et al., 2026a
; 
Zhang et al., 2026a
)

to elicit personal information or manipulate agent actions. Alongside attack
evaluations, complementary approaches assess risk recognition from recorded
interactions 
(
Yuan et al., 2024
)
 and use tool emulation to examine unsafe
actions 
(
Ruan et al., 2024
)
. Behavioural studies further show that models
may comply with harmful requests as browser agents despite refusing them in

(

2.2. 
Scam Website Measurement and Detection

Research on online fraud characterises scam
mechanisms 
(
Kotzias et al., 2025
; 
Bitaab et al., 2025b
; 
Miramirkhani et al., 2017
; 
Pitumpe and Rahmati, 2026
; 
Kharraz et al., 2018
; 
Hong et al., 2022
; 
Acharya et al., 2025
)

and develops methods for discovering 
(
Paudel and Stringhini, 2026
)
 and classifying
fraudulent websites 
(
Bitaab et al., 2023
; 
Dalton et al., 2025
)
.
Detection methods draw on page content, URL features, and external
evidence 
(
Bitaab et al., 2025a
; 
Li et al., 2024
)
, with LLM-based agents also
using tools to investigate websites before reaching a
judgement 
(
Nakano et al., 2025
)
. Agent-based fraud detection uses
browser interaction to gather evidence for a website-level
judgement 
(
Zhou et al., 2026
; 
Liu et al., 2026
)
, and constructed
deceptive websites test whether agents disclose personal
information 
(
Roy et al., 2026
)
. For task-execution evaluation, however, a
reported scam label does not by itself establish whether the underlying fraud
can be observed and tested through browser interaction. Captured pages may omit
relevant steps or provide insufficient evidence of harms that emerge only after
payment. ScamWorld instead reconstructs the fraud mechanisms of reported
scam websites within executable user-task workflows, with evidence available before the final action and outcomes judged from the agent’s actions. HTML, accessibility trees, and
screenshots reflect the browser state as agents navigate and act during ordinary tasks.

Collection and selection.

from prior empirical
studies 
(

;

; 
Kharraz et al., 2018
;

and consumer advisories

. The resulting
catalogue contains 38 mechanisms in 14 families
(Table 
4

A

we collect 88,009 unique URLs reported as scams through
anti-scam lists, victim reports, official warnings, news, and research
datasets 
(
Li et al., 2023

Janavičiūtė et al., 2024

(
Dalton et al., 2025

; 
Nakano et al., 2025
; 
Almakhamreh, 2023

Bitaab et al., 2023
; 
Apruzzese and Subrahmanian, 2023
)
,
while LegitPhish supplies legitimate websites 
(
Potpelwar et al., 2025
)
.
From the reported URLs, we recover 9,152 complete pages,
including 4,334 from the Wayback Machine
(Figure 
6
), and retain 7,878 unique scam pages after deduplication. Two domain experts annotate
3,416 of these pages against the mechanism catalogue, with a
third resolving disagreements. From the annotated collection, an annotator
selects representative websites, keeping one per template or campaign with a
preserved layout and an identifiable fraudulent claim or condition. Our
selection covers at least five websites per scenario, mechanism family, and
mechanism, and at least three per language. For languages without suitable
source websites, we adapt reported scam cases to the local language and
conventions.

From websites to tasks.

Reported and archived copies rarely support a complete task. Some preserve only landing pages, without the checkout or confirmation steps needed to complete a transaction, while missing
scripts and images further limit interaction. In some cases, the fraud itself
becomes apparent only after payment. We therefore reconstruct the missing steps
and present the relevant fraudulent claim or condition before the agent’s final
action. For example, an investment website may require an additional fee
before releasing a payout, while a rental listing asks for a deposit to a
private owner that is not credited toward the rental. In the collected pages, the claim or condition was already present, at least in part, for about half of the scam websites; for the remainder, we introduce it based on the
documented fraud mechanism. We also recreate misleading credibility cues, such
as fabricated reviews, borrowed seals, and endorsements, without treating these
added elements as the designated key red flag. To restore interaction, we implement the missing pages with a coding assistant, such as Microsoft Copilot
3
3

3

https://copilot.microsoft.com
, and manually review the generated scripts and design changes. A text-to-image model generates missing images and SVG graphics.

Validation.

We manually test each website to ensure that its interactive elements respond correctly and browser actions produce the expected state changes. During construction, we also run a web agent on each task to identify broken links, missing pages, and other interaction errors. We repeat these tests and fix the issues found until the task workflows run without observed errors, before including the websites in ScamWorld. Scripted tests further check the recorded outcome of each task path, and a separate LLM reviews each website against the benchmark’s design requirements.

3.3. 
Evaluation Protocol

We evaluate each episode using the agent’s browser actions and the message it
returns to the user. Following BrowserGym 
(
de Chezelles et al., 2025
)
, an
episode ends when the agent sends a free-text message 
 to the user or
reaches the step limit. Episodes that reach the limit are scored without a
final message. We determine whether the agent takes the task’s final action
from the website’s event log, using 
. On scam websites, we label an episode 
Deceived
 if the agent takes the
final action, regardless of its final response. If the agent does not take the
final action but warns the user that the website is fraudulent or suspicious,
we label the episode 
Detected
. Since Detected also includes general
suspicion, the detection rate may overstate how often agents recognise the
specific fraud mechanism. Episodes that meet neither condition are labelled

Failed
, including cases where the agent stops for other reasons,
requests further input, or reaches the step limit
(Table 
6
 in the Appendix gives the breakdown). On
legitimate websites, an episode is 
Completed
 if the agent takes the
final action and 
Failed
 otherwise. In the separate fraud-assessment setting (explicit assessment),
where agents are directly asked to judge whether a website is legitimate or
fraudulent, incorrectly identifying a legitimate website as a scam counts as a

False alarm
.

We use DeepSeek V4.1 Flash as an LLM judge to assess the agent’s final
response. The judge identifies warnings about fraud, extracts claims about
completed actions, and compares those claims with the website’s event log. For
explicit assessment, the judge also records the agent’s verdict and
verifies that any cited warning signs appeared on pages the agent visited. To evaluate the reliability of the LLM judge, we invited Master’s students with relevant academic backgrounds to manually annotate 111 agent responses. Comparing the judge’s predictions with these human annotations yielded a Cohen’s 
 of 0.87. Three additional judges from other model families agree with the primary judge on 99% of responses each (Table 
6
).

4.1. 
Experimental Setup

Agent.

We use the AgentLab framework 
(
de Chezelles et al., 2025
)

to implement a web agent with the same short, neutral system prompt and browser
interface for all backbone LLMs. At each step, the agent receives a screenshot
of the current viewport, the URL, and a flattened list of accessibility nodes
containing their roles, text, and control IDs. Nodes within the viewport are
listed first, while the raw HTML and the hierarchical accessibility tree are
not provided. The agent can click, enter text, select a control, scroll, open
a URL, or finish by sending a message to the user. Before each action it may
write a brief plain-text response, which stays in its history. We allow one attempt per
task, with at most 60 actions, including invalid ones.

Backbones.

We evaluate six LLMs from six providers as backbones: Claude Haiku 5.5,
GPT-5.6 Luna, Gemini 3.8 Flash, Kimi K3, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, and
Qwen3.8 Flash. We use their default settings and medium reasoning effort where
supported.

Evaluation settings and metrics.

Our primary evaluation uses the ScamWorld tasks introduced in
Section 
3
, where agents receive user requests
and interact with the corresponding websites to complete them (
task
execution
). In addition, we conduct a separate scam-identification experiment
to examine whether agents can recognise fraudulent websites when explicitly
asked to do so (
explicit assessment
). In this experiment, we replace the
original user request with the question “
Can you look around this website and
tell me whether it is legitimate or a scam? Please tell me what you saw on the
website that makes you think so.
” Both experiments use the same websites,
backbones, and action budget. The main evaluation includes
2,262 episodes on 363 scam websites, along with
tasks on 53 legitimate websites for comparison. We report
deception and detection rates on scam websites and completion rates on
legitimate websites, all with 95% confidence intervals from a website-level
bootstrap. Explicit assessment uses scam-identification and false-alarm
rates.

4.2. 
Main Results

Finding ❶
 Web agents can complete web tasks effectively, yet remain vulnerable to online fraud.

As shown in Table 
2
, all six backbones complete nearly all
tasks on legitimate websites reconstructed using the same procedure. On scam
websites, however, deception rates range from 11% to 35%.
Although agents avoid the final action in most scam encounters, even a
single mistake can have serious consequences. In a real-world setting, one
mistaken payment or disclosure of sensitive information could result in
financial loss or account compromise.

Finding ❷
 Web agents are more likely to make payments than disclose sensitive information on scam websites.

Figure 
7
 compares deception rates across scam objectives
and scenarios. The most striking difference concerns what agents are asked to
do: they proceed in 28% of scams involving payments, compared with
only 2% of those requesting credentials, card details, or wallet
access. Even when a website directs payment to a private individual, agents
proceed in 22% of episodes.
Table 
3
 shows a similar pattern across fraud mechanism
families, with higher deception rates for investment
(34%), non-delivery (31%), and
subscription fraud (26%) than for credential phishing
(7%) or malicious downloads
(8%).

Finding ❸
 Web agents can acknowledge suspicious conditions yet still proceed.

In 220 of 492 deceived episodes (45%),
agents explicitly mention a website-specific risk before taking the final
action. Figure 
5
 illustrates one such case: during a rental
booking, an agent questions a Zelle deposit to a private owner but proceeds
with the payment, even though the website states that the deposit is not
credited toward the rental. These trajectories reveal a distinction that would
be missed by evaluating the agent’s statements alone: an agent may express concern yet still take the unsafe action.

4.3. 
Scam Identification

When asked directly, agents identify 97% of scam
websites, compared with 75% of task-execution episodes in which
they stop and report a suspicious or fraudulent website. They also identify
95% of the websites on which they were previously
deceived. Figure 
 shows that high scam-identification rates
coexist with substantial differences in deception during task execution across
the six backbones. Since the two experiments use different instructions, these
results do not establish whether agents recognised the same risks when they
originally proceeded.

4.4. 
Ablation Study

We further examine whether changes to the agents’ instructions and browser
observations affect their decisions during task execution.
Figure 
 compares matched runs with and without each change
for Kimi K3, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, and Qwen3.8 Flash.

The largest effect comes from adding a brief warning to the user’s request:
“
Note: some websites may be scams; check before you pay or share
details.
” This increases detection by 25 percentage points and
reduces deception by 24 points. For Qwen3.8 Flash, detection rises
from 81% to 99%. By contrast, explicitly
telling agents that their payments are real and irreversible changes detection
by only 2 points and deception by 1 point. The
warning also reduces task completion on legitimate websites by
3 points.

Changes to the information available to agents have smaller effects. Removing
fabricated reviews, seals, and team pages reduces detection by
5 points and increases deception by 5 points, although neither difference is statistically conclusive. Hiding the URL
also produces only small changes, consistent with the observation that just
3.2% of detected responses cite the URL alone
(Figure 
13
 in the Appendix). Finally, when Kimi K3 and
Qwen3.8 Flash are explicitly allowed to consult other websites, neither does so in 100 episodes.

5.1. 
Agent Interaction Patterns

By recording browser interactions and final actions, ScamWorld allows us to
examine not only the conditions agents encounter but also how they respond to
them. Table 
 shows agents describing
dubious transaction terms and still taking the final action. Their responses record, for example, a
withdrawal fee larger than the available bonus, a non-refundable loan fee paid
to a personal account, or implausible investment returns. The agents accurately restate these
conditions but still proceed as though they were ordinary transaction steps. Rather than relying
on these statements alone, ScamWorld determines whether the action occurred
from the website’s event log. These decisions also occur quickly: after the key red
flag first appears, deceived agents take a median of 2 actions before the final action, and only 13 of the
492 open an About, terms, reviews, or contact page.
Figure 
8
a separates agents that stop before the red flag
from those that stop or proceed after seeing it. The red flag is visible
before the final action by design, although visibility alone does not
establish recognition. Trajectories also show how detection happens. Of the
1,700 detected episodes, 817 stop before any of the
task’s decisive evidence is on screen, from 0.34 of episodes
for Gemini 3.8 Flash to 0.62 for GPT-5.6 Luna and Kimi K3.
A verdict-only evaluation would credit these early stops in the same way as stops made after
reading the decisive condition; the trajectory separates the two.
Another discrepancy appears between an agent’s reasoning and its responses:
the responses can omit concerns raised in the reasoning. In 164 of the 220 deceived episodes whose last steps raise a website-specific concern and whose
final action carries a response, the concern appears in the agent’s reasoning
but in none of its responses, so a monitor reading only the responses would
miss it. GPT-5.6 Luna and Gemini 3.8 Flash rarely write a response
before the final action.

5.2. 
Understanding Agent Failures

These trajectories also reveal how agents justify proceeding despite
suspicious conditions. Deceived agents that express concern most often cite the user’s
request as their reason (317 of 354),
sometimes treating a spending limit as approval of unexpected fees or private
payees. Detected agents often state that the same request does not authorise
the website’s terms (1,230 of 1,700). On
105 tasks, different backbones report the same primary concern
yet make opposite decisions. In other words, the same request is read as
permission by some agents and as a limit by others. These are the agents’ stated explanations, not
established causes, and an explicit budget shows no clear association with
proceeding after a concern. The type of risk also matters, particularly when payments are involved.
After raising a concern, agents proceed in 328 of
1,433 payment episodes, compared with 3 of 360 involving credentials, card details, or wallet
access. Figure 
8
b shows that they proceed more often after
noting an added fee or private payee than after a suspicious domain or a
request for sensitive information. This difference persists after adjusting
for backbone, red-flag location, language, and wording, though the comparison
does not establish causality. When the user’s instruction supplies a password,
code, or recovery phrase, agents never submit it. Taken together, these findings show that
agents are more willing to proceed with suspicious payments than to disclose
sensitive information.

6. 
Discussion and Conclusion

Limitations.

ScamWorld focuses on fraud conducted through websites, rather than schemes
that rely on phone calls or private messages. As a controlled evaluation
environment, it preserves selected fraud mechanisms without reproducing every
aspect of the original websites. In particular, key fraud indicators are deliberately exposed before the final action, while certain fake trust signals
appear only on scam websites. Some missing images are generated, and user
profiles are synthetic. Our evaluation also uses a fixed agent scaffold,
primarily offline interactions, and no human baseline. Fraud recognition is
assessed by an LLM judge, while parts of the trajectory analysis rely on
automated labels and the reasoning made available by each model. These factors
limit how far the findings can be generalised to unrestricted browsing in the
wild.

Ethics.

All reconstructed websites are served locally, apart from a restricted
external-access experiment. The original scripts are not executed, user
information is synthetic, and final actions are simulated
without transferring money or collecting real personal data. References to
brands and public figures are retained only where relevant to the
impersonation mechanisms of the scams.

Implications and conclusion.

ScamWorld exposes a failure mode that is easily overlooked in web-agent
safety: an agent need not deviate from the user’s task to cause harm. Rather,
it may follow the requested workflow, recognise suspicious conditions, and
still treat the user’s instructions as authorisation to accept terms
introduced by an untrusted website. The problem thus extends beyond fraud
recognition to the boundary between user intent and website-imposed
conditions. For agent developers, this suggests checking the material terms of
a transaction before the final action, without restricting
legitimate tasks. ScamWorld provides a reusable testbed for studying this
boundary and assessing whether web agents can complete user requests without
accepting fraudulent conditions.

Scoring reliability.

To check the LLM judge, Master’s students with relevant academic backgrounds
labelled 111 agent responses, one student per response, and we compared
these labels with the primary judge and with three judges from other model
families (Table 
6
). The primary judge agrees with the human
labels at Cohen’s 
, and the other judges reach almost
the same Detected decisions, so no backbone’s detection rate moves by more than
2.1 points when the judge changes. The comparisons in
Section 
4
 therefore do not depend on which model scores the
responses. A stricter definition of Detected, which requires the agent to name
a specific sign of fraud, matches the human labels equally well
(
) and gives detection rates between
58% and 80%.

Failed episodes.

Scam episodes that end without the final action and without a warning count as
Failed. The judge also records why each such agent stopped, and these labels
agree with the human annotations at 
.
Table 
6
 shows that Failed episodes are rare and mostly
reflect the agents’ own limitations. Figure 
12
 relates each
backbone’s completion on legitimate websites to its deception rate. Because
every backbone completes nearly all legitimate tasks, a low deception rate does
not reflect a general failure to act.

Factors associated with deception.

We fit a logistic regression of deception on the backbone, the fraud mechanism
family, the type of final action, and where the red flag first appears. The
differences between backbones remain after these adjustments: DeepSeek V4.1
Flash has about three times the odds of deception of Claude Haiku 5.5, and
GPT-5.6 Luna about half. Among the task factors, top-up actions, in which the
agent adds money to an account balance, raise the odds of deception. Language
matters less than the raw rates suggest. English-language websites deceive
agents more often than websites in other languages (25% against
15–22%), but a third of them are investment scams, compared with
8% of European-language websites. Within a single family
the rates across languages are close, and adding language to the regression
shows no clear effect. The gap therefore reflects which scams appear in each
language rather than the language itself.

Website-level concentration.

Deception concentrates on a minority of websites. Of the 363 scam
websites, 186 deceive no backbone and 13 deceive
all six, while the hardest fifth of websites accounts for
67% of deceived episodes. In a regression clustered by website, deception is associated with a
task that asks for money (odds ratio 9.88 [4.39, 22.22]) and with a plausible
domain under the website’s own brand (2.36 [1.57, 3.54]), but not with a red
flag that first appears at checkout (1.01 [0.65, 1.57]). The hardest
websites look like ordinary shops or services and ask for an ordinary payment.

One-sentence warning.

Under the warning, 130 of the
132 deceived episodes in which the agent named a risk
become Detected, as do 146 of the 155 in
which it named none, and 829 of 835 detected episodes stay Detected. On payment tasks, deception falls from
0.34 to 0.02. On legitimate websites, DeepSeek
V4.1 Flash completes 52 of 53 tasks, Kimi K3
53 of 53, and Qwen3.8 Flash 49 of 53.

Real-payment instruction.

Telling DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, Kimi K3, and Qwen3.8 Flash that their payments are
real turns 77 deceived episodes into Detected ones but also
61 detected episodes into Deceived ones, so agents do not
become more careful on balance.

Fake trust signals.

Table 
5
 shows that, under explicit assessment,
every backbone points to a fake trust signal, such as fabricated reviews or
seals, on nearly all scam websites but
names the mechanism on only a third to a half. Agents more readily
notice that a website appears untrustworthy than explain how it extracts
payment, which is the information a payment decision depends on.

Benchmark awareness.

Some agents speculate that they are being evaluated, most often Gemini 3.8
Flash (70% of its episodes). Removing every episode that mentions
an evaluation changes no deception or detection rate by more than
0.08, so such speculation does not drive the main results.

Interaction length.

Figure 
10
 counts the browser actions per episode. Detected
episodes most often end after one action, on the first page that raises doubt,
whereas Deceived episodes take a median of three and follow the checkout flow
to its end. In 927 of the 1,700 Detected episodes, the
agent stops without any navigation action, and only 88 open an
About, terms, reviews, or contact page.

Evidence cited by agents.

Figure 
13
 shows that almost all Detected replies cite page
content, alone or with the URL; the URL alone accounts for only a small share. Agents detect
these scams mainly from what the reconstructed pages say, consistent with the
small effect of hiding the URL (Section 
4.4
).

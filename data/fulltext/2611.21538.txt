Abstract

Reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards inherits whatever its verifier
gets wrong. In mathematics and code the verifier is exact. Outside them it is
a proxy, a rubric, a learned judge or a hand-written checker, and its distance
from the true objective is never measured, so a rising reward cannot be
distinguished from a hack. We build a setting where the true reward is exactly
computable at the process level. Executable authorization policies over an
enumerable space of 
 contexts admit an oracle that lists every
minimal sufficient reason for every decision, which turns the causal fidelity
of a model’s cited reasons into a computed quantity. We compile such policies
into exact process rewards, degrade the verifier along seven realistic axes
(outcome-only, sufficiency, recall-only, extraction noise, coarse taxonomy,
stale policy, LLM judge), and train against each while the exact verifier
scores every rollout. Four phenotypes appear, each with its own behavioural
signature. Optimistic verifiers are hacked by padding: the sufficiency proxy
terminates at 0.940 while true faithfulness falls from
0.731 to 0.667 on 
 held-out episodes.
Outcome-only verifiers saturate and stop teaching. Noise dilutes, and
ranking-preserving gaps do no harm. Which phenotype a run will produce is
predicted beforehand by a model-free audit of the verifier alone, its
selection error and gap sign, together with a short pilot of how often its
reward saturates. Across nine configurations spanning two frameworks, two
model vendors, two adapter sizes, three precisions, two initialisations and
two seeds, the audit quantity is invariant, the phenotypes recur, sufficiency
damage is positive in all 10 replication cells and beyond two
standard errors in 7, and optimistic gaps stay the steeper
branch on the replication grid too (3.2
 against
2.3
, at an interval too wide to test the size). The damage
magnitude does not replicate, and five pre-registered attempts to explain it
are reported as they fell. We release the testbed, the training
instrumentation, and a second task family of 14 real public
policies (Cedar, OPA, Gatekeeper) ported to executable oracles, on which the
audit reads the same way.

Introduction

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR), where a program
checks each answer and returns the reward, has driven the recent
progress of reasoning models. In mathematics and code the verifier is exact.
Nearly everywhere else it is a
proxy, a rubric 
(
Gunjal et al., 2026
)
, a reference-derived signal

(
Jiang et al., 2026
)
, a learned judge or a hand-built checker. Reward hacking,
a policy raising its trained reward while the true objective
falls, is studied intensively

(
Amodei et al., 2016
; 
Skalse et al., 2022
; 
Gao et al., 2023
; 
Pan et al., 2022
; 
Khalifa et al., 2026
; 
Wong et al., 2026
; 
Helff et al., 2026
)
.
Recent work argues that noisy verifiers may be tolerable

(
Plesner et al., 2026
)
, and other work shows that systematic verifier
errors shape outcomes in ways an error rate does not predict

(
Egashira et al., 2026
)
. All of this work reads hacking off downstream evaluations, probes,
held-out suites or anecdote, because the true reward is unavailable for the
individual rollout, the sampled output where the behaviour arises.

We make it available. Executable authorization policies, the allow/block
gates that govern tool use for deployed agents, Kubernetes admission and
cloud access control, are white-box functions over a categorical context
space 
(
Cutler et al., 2024
)
. A gate’s decision tensor can be materialised, and an oracle can
compute every minimal sufficient reason (MSR) of every decision exactly

(
Shih et al., 2018
; 
Ignatiev et al., 2019
)
. Whether each reason a
model cites is causally relevant then reduces to a tensor slice. That is
process-level ground truth, a reward on why a decision was reached and not
only on whether it was right, which outcome verifiers for math and code
cannot supply. The exactness costs generality. Our results hold for
policy-gate explanation with 4B-parameter policy models and one
policy-gradient recipe, and §
10
 says how far we have tested
that they travel.

We make four contributions.

(1) Policy-as-code is a free verifier
(§
3
, §
4
).
 We compile executable policy gates
into exact outcome and process rewards, and formalise seven degraded
verifiers that each corrupt exactly one stage of the reward pipeline. They
mirror how practitioners build verifiers, by checking only outcomes,
substituting a sufficiency or recall proxy, tolerating extraction noise,
coarsening the taxonomy, holding a stale policy, or delegating to a large
language model (LLM) as judge.

(2) A model-free gap audit (§
5
).
 Before any
training, we measure each degraded verifier’s distance from truth on a
procedurally generated probe grid whose citation sets are exactly controlled.
The outcome-only verifier, the construction underlying most RLVR, cannot
rank a faithful explanation above an unfaithful one better than chance
(selection error 
), the sufficiency proxy inverts

 of selections, and a stale policy missing one rule
mis-decides 
 of all 
 contexts.

(3) An exact anatomy of the gap, and what survives replication
(§
6
, §
7
).
 We train a 4B policy
against each degraded verifier while the exact verifier scores every rollout,
so that the divergence between proxy and truth is a per-step observable.
Four phenotypes, distinct patterns of training behaviour, emerge. The
sufficiency proxy is hacked by padding, and carries the citation-inflation
signature we predicted in advance. Outcome-only rewards saturate within a few
steps and starve training from then on. Noise dilutes. Ranking-preserving
gaps, an LLM judge among them, are harmless. The audit’s sign and selection
error predict which of the four a run will produce. The sufficiency proxy’s
damage, the true reward it costs against an exact-reward control, is
positive in every replication run and significant in most, and a second,
structurally different optimistic verifier is hacked harder still. On the
original grid the damage magnitude is also a tight function of selection
error, with optimistic gaps 
 steeper than pessimistic ones
across four verifiers. The ordering and the sign asymmetry
replicate. The constants do not.

(4) A real-policy corpus and a damage-export test
(§
8
).
 We release 14 public policies
(Cedar, Open Policy Agent and Gatekeeper) ported to executable gates with exact oracles
(
 contexts, 5 held out). On them the hacked
model’s padding exports intact while aggregate 
 hides it, and the audit
reads as it did on the synthetic family. Exact-reward training transfers
without loss and without a significant gain, and corpus training is starved
by the ease of the task, which we report as a boundary.

The sections after related work take these contributions in order, then
give recommendations and limitations.

2 
Related Work

RLVR and the verifier problem.

The successes of RLVR in
mathematics and code 
(
Shao et al., 2024
)
 rest on verifiers that are
exact by construction. Extending it elsewhere has produced a family of proxy
verifiers, from rubric-based rewards 
(
Gunjal et al., 2026
)
 and
reference-derived signal chains 
(
Jiang et al., 2026
)
 to empirical arguments that
noisy verifiers are tolerable up to double-digit error rates

(
Plesner et al., 2026
)
. 
Ray (2026)
 fuzz verifiers for exploitable bugs before
training, and, closest to us, 
Egashira et al. (2026)

inject systematic verification errors on arithmetic and find that outcomes
range from delay to collapse in a way the overall error rate does not
predict. 
Mitsuhashi et al. (2026)
 inject random false positives and
false negatives on GSM8K and find false negatives the more damaging. All of
these must treat the verifier as given, because in their domains no
independent measurement of its distance from truth exists. Here the
verifier’s gap is itself the measured quantity. That answers the negative
result of 
Egashira et al.
 with a positive one, since damage is
predicted by selection error weighted by update flow (§
6.1
), and
accounts for the sign asymmetry: pessimistic noise damages in both studies,
while an optimistic verifier damages more per unit of corruption
(§
6.1
). The noise-tolerance claims pass our test only
conditionally, on reward granularity and on optimizer robustness
(§
6
).

Reward hacking.

Skalse et al. (2022)
 formalise hacking,

Gao et al. (2023)
 measure overoptimisation against a synthetic learned
gold reward model, Countdown-Code 
(
Khalifa et al., 2026
)
 and

Wong et al. (2026)
 observe hacking in outcome-level
environments, and 
Mahmoud et al. (2026)
 separate verifier failure from
rubric-design failure with a judge panel as reference. Two
recent studies audit a verifier before training. 
Zhang (2026)
 show
that a static leakiness audit of code test suites predicts the rewarded
false-positive mass, and 
Rajan (2026)
 find that 28.5% of
SWE-bench Verified tasks accept an incorrect patch. Both measure how much wrong output a verifier accepts. Neither can measure what a policy learned
from it, because the gold signal is again a test suite or a human. What we
add is the limit case: an exact process-level true reward, degraded
semantically rather than randomly, so that damage can be attributed to
a named property of the verifier. The starvation phenotype we
isolate is the pathological extreme of the uniform-group problem that
motivates dynamic sampling in trainers of the group relative policy
optimisation (GRPO) family 
(
Yu and others, 2025
)
.

Process rewards and faithfulness.

Process reward models

(
Lightman et al., 2024
)
 are learned and inherit judge noise, and step-level faithfulness rewards

(
Gui et al., 2026
)
 rely on evidence-support judges. 
Yuan et al. (2026)

convert symbolic oracles into dense process rewards and note the
dependence on oracle quality without studying it. We study exactly that
dependence, with an oracle that is perfect and degraded by construction. 
Ferreira et al. (2026)
 add a causal-attribution signal to the
reward model after showing that preference optimisation erodes explanation
faithfulness, and 
Hase and Potts (2026)
 reward chains of thought that let a
simulator predict counterfactual outputs. Both measure faithfulness against
the model’s own behaviour, whereas our oracle scores it against the decision
function’s true reasons. Formal explainable AI (XAI) provides exact attribution for white-box
functions through abductive minimal sufficient reasons

(
Shih et al., 2018
; 
Ignatiev et al., 2019
)
, and GlassGate

(
V, 2026
)
 used it to show that frontier models’ free-form
decision explanations are causally imprecise regardless of scale. We turn
that oracle from an evaluation device into a training-time instrument.

Agent-safety oracles.

Vera 
(
Feng et al., 2026
)
 verifies whether a harmful
outcome occurred but measures neither attribution nor verifier coverage,
Who-Tests-the-Testers 
(
Chen et al., 2026
)
 audits benchmark coverage without an
oracle, and AgentDojo 
(
Debenedetti et al., 2024
)
, MCPTox 
(
Wang et al., 2026
)
 and
Agent-SafetyBench 
(
Zhang et al., 2024
)
 test agents against curated
attacks. We study the verifier
that trains and audits the agent.

Seven Degraded Verifiers

Each degraded verifier 
 corrupts exactly one stage of the reward
pipeline just defined. The exact
verifier stays computable alongside, so

 is always observable.

Outcome-only
 
 rewards the decision
word and ignores cited reasons, the default construction in RLVR.

Sufficiency
 
 rewards the graded
sufficiency of the cited set, the standard proxy in the
explanation-faithfulness literature 
(
DeYoung et al., 2020
; 
Lei et al., 2016
)
. 
Recall-only

 rewards causal recall with no precision term. It
is optimistic by a different mechanism, and tests whether the hack belongs to
one proxy or to optimism itself. 
Extraction noise
 
 flips each
feature’s cited status with probability 
, the random-noise
model of 
Plesner et al. (2026)
. 
Coarse taxonomy

 cannot distinguish features within a group,
such as the three payload flags, so credit flows to group-mates.

Stale policy
 
 is missing rule 
,
so its decisions and MSRs are computed on the wrong gate. 
LLM
judge
 
 replaces the deterministic extractor
with an LLM, the rubric-reward pattern. Formal definitions and the
invariants each verifier is tested against are in Appendix 
B
.

Optimistic proxies are hacked by padding.

Under

0.940 while true reward falls from 0.731 to
0.667, a terminal gap of 0.273 on the

the only ones whose citation counts inflate beyond the initialisation band,
reaching 7.5 and 8.6 features here, which is

Sufficiency cannot see padding. A late-training hacked explanation cites
eight features where three carry the decision, which makes the cited set
trivially sufficient (Appendix 
H

verbatim). Across the grid (Fig.

Appendix

leave every other cell within the first third of training while their word
counts stay unremarkable, so the hack targets the quantity the proxy cannot
see and leaves length alone. A pre-registered recall-only proxy, optimistic

against a true 0.692 with citations rising from

What gets exploited is optimism about a behaviour the policy can reach. The
sufficiency formula is incidental.

Outcome-only rewards starve training.

the symptom dynamic sampling treats

Yu and others, 2025

9 
Recommendations

Within the scope tested here (§
10
), our results support
four recommendations. Audit a process verifier’s selection error on a probe
grid before training against it. In our grids it predicted the phenotype
where reward accuracy did not, and it needs no policy model. Read the sign
too: optimistic verifiers were hacked and pessimistic ones only diluted, so
bias pessimistic when forced to choose. Do not train a process objective on
an outcome-only signal, which is blind to reasons and, once saturated,
silent. Give sufficiency- and recall-style rewards a precision term, since
alone they reward padding. An LLM judge can be used once its rankings pass a
held-out exact audit, as the one judge we trained against did.

10 
Limitations

Exactness is bought by a restriction to first-match and deny-overrides
(Cedar-style, where a matching forbid overrides any permit) gates over categorical contexts. The quantitative fit belongs to one grid
(one task family, one 4B model, one recipe, MLX 4-bit), its constants did
not transfer, that grid’s damage size is unexplained, and its outcome-only,
judge, recall-only and two of three noise cells are single-seed. No model
above 4B was trained, since an 8B model in 4-bit did not fit a T4. The
deterministic extractor anchors truth, audited by a held-out LLM extractor,
not humans. Corpus training was starved, so the corpus gives an audit and a
transfer test but no damage measurement, and ported policies abstract some
constructs (Appendix 
C
).

Broader Impact Statement

This work studies failure modes of reward verifiers using synthetic policy
gates and public, permissively licensed policy corpora. No human subjects,
personal data or proprietary data are involved. Understanding how proxy
rewards are hacked could in principle inform adversaries, but the attacks
demonstrated (padding, saturation exploitation) are already folklore. The
work contributes a defence, an audit that detects a dangerous verifier before
it is trained against.

Reproducibility Statement

Every number in this paper is generated from committed result files by a
single script. No number in any table or figure is hand-typed, and the few
narrative literals restate macro values or logged training printouts. The
full pipeline (oracle, verifiers, probe audit, trainer with per-step
dual-reward logs, corpus porting protocol with per-policy provenance,
Kaggle kernel and figure generation) will be released as one self-contained
repository, including the episode data and the SFT initialisation every run
starts from, so every figure and table regenerates from a clone. The
padding-signature prediction for the sufficiency cell was written before
that cell ran but was not timestamped, so we describe it as predicted rather
than pre-registered. The seven replication pre-registrations
(Appendix 
J
) are committed, timestamped files in the
artifact. The MLX grid ran on a single Apple-silicon laptop (4B model, LoRA,
about 30 hours). The replication grids ran on single NVIDIA T4 sessions.

AI Use Statement

LLMs appear in this work as subject models (the trained 4B policies and the
frontier models in the prompting study), as one of the audited verifiers
(the LLM-judge cell) and as the held-out extraction auditor. Beyond that,
LLM assistance was limited to grammatical checks on author-written text and
to coding assistance. No claim, result, figure or citation in this paper was
generated by an LLM, and all experimental results derive from the released
deterministic pipeline.

Appendix E 
Optimizer Fragility

Two negative results shaped the recipe. First, an off-the-shelf MLX GRPO
implementation produced checkpoints byte-identical to initialisation, a
silent zero-gradient no-op. The released trainer is a transparent REINFORCE
loop with group-relative advantage, the GRPO core at on-policy single-step,
with a first-step proof-of-life assertion on gradient norms and weight
deltas. Second, the initial recipe (learning rate 
, group 4, no
clipping) was seed-unstable. The exact-reward control itself collapsed on
one seed to a terminal true reward of 
, and under it the
noise
 cell collapsed to 
, against
0.678 under the final recipe. The two old-recipe figures in
this paragraph are in-training 8-episode evaluations. Those runs predate the
terminal re-scoring and saved no adapter to re-score, so they are not
comparable at the third decimal with the

everywhere else. The comparison they support is a collapse against a stable
level, which no measurement noise explains. Figure

both comparisons step by step: the noise cell decays roughly monotonically to
collapse under the fragile optimizer and holds a diluted level under the
clipped one, and the exact control survives both but dips sharply mid-run
without clipping. Verifier noise and optimizer fragility therefore compound:
a gap that is benign under a stable optimizer can be catastrophic under a
fragile one. All old-recipe runs are preserved in the artifact.

Appendix G 
Prompts and Probe Construction

Generation uses the audit-log prompt (context rendering, full policy text,
decision, “write 2 to 4 sentences”). Extraction uses the schema-generic
constrained-JSON prompt with the episode’s feature list. Probe texts are
rendered from templates whose phrases are written against the deterministic
extractor’s alias patterns, so each probe’s cited set is exact by
construction. The six probe types are faithful (canonical MSR), partial
(half the MSR), padded (all features), bare (decision word only),
wrong-decision and distractor-only. Verbatim prompt texts ship in the
artifact.

Abstract

Deep research, or any use of large language models (LLMs) to read a large document collection and produce insights, requires synthesizing information from different sources. In this work, we find that LLMs produce long-form research reports that read as confident syntheses but which fail to accurately reflect the underlying documents. We call this failure 
smoothing
. In collapsing diverse and potentially conflicting sources into a single narrative, the model discards the evidential “jaggedness” of its corpus and asserts conclusions more decisive than the citations can support. We introduce a claim level scoring pipeline, 
SmoothScore
, to quantify this effect. Our pipeline extracts key conclusive claims from model generated reports, then finds evidence relevant to each claim and clusters that evidence along high level aspects. Finally, each claim is scored with respect to the evidence to quantify the degree of smoothing done by the agent. Across 7 systems and 4 datasets, we find that agents consistently produce stronger claims than warranted

Introduction 

Figure 1: 
When synthesizing over many documents, agents can present a claim as well supported when the evidence is actually more mixed. 
SmoothScore
 flags these smoothed-over claims and quantifies the degree of smoothing.

Large Language Models (LLMs) are often deployed within systems that read across many sources of information and return long-form reports with citations 
(
Shao et al., 2026
; 
Xie et al., 2026
; 
Li et al., 2026b
; 
Nakano et al., 2021
)
. These systems, either general-purpose agents paired with Internet access or special-purpose “deep research” agents, retrieve large volumes of documents and analyze and synthesize this information into coherent reports 
(
Du et al., 2026
; 
Onweller et al., 2026
)
. The skills required to do this go beyond simple multi-document summarization  
(
Fabbri et al., 2019
)
: agents must weigh evidence, integrate conflicting findings, and determine what the evidence warrants 
(
Narayanan Venkit et al., 2026
; 
Rasheed et al., 2026
; 
Zhu et al., 2026
)
. The resulting reports are typically fluent, well-organized, and densely cited, which makes them read as confident, authoritative syntheses. As a result, users increasingly treat these outputs as substitutes for reading the underlying literature, even when the synthesis misrepresents or overstates what the cited sources actually support 
(
Li and Aral, 2025
; 
Huang et al., 2026
)
.

In this work, we study the prevalence of 
smoothing
 in LLM generated reports. We define smoothing as the tendency of LLMs to synthesize claims that are more decisive than supported by the documents they cite. Such claims may, for example, fail to portray mixed evidence or overclaim a result to hold in all circumstances when it has only been shown more narrowly. By contrast, a faithful claim should hedge when the evidence is mixed and narrow its scope when the support is narrow.

To identify instances of this behavior, we propose 
SmoothScore
, a framework for measuring whether the claims in a synthesized report are calibrated to the cited documents. As shown in Figure 
1
, we find that an agent’s report can present a claim as well supported by some of its citations, even though other retrieved evidence shows the claim only holds under certain conditions. The bulk of prior work on claim verification and long-form factuality 
(
Min et al., 2023
; 
Song et al., 2024
; 
Tang et al., 2024
)
 can check whether individual claims are supported by their cited evidence, but breaks down for synthesized claims: these systems usually return binary judgments at the claim or subclaim level, but do not handle calibration and scoping across a large body of potentially conflicting evidence.

To compute 
SmoothScore
, we first decompose each report into its main conclusive claims and identify the documents the report cites. As shown in Figure 
2
, for each claim, we run two agents over the report’s cited documents: (a) 
Support Agent
: which surfaces evidence consistent with the claim, and (b) 
Skeptic Agent
: which actively searches for evidence that qualifies, limits, or contradicts the claim. Evidence from both these passes are clustered into aspects reflecting different dimensions of the claim, such as viewpoint, strength of argument, and certainty 
(
Kargupta et al., 2025
)
.
Given the claim and its mapped aspect-evidence pairs, we score the claim’s calibration on a 1–5 scale, reflecting how well its stated strength and scope match the evidence collected across both passes.

Across four benchmarks and seven systems, we find that smoothing is pervasive. On average, for each system at least 25% of generated claims received a 
SmoothScore
 
. For most systems over half of claims show at least material smoothing, and up to 35% of Gemini Deep Research’s claims show severe smoothing (
). We also find that limited interaction with the cited documents contributes to smoothing: Luna opens only 19% of the pages it cites, and actively engaging with the full documents can help reduce smoothing.

Contributions
 Our main contributions are: (a) We introduce 
SmoothScore
, a framework for measuring whether the claims in a synthesized report are calibrated to the strength and scope of their cited evidence, rather than merely supported by it and (b) we find this issue to be widespread across frontier systems on 4 different benchmarks.

Smoothing in LLM Research Agents

2.1 
Defining Smoothing

Setup and Definitions

Given a prompt 
, a retrieval-augmented agent 
 produces a
long-form response or report 
 with inline citations. We collect the full text of the cited documents in the report

.
Our goal is to measure if the synthesized conclusive claims in 
 faithfully
represent what 
 supports.

We call a claim 
smoothed
 if it satisfies three criteria:(1) it reflects a conclusion drawn from aggregated evidence; (2) some of the evidence supports at least part of the conclusion; and (3) the conclusion as a whole is stronger or broader than the body of the evidence warrants.

This goal is closely related to hallucination detection

(
Zha et al., 2023
; 
Min et al., 2023
; 
Tang et al., 2024
)
; we view it as a special case of hallucination detection. However, typical methods for hallucination detection assume binary factuality judgments. Past work has looked at more nuanced judgments for narrative summarization 
(
Subbiah et al., 2025
)
 and opinion summarization 
(
Bhaskar et al., 2023
)
, but these methods are designed for simpler settings and do not handle the complexity of smoothing in deep research systems.

Evaluating whether a claim is smoothed requires more than checking whether a report’s statements are supported by individual citations. For instance, in Figure 
2
, the extracted claim 
‘discrete image-token modeling
appears more promising than a Stable-Diffusion-centered architecture’
 is a conclusion made by the deep research system (satisfying condition (1) above). It is supported by the cited documents, 
 Emu3 paper 
(
Wang et al., 2024
)
) and 
 (the Show-o paper 
(
Xie et al., 2025
)
) (satisfying condition (2)). However, we find that the same sources along with other documents, namely 
 (the Transfusion paper 
(
Zhou et al., 2024
)
), also contains results that show that the statement is only true under certain conditions present counter evidence (satisfying condition (3)).
Therefore, smoothing can occur even when a claim appears supported, and methods producing binary judgments from small evidence sets cannot comprehensively capture the distribution to identify it.

Identifying smoothing via aspects

We propose 
SmoothScore
 as a way of measuring the degree of smoothing a conclusion shows. Figure 
2
 outlines the pipeline. At a high level, first, we extract such claims from a generated report that represent the key takeaways, then find evidence that supports and refutes those claims. However, it is not straightforward to combine this evidence into a final score: counting supporting documents vs. refuting documents does not account for the nuances of 
how
 a claim is supported or refuted.

Instead, for the claim 
 in the report 
 and evidence from a cited document 
, we identify a set of dimensions 
 that we call 
aspects
, following 
(
Kargupta et al., 2025
)
. These aspects are consolidated across documents, to capture fine-grained ways in which a claim might be refuted. For instance, in Figure 
2
, step 3, we are able to find 
‘performance and efficiency of tokens vs diffusion models’
, under which we see some evidence state that 
‘tokens match or beat diffusion’
 but we also see evidence for 
‘diffusion or hybrid set ups are competitive’
. As aspects are discovered per claim and tailored to the claim and its evidence, we use them to score claim against the aspect-organized evidence to quantify its degree of smoothing.

Overview of 
SmoothScore
. Given a query and a system generated report, we (1) first extract conclusive claims. Then from the cited documents, (2) we get supporting and skeptic evidence relevant to the claim. (3)/(4) We then organize the evidence into

. (5) The claim is then scored with respect to the gathered evidence to quantify the degree of missing information.

2.2 
Measuring Smoothing with 
SmoothScore

Step 1: Claim Extraction

To focus on the agents’ ability to synthesize from aggregated evidence, we extract 
conclusion-bearing claims
 (condition (1)). These are cases where report takes a stance or presents a takeaway. For example, in Figure 
2
, 
‘image-token modeling
appears more promising than a Stable-Diffusion-centered
architecture
’ is a takeaway from the report which we extract as a claim.

Using prompt, Appendix 
B.1
, we extract,

Each claim preserves its direction, confidence, scope, and qualifications as stated in the report.

Step 2: Evidence Extraction

In Figure 
2
, we see that a cited document, for example, 
 can carry information relevant to the claim that can both support or undermine it. To identify information relevant to the claim from the cited documents throughout the report, we extract evidence using a ‘support agent’ and a ‘skeptic’ agent.

•

Support Agent (Appendix 
B.2
): Given (claim, document), this agent extracts evidence that supports the assertions made by the claim. We also include stated concession as evidence when they confirm limitations or conditions present within the claim.

•

Skeptic Agent (Appendix 
B.3
): Given (claim, document), this agent extracts evidence that contains any information against the claim. To capture the “jaggedness” we go beyond just finding counters to the assertions made by the claim. The agent also extracts evidence to support limitations and conditions stated in the claims. Additionally, we include evidence on alternate approaches and their success related to the claims where relevant. This is to provide context for scope.

For each agent, the evidence is composed of: a source quote from the document, aspect detailing the assertion, a note on how the evidence bears on the claim, and strength rating.

Step 3: Aspect Induction

Different cited documents can be relevant to different parts of a claim, so judging the claim against the raw documents forces the judge to compare evidence that is not directly comparable. If we look across the cited documents, however, the relevant information naturally clusters into topics that bear on the claim. For example, in Figure 
2
, some documents discuss 
comparing the performance and efficiency of image tokens vs. diffusion approaches
, while others discuss 
fit with LLM-style next-token scaling
. We refer to these topics as 
aspects
 
(
Kargupta et al., 2025
)
.

To construct aspects, for each claim–document pair 
 we generate a set of document-specific aspect proposals,

Each proposal identifies a dimension discussed in 
 that bears on 
, and consists of a short name, a brief description, and a set of keywords. If 
 contains nothing relevant to 
, then 
 (see Appendix 
B.4
 for the prompt). Because proposals are generated independently for each document, different documents will often propose overlapping or near-duplicate aspects. For each claim, we therefore merge the proposals across documents into a single, deduplicated set of aspects, keeping track of which documents contributed to each one (see Appendix 
B.5
).

Organizing evidence by aspect places findings that address the same part of the claim side by side, regardless of which document they came from. This allows the judge to weigh evidence both supporting and opposing the claim on a like-for-like basis. For example, along a comparative dimension there may be supporting and countervailing evidence for either stance, as seen in aspect 
A2
 of Figure 
2
.

Step 4: Evidence Assignment

We assign every extracted evidence item in 
 to the aspect it discusses, so that findings addressing the same part of the claim are grouped together and can be compared directly (prompt in Appendix 
B.6
). Assignment depends only on 
what
 the evidence discusses, not on whether it supports or counters the claim, so an item’s stance, source, and strength rating are left unchanged. For example, in Figure 
2
, the support-agent finds that 
Emu3 beats SDXL in human evaluation
 and the skeptic-agent finding that 
Emu3’s own table shows a lower GenEval score than SDXL
 are placed under the same aspect, even though they point in opposite directions.

Step 5: Computing Smoothing

Finally, a judge 
 produces a 
SmoothScore
 
 for each claim. The judge receives the input question 
, the claim 
, and all extracted evidence 
 organized under the claim’s aspects 
, including evidence that is not tied to any single aspect:

The judge assesses how faithfully the claim compresses the supplied evidence, focusing on whether it omits or understates assertions, conditions, or scope in a way that changes its central takeaway. Using the five-point rubric in  
B.7
 we score degree of smoothing (condition (3)). The criteria for receiving a score of 5 includes cases that are pure hallucination and don’t match condition (2). However, we rarely observe cases of 5 (Section 
4
) and therefore view our system as primarily a method for analyzing smoothing rather than broader hallucination detection (even though it can identify such cases). Figure 
2
 and Table

 show examples of the generated outputs for each stage of the pipeline.

4.2 
Qualitative Observations

Query framing influences 
SmoothScore
.

We find that query framing influences how the model writes its claims. As seen in Appendix 
G.1
 and  
G.2
, there are certain categories of questions where smoothing is more pronounced: (1) Comparative questions: if the query is comparative in nature, for example, 
‘In multimodal pretraining, the paradigms are based on image tokens and stable diffusion, which appears more promising and why?’
 or 
‘Which paradigm between RL and distillation appears more promising and why?’
, it can presuppose a choice from the agent where to motivate or justify a choice the agent drops important qualifications, (2) Topic overview questions: queries that require an overview of a topic, for example, 
‘Describe the Sunflower conjecture, progress towards a solution, and related problems’
, the agent can assemble multiple results a single conclusion or just generalize from a single finding to the whole topic.

Recurring Smoothing patterns across systems

Table 
2
 shows three patterns that we observe across systems: (1) 
Unsupported composition
: Agents combine individual fragments of evidence into a stronger conclusion. Although each component may be supported, the claim presents its unsupported composition as a conclusive statement. (2) 
Scope expansion
: A claim takes an established result for a particular setup or experimental condition and removes certain boundaries, so that a the scope for an empirical observation expands into a general conclusion.
(3) 
Premature Closure
: Claims often adopt conclusive language, using phrases like 
‘strictly preserving,’
 
‘the best approach,’
 
‘definitively proves’
 and 
‘most optimal.’
.

Related Work

Claim decomposition and aspect-based verification

A standard recipe for verifying long-form text is to decompose it into smaller checkable units and score each against a knowledge source  
(
Min et al., 2023
; 
Chen et al., 2024
; 
Gunjal and Durrett, 2024
)
. These methods generally return a binary decision per claim, which is a good granularity for factual precision, but it does not work for synthesized claims that can have varying degrees of smoothing. ConflictScore 
(
Liu et al., 2026
)
 is the closest to our setting, and it extends this atomic claim recipe in a direction close to ours. They mark a claim as supported, contradicted or irrelevant per document retrieved, and aggregate these into claim-level conflict measures. However, a claim can be labeled 
supported
 by every one of its cited documents and still smooth over information it omits. Our work instead follows 
Kargupta et al. (2025)
 in organizing evidence along 
aspects
 (e.g., viewpoint, strength, certainty), so that supporting and qualifying evidence on the feature of a claim can be considered against each other.

Multi-Document Summarization

In science communication, prior work measures certainty in how findings are stated 
(
Pei and Jurgens, 2021
)
 and models how information changes as findings move from papers to news and social media 
(
Wright et al., 2022
)
. For LLM summaries of scientific text, 
Peters and Chin-Yee (2025)
 test ten widely used LLMs on about 4,900 summaries and find that most produce broader generalizations than the source texts, and that LLM summaries are nearly five times more likely than human-written ones to overgeneralize. In medicine, neural multi-document summaries of clinical trials are fluent but often factually inaccurate 
(
Wallace et al., 2021
)
, and LLM summaries of medical evidence can misstate the certainty of the evidence or overgeneralize by omitting attributes such as a disease subtype 
(
Tang et al., 2023
)
. These studies summarize a single document or a small set of abstracts. We extend the concern to long-form, multi-document research reports written by agents, and give a claim-level measure grounded in the documents a report cites.

Correctness evaluation for deep research systems

Agentic deep-research systems 
(
Du et al., 2026
; 
Xu et al., 2026
; 
Huang et al., 2026
; 
Li et al., 2026a
; 
Sharma et al., 2025
)
 pair rubric-based insight coverage with citation grounded factuality checks. Several works audit the reliability of these systems. DeepTRACE 
(
Narayanan Venkit et al., 2026
)
 decomposes answers into statements and finds that deep-research agents remain one-sided on debate queries and include many statements unsupported by their listed sources. 
Onweller et al. (2026)
 find that citations that resolve and are topically relevant often still fail a factual check against the cited content. 
Zhu et al. (2026)
 show that a single misleading document can induce false conclusions in final reports. 
Rasheed et al. (2026)
 argue for claim-level auditability, including whether conflicts in the evidence are surfaced or suppressed. Across this literature, factuality is largely operationalized as citation validity: whether a claim is supported by some retrieved source. Our results show that this is not enough. Reports whose claims are individually supported can still present mixed or conditional evidence as a decisive conclusion, so assessing how faithfully the underlying evidence is 
presented
 requires the claim-level, evidence-calibrated analysis we propose.

8 
Conclusion

In this work, we studied the propensity of LLMs to 
smooth
 their conclusions when generating reports based on sources. They frequently generate oversimplified statements or conclusions that aren’t really backed up by the evidence cited. Our pipeline, 
SmoothScore
, allows us to quantify this and establish its frequency across several state-of-the-art agents. Finally, we find that this models fail to read with their own cited documents, and suggest smoothing can improved by more thorough agents engaging wih their sources.

Aspect Induction

As mentioned in

Merging is needed so that evidence on the same topic is grouped together, rather than being split across near-duplicate aspects.

Emitting the assertive half of a hedged or paired statement is the worst possible error – if the pieces cannot be included coherently, skip the conclusion entirely.

2. CARRY THE SCOPE. When a verdict’s scope lives just OUTSIDE its sentence – in the immediately governing heading, a dimension label, or the sentence before – stitch that report wording in front (join stitched spans naturally; the heading text is report wording). NEVER present a per-dimension or per-condition winner as a global verdict.

6. STRIP DISCOURSE CONNECTIVES at the very start of a claim (”On the other hand,”, ”In summary,”, ”However,”, ”Overall,”) – but connectives INSIDE the claim stay; they are doing real work joining the verdict to its counterpart.

Prompt B.7: Evidence-Smoothing Judgment

System Prompt:

You are an evidence-smoothing judge. You assess how faithfully a claim compresses the evidence supplied to it — not whether the claim is well-written, complete, or true by outside standards.

User Prompt:

# Inputs

Research question:
{{QUESTION}}

Claim under evaluation:
{{CLAIM}}

Evidence by aspect:
{{ASPECTS_AND_EVIDENCE}}

Each evidence item may include: a source identifier, an exact quotation, a generated context note, and a retrieval-pass label (”support-pass candidate” / ”countervailing-pass candidate”).

# What you’re measuring

Evidence smoothing: the claim collapses material heterogeneity, uncertainty, conditionality, or disagreement in the evidence, producing a conclusion broader, more certain, more universal, or more decisive than the evidence proportionately warrants.

Not in scope: coverage (the claim need not mention every finding), writing quality, or accuracy against knowledge outside the supplied evidence.

# Ground rules for reading the evidence

1. **The supplied evidence is the entire universe.** No outside knowledge.

2. **Retrieval labels describe provenance, not verified stance.** Interpret each item’s actual content independently — a support-pass item can fail to support the claim; a countervailing-pass item can support, qualify, or be irrelevant to it.

3. **Item counts and pass labels are not evidence of consensus or its absence.** Never infer balance, or lack of it, from how many items landed in each pass.

4. **Context notes clarify; they don’t add evidence.** Ground judgments in the quotation. Preserve the claim’s actual scope, certainty, and role in answering the question. Do not weaken a strong assertion into a vague statement of usefulness, or strengthen a qualified claim into an unconditional guarantee. 

5. **Weigh by relevance, directness, applicability, and any quality signal stated in the text — not by quote count.** Restatements of the same finding are one data point, not several.

6. **The claim’s own logical force sets what can defeat it:**
- ”X can work” — isolated failures of X don’t count against it.
- ”X always / necessarily works” — one sound counterexample is decisive.
- ”X generally works / dominates” — isolated counterexamples aren’t enough; the evidence must speak to prevalence or the overall comparison.
- ”X is promising” — a rival promising method doesn’t refute it.
- ”X is comparatively best” — comparable alternatives are directly relevant.
- A claim need not preempt exceptions its own wording already allows.

7. **Absence of support 
 contradiction**, but an unsupported specific assertion is still unwarranted — weight this by whether it’s peripheral or central to the takeaway.

8. **For multi-part claims, weight problems by their effect on the overall takeaway.** Don’t average proposition-level defects or default to the score triggered by the single worst one.

# Procedure

Work through these in order. Your response’s structure follows this order (see Output).

1. **Claim’s contribution.** What is the claim doing relative to the question — explaining a possibility, asserting a general pattern, reporting a result, making a recommendation? This determines what kind of evidence can defeat it.

2. **Substantive takeaway.** State the scope, certainty, comparison, and causal content the claim actually asserts, independent of phrasing. Don’t pre-weaken an overstated claim before judging it.

3. **Positive evidential case.** What do the relevant findings, taken together, justify? Include reasonable inferences across findings. Flag anything the claim asserts that the evidence doesn’t actually reach (a specific mechanism, magnitude, or comparison it never establishes), even if the surrounding evidence is generally favorable.

4. **Objections.** For each apparent counterpoint: does it address the same assertion, in the applicable setting? Distinguish a consequential restriction from a compatible failure, a different mechanism, or an unrelated limitation. Treat a finding as an outlier only when the evidence itself gives a basis for that, not because it’s outnumbered or inconvenient.

5. **Net resolution.** Combine 3 and 4. Does the claim survive as stated, survive with a limitation attached, or require a substantially different conclusion?

Abstract

Spam detection is usually treated as single-message classification, ignoring behavioral patterns that emerge across sequences of messages from the same sender. We study outbound SMS spam detection at the carrier level, where the goal is to predict at send time whether a carrier will flag a message as spam. Using a proprietary production database of 43,741 outbound SMS messages with UTC transmission timestamps and carrier delivery statuses, we report three sets of results. On an 8,749-message held-out test set, a TF-IDF + logistic regression baseline reaches AUC-ROC 0.8721, and a text-only DeBERTa-v3-base classifier reaches AUC-ROC 0.9011 and F1 0.8412. A deployed DistilBERT model augmented with inter-message temporal features reaches accuracy 0.8238, spam-class F1 0.8382, and recall 0.9128 at threshold 0.5 on a separate, class-balanced 20,000-message production evaluation set; these results are not directly comparable with the held-out test results. We also describe 
TemporalBertSpam
, an architecture that encodes each sender’s message history with a causal Transformer, and outline the same-split evaluation needed to measure its contribution.

1 
Introduction

SMS carriers run automated filters that flag outbound messages as spam based on content, sender reputation, and behavioral signals. These carrier-side filters differ from classic spam classifiers, which label inbound unsolicited messages: here the task is to predict, at send time, whether the carrier network will block a message. This framing matters in practice, because a blocked message is never delivered and false positives harm legitimate senders.

Most prior NLP work on spam detection classifies single messages with transformer-based text encoders 
(
Jamal et al., 2024
; 
Asliyuksek et al., 2025
)
. These approaches treat each message independently. However, spam campaigns often vary their wording (spintax) to evade per-message text filters while still showing detectable behavior: high-volume bursts, regular intervals between messages, and many recipients reached from a single phone number in a short time.

This paper studies whether information about the temporal sequence of a sender’s messages can complement message content. We frame three research questions:

•

RQ1:
 Do inter-message temporal features (time delta 
 and 
) improve detection over a text-only transformer baseline?

•

RQ2:
 Does sequence-level modeling of sender histories improve detection over temporal features alone?

•

RQ3:
 How do message content type (URL presence, urgency language) and sender volume relate to detection performance?

This paper reports baseline results on a held-out test set and production results for a deployed temporal-feature model. Answering RQ1 and RQ2 requires evaluating all systems on a single time-ordered split, and RQ3 requires a subgroup analysis on that same split; we leave both to future work (see Limitations). Our dataset contains per-message timestamps, which are absent from common benchmarks such as the SMS Spam Collection 
(
Almeida et al., 2011
)
.

2 
Related Work

Transformer-based text classifiers for spam

Devlin et al. (2019)
 introduced BERT, which became the dominant paradigm for NLP classification. 
Sanh et al. (2019)
 distilled BERT into DistilBERT, which is 40% smaller while retaining 97% of BERT’s language understanding performance, making it well suited to high-throughput inference. 
Jamal et al. (2024)
 fine-tune DistilBERT and RoBERTa on a three-class phishing/spam/ham task, reporting 97.1% and 99.0% test accuracy respectively. Their labels come from human-annotated corpora; our labels reflect network-level carrier decisions on outbound messages.

Combining text with non-textual features

Asliyuksek et al. (2025)
 combine DistilBERT representations with static structural features of each email and report 99.62% accuracy on a combined corpus of 81,586 emails from seven datasets. Their non-textual features are computed per message, with no cross-message temporal component. Our approach instead targets behavioral patterns across sequences of messages from the same sender.

Behavioral and temporal signals in spam detection

Network and security research has long used sender behavior rather than content. 
Ramachandran et al. (2007)
 classify email senders by their sending patterns across target domains, and 
Hao et al. (2009)
 detect email spammers with lightweight spatio-temporal network-level features. For SMS, 
Xu et al. (2012)
 detect spammers on the service side using noncontent features, including temporal and network features, without inspecting message text. At carrier scale, 
Jiang et al. (2013b)
 find that spam numbers with similar content show strongly similar sending patterns, and 
Jiang et al. (2013a)
 detect SMS spam numbers from their sending behavior in a large cellular network. Surveys of SMS spam filtering methods and data 
(
Delany et al., 2012
)
 focus mainly on content-based methods. Our work combines a transformer text encoder with temporal features and, in the full TemporalBertSpam design, sequence modeling over each sender’s history.

4 
Dataset

4.1 
Production SMS Database

Our data come from the outbound SMS database of a commercial CRM platform that sends messages through the Telnyx telecommunications API. Records were pulled from the platform’s PostgreSQL database in streaming batches of 10,000 rows, joining message records (text, sender number, send timestamp) with carrier delivery metadata (status and error codes).

Data scope

The raw pull contains 59,342 outbound SMS messages sent between September 22 and December 19, 2025 (about 89 days), from multiple outbound phone numbers used by CRM customers for appointment reminders, marketing, and transactional notifications. We keep only the two terminal statuses that reflect carrier filtering decisions, 
spam

delivered

Labels come from carrier delivery statuses, not human annotation. A message labeled

Class distribution

The dataset contains 18,283 spam messages (41.8%) and 25,458 delivered messages (58.2%). We report AUC-ROC alongside F1 because of this imbalance.

Timestamps and sender identity

Each message has a UTC transmission timestamp at microsecond precision, recorded from Telnyx webhook events. The sender field holds the outbound phone number (E.164 format), which we use only to group messages into per-sender sequences. Sender numbers are not reported anywhere in this paper.

4.2 
Preprocessing

Near-duplicate templates

Spam campaigns often send many near-duplicate variants of one template. No messages were removed for near-duplication, so all 43,741 messages are used for modeling and near-duplicate variants may remain in the data. Section 
8.2
 describes a screening procedure for future experiments.

Train/test split

For the text baselines we use an 80/20 split stratified on the label, giving 34,992 training and 8,749 test messages (41.8% spam in each). Because this split is random, messages from the same sender and template can appear in both partitions. For the sequence model we specify a time-ordered split, with the earliest messages used for training and the most recent for testing, to avoid temporal leakage.

Evaluation Setup

Metrics

We report AUC-ROC (threshold-free and invariant to label encoding) for the text baselines, and F1 and accuracy for all models. Because the class balance differs between our two evaluation sets, we state the positive class for each F1 value.

Evaluation sets

Text baselines are evaluated on the 8,749-message held-out test set. The deployed DistilBERT + temporal model is evaluated on a 20,000-message set drawn from the production monitoring system, with 10,000 spam and 10,000 delivered messages. Results across the two sets are not directly comparable.

8 
Discussion

8.1 
Expected Behavior of the Full Model

The following are design hypotheses for TemporalBertSpam, not measured results.

High-volume campaigns

Burst sending produces short intervals (
) and many messages from one sender. The sequence encoder is designed to associate such patterns with higher spam probability, independently of text, which a per-message classifier cannot observe.

Spintax evasion

When individual variants look benign to the text encoder, the sending pattern (regular intervals, many messages from one number) may still provide a useful signal through the causal attention over earlier messages.

Cold start

For a sender’s first message, no history exists, and the model relies on content and the temporal features of that message alone.

8.2 
Near-Duplicate Screening for Future Experiments

We propose the following screening procedure. Each message is lowercased, whitespace is collapsed, and the text is represented as a TF-IDF vector over unigrams and bigrams, with weight 
 and sublinear term frequency 
. Vectors are L2-normalized, so cosine similarity reduces to a dot product. Messages are processed in corpus order; a message is discarded if its maximum cosine similarity to any retained message is at least a threshold such as 0.9.

A high threshold catches variants that differ in only a few tokens, but spintax variants that swap many words or phrases share less vocabulary and fall below it. Since near-duplicates across the train/test split can inflate metrics, combining lexical screening with embedding-based deduplication 
(
Reimers and Gurevych, 2019
)
 is a priority for future experiments.

8.3 
Positional Encoding Design

An alternative design would feed temporal features as extra token embeddings into DistilBERT. That would mix inter-message information into a model pre-trained only on intra-message token sequences. Keeping a separate sequence Transformer with its own sinusoidal encoding keeps the two levels apart and makes the ablation clean: removing the sequence Transformer (the DistilBERT + temporal model) changes nothing in the text encoder. Sinusoidal encoding also adds no parameters, which matters given the limited number of distinct sender histories.

8.4 
Comparison to 
Asliyuksek et al. (2025)

Asliyuksek et al. (2025)
 combine DistilBERT with static structural features and reach 99.62% accuracy on a combined email corpus. Our setting differs in three ways: (1) labels come from carrier decisions rather than curated corpora, which adds noise but reflects real network behavior; (2) our non-textual features are cross-message temporal signals rather than per-message statistics; (3) TemporalBertSpam explicitly models dependencies across a sender’s messages. Given these differences in domain, labels, and data, accuracy figures are not directly comparable.

8.5 
Newer Encoders

DistilBERT (2019) and DeBERTaV3 (2021) predate several stronger encoders. ModernBERT 
(
Warner et al., 2025
)
 is trained on 2 trillion tokens with a native 8,192-token context; for SMS, which is short, its benefit would come mainly from better pre-training rather than longer context. Sentence-BERT 
(
Reimers and Gurevych, 2019
)
 and later embedding models could replace TF-IDF deduplication with semantic deduplication that catches paraphrased spintax. LLM-based embeddings could better capture commercial intent in novel phrasings.

A stronger text encoder would mainly help with hard cases: messages that are spam in intent but phrased in ways the encoder’s pre-training data did not cover. Behavioral signals such as burst sending and short intervals are independent of content, so we expect temporal information to remain useful even with a stronger encoder; testing this with ModernBERT or an LLM encoder at Stage 1 is a natural next experiment.

9 
Conclusion

We study outbound SMS spam detection with carrier delivery labels and real transmission timestamps, using 43,741 production messages. A text-only DeBERTa-v3-base baseline reaches AUC-ROC 0.9011 and F1 0.8412 on a held-out test set, and a deployed DistilBERT + temporal model reaches spam-class F1 0.8382 and recall 0.9128 at threshold 0.5 on a separate, balanced 20,000-message production set. Unlike common public SMS spam benchmarks, the dataset includes per-message timestamps and carrier-decision labels. We also describe TemporalBertSpam, which models each sender’s history with a causal Transformer.

The key next step is to evaluate the TF-IDF, DeBERTa, DistilBERT + temporal, and TemporalBertSpam models on one time-ordered split, which would directly answer RQ1 and RQ2, together with a subgroup analysis by message type and sender volume on the same split (RQ3). Further directions include near-duplicate removal, newer encoders at Stage 1, and few-shot methods for senders with sparse histories.

Limitations

Incomplete comparison

The text baselines and the deployed model are evaluated on different sets with different class balance (41.8% vs. 50% spam), so we cannot yet attribute performance differences to temporal features. The full TemporalBertSpam model has not been evaluated in this paper.

Evaluation protocol

The text baselines use a random split, so messages from the same sender or template can appear in both training and test data, which may inflate their scores. The production evaluation set was drawn from the monitoring system, and we do not document its sampling procedure or rule out overlap with the validation data of the deployed model version. The production decision threshold was selected on the same set used for evaluation. Production results should therefore be read as optimistic.

Label noise and selection bias

Carrier statuses are an imperfect proxy for spam, and carrier filtering policies may change over time. The production system uses a stochastic send policy that holds some messages; held messages receive no carrier label, so the labeled data are not a random sample of all attempted messages.

Near-duplicates

No near-duplicate removal was applied, so templated variants may appear in both training and test data.

Generality

The data come from one CRM platform, one messaging provider, and an 89-day window, and the dataset cannot be released because it is proprietary. Results may not transfer to other platforms, regions, or periods.

Ethics Statement

Message text contains real SMS content sent by CRM users. We report only aggregate statistics and model metrics; and no message text, phone number, or sender identifier is reproduced. The data were accessed under the data use agreement governing the authors’ access to the platform’s systems.

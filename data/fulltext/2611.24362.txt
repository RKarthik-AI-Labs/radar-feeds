Abstract

Large language model (LLM) agents routinely interact with untrusted external data, exposing them to indirect prompt injection (IPI) attacks that hijack agents into executing attacker-chosen tasks. To uncover these vulnerabilities at scale, automated red-teaming methods often leverage reinforcement learning (RL) to generate attacks that achieve high attack success rates (ASR). Model developers use the attacks discovered during internal red-teaming to harden agents through adversarial training before deployment, a collaborative offensive-defensive process known in cybersecurity as 
purple-teaming
. However, we show that achieving high ASR during red-teaming does not guarantee robustness gains from subsequent adversarial training. Specifically, we find that two state-of-the-art RL-based red-teaming methods, RL-Hammer and PISmith, suffer from mode collapse, repeatedly discovering similar attack strategies whose additional successes add no additional robustness improvements to adversarial training. To address this gap, we introduce 
Pipette
, a unified prompt injection purple-teaming framework that effectively converts red-teaming discoveries into defender robustness improvements. 
Pipette
 employs a dictionary learning-based reward that encourages broader coverage of attack strategies during red-teaming. It also integrates an on-policy adversarial training pipeline tailored to IPI defense, enabling agents to resist attacks while completing legitimate user tasks. Extensive evaluations across agentic benchmarks (AgentDojo and ASB) demonstrate that 
Pipette
 produces defenders that achieve near-zero attack success rates on static attacks and can withstand strong adaptive attacks, while maintaining benign task utility close to that of the undefended baseline.

I 
Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed as autonomous agents that execute multi-step tasks, such as managing emails, querying databases, and invoking external APIs 
[
1
, 
2
]
. To complete a user task specified by a trusted user instruction, an LLM agent must process untrusted external data retrieved from third-party sources, including web pages, documents, and tool outputs. Because LLMs cannot reliably distinguish between user instructions and external data

, an attacker can embed a malicious injected instruction inside external data to override the user instruction and redirect the agent toward an attacker-chosen injection task

. This vulnerability, known as

Despite the effectiveness of these methods as offensive stress tests, we show that

, 
17
]
, yet existing RL-based red-teaming methods optimize their policy primarily to maximize attack success. Such an objective can lead to 
mode collapse
, a failure in which the policy converges to a narrow set of outputs that repeatedly exploit the same vulnerability 
[
18
, 
16
]
. Specifically, once the red-teaming policy discovers a single effective 
attack strategy
, a way of overriding the user instruction such as claiming that the user task is already complete, it tends to reuse that strategy across rollouts to collect high reward. To mitigate mode collapse, PISmith 
[
12
]
 and RL-Hammer 
[
11
]

reward hacking
 by rephrasing sentences or inserting unrelated text while relying on the same attack strategy. As a result, training an agent on thousands of rephrased versions of one attack strategy may patch only a single vulnerability and leave the agent exposed to unseen attacks at deployment.

Even when red-teaming uncovers distinct attacks, converting those discoveries into a practical defense is a second challenge. A useful prompt injection defense requires preserving 
benign utility
 on clean inputs and 
utility under attack
 when external data is contaminated 
[
2
]
. Unlike jailbreak attacks 
[
19
]
, where the user is the attacker and the agent can refuse the harmful request, an agent under IPI must ignore the injected instruction inside external data while still using the surrounding data to complete the benign user instruction. Yet standard RL defenses 
[
20
]
 train the agent with trajectory-level, 
outcome-based rewards
 that assign a single scalar score to the entire trajectory based on whether the task succeeded or failed. Because such a score cannot identify which tokens caused the failure 
[
21
]
, agents trained with these rewards can learn to abort execution or refuse the user whenever external data appears suspicious, and thus sacrifice task completion for lower ASR.

To address both challenges, we introduce 
Pipette
, a unified prompt injection 
purple-teaming
 framework that coordinates red-teaming and adversarial training toward a single objective of maximizing the hardened agent’s robustness. First, to mitigate mode collapse, 
Pipette
 guides the red-teaming policy with a dictionary learning-based reward. We decompose generated attacks into a sparse dictionary of reusable components that capture distinct attack strategies, and reward the red-teaming policy when a successful attack cannot be explained by previously discovered components. Second, to preserve benign utility during adversarial training, 
Pipette
 trains the agent on the collected attacks by combining reasoning-guided RL with token-level on-policy self-distillation (OPSD) 
[
22
, 
21
]
. By assigning credit to individual tokens rather than relying solely on trajectory-level outcome rewards, 
Pipette
 aims to suppress reasoning and action tokens driven by the injected instruction while reinforcing tokens needed for the user task.

We evaluate 
Pipette
 on two standard IPI benchmarks under static and iterative-adaptive attacks, and summarize our contributions as follows:

•

We show empirically that maximizing ASR or surface-level text diversity in state-of-the-art RL-based red-teaming methods 
[
11
, 
12
]
 does not prevent mode collapse at the strategy level, which likely limits the robustness gains of subsequent adversarial training (§
III-B
 and §
IV-A
).

•

We design a dictionary learning-based strategy coverage reward that guides the red-teaming policy to discover new attack strategies rather than surface-level wording variations (§
IV-B
).

•

We pair our red-teaming stage with an on-policy adversarial training pipeline using token-level self-distillation to preserve benign utility while resisting injected instructions (§
IV-C
).

•

We show on two benchmarks that 
Pipette
 compares favorably with existing red-teaming and defense baselines, with ASR under iterative-adaptive attacks on AgentDojo ranging from 
 to 
 across the three models tested and benign utility remaining close to that of the undefended policies on all four models (§

VII 
Conclusion

We find that pairing high-ASR RL-based red-teaming with standard adversarial training does not necessarily make LLM agents robust to unseen attacks. The red-teamers we study exhibit strategy-level mode collapse, and adversarial training with off-policy SFT or trajectory-level outcome rewards can reduce benign utility. To address both problems, we present 
Pipette
, a two-stage prompt injection purple-teaming framework. 
Pipette
-Red guides RL-based red-teaming with a rolling strategy dictionary to keep uncovering new attack strategies, and 
Pipette
-Blue then combines task-alignment reasoning with a contrastive token-level reward to help the agent ignore injections while completing user tasks. Across four open-weight models trained on AgentDyn, 
Pipette
 achieves lower average ASR on the unseen AgentDojo and ASB benchmarks than the training-based and non-training-based baselines while preserving benign utility.

Abstract.

Product search is a crucial component of large-scale e-commerce platforms, where first-stage retrieval should achieve high recall while ensuring efficient online deployment.
Sparse retrieval remains attractive for this setting due to its interpretability and deployability, yet it suffers from severe vocabulary mismatch challenges, especially for short queries and product titles.
Large language models (LLMs) offer strong semantic capabilities, but directly applying them to sparse retrieval in product search introduces new challenges:

(i)
 
semantic expansion may distort critical literal signals such as brand names and model identifiers; and

(ii)
 
learning meaningful sparse representations is difficult in the high-dimensional vocabulary space induced by LLMs.

To address these challenges, we propose PROSPER, a framework that uses LLMs as sparse retrievers for product search while explicitly preserving lexical fidelity.
PROSPER combines

(i)
 
a literal residual network that alleviates lexical expansion hallucination by reinforcing underweighted literal terms through a residual compensation mechanism; and

(ii)
 
a lexical focusing window that uses top-
 selection for rapid early sparsification and effective training initialization.

Extensive offline evaluations and large-scale online A/B tests demonstrate that PROSPER consistently improves over existing sparse retrievers, and provides revenue gains for industrial hybrid retrieval systems.

Keywords: 
Sparse retrieval, Large language models, Product search

1. 
Introduction

E-commerce product search connects users with billion-scale product catalogs while processing hundreds of millions of daily queries under strict latency and memory constraints.
Industrial retrieval pipelines typically follow the “index-retrieve-then-rank” 
(
Li et al., 2024
)
 paradigm, where first-stage retrieval plays a key role by establishing the upper bound of achievable recall, i.e., relevant products missed at this stage cannot be recovered downstream.

Dense retrieval: effectiveness at the cost of deployability.
 Dense retrieval has emerged as the dominant paradigm for first-stage retrieval, using neural encoders to map queries and documents into low-dimensional semantic spaces 
(
Guo et al., 2022
; 
Karpukhin et al., 2020
; 
Khattab and Zaharia, 2020
)
.
Despite their effectiveness, dense representations are difficult to interpret, hindering failure diagnosis in commercial systems. Dense indices also incur substantial storage and serving overheads and can be several times larger than the original corpus 
(
Ma et al., 2024
; 
Xu et al., 2025
)
. These limitations motivate retrieval methods that better balance effectiveness, efficiency, and interpretability.

Sparse retrieval: efficiency and interpretability, but limited semantics.
 Sparse retrieval represents queries and documents as high-dimensional but sparse vectors aligned with lexical vocabularies, naturally supporting efficient inverted indexing and transparent matching 
(
Spärck Jones, 1972
; 
Robertson and Zaragoza, 2009
; 
Formal et al., 2021b
)
.
They therefore remain a core component of industrial search systems. Learned sparse retrievers, most notably SPLADE 
(
Zamani et al., 2018
; 
Dai and Callan, 2020b
; 
Formal et al., 2021b
)
, jointly learn lexical expansion and term weighting, improving over classical statistical approaches. However, their masked language model backbones still struggle with vocabulary mismatch in product search, where short queries and titles are dominated by literal terms such as brands and model identifiers, leaving a persistent gap compared to dense retrievers.

LLMs as sparse retrievers: opportunities and challenges.
 Large language models (LLMs) offer stronger semantic representations and broader world knowledge than prior encoder architectures 
(
Qwen et al., 2025
; 
Tao et al., 2024
; 
Minaee et al., 2025
)
, making them promising backbones for combining semantic understanding with efficient and interpretable sparse representations.
However, our preliminary analysis (Section 
2.2
) reveals that naively replacing BERT with an LLM in existing sparse frameworks is ineffective and even detrimental in product search.
Two fundamental challenges arise:

(i)
 
Semantic expansion driven by LLMs often distorts critical lexical signals: expanded terms may dominate the sparse representation, while exact-match terms, such as brands or model numbers that are essential for user intent, are underweighted.

(ii)
 
The vastly enlarged vocabulary of LLMs makes training unstable and inefficient. Learning sparse representations for short queries and titles in a very high-dimensional space without guidance leads to poor initialization and slow convergence.

PROSPER: Literal-preserving LLM-based sparse retrieval.

To address these challenges, we propose PROSPER, a framework for PROduct search using LLMs as SParsE Retrievers while explicitly preserving lexical fidelity.
PROSPER contributes at both the architectural and training levels:

(i)
 
We design a 
literal residual network
 (LRN) that employs a compensatory weighting mechanism to amplify the importance of literal terms in user queries and items. This effectively mitigates irrelevant term generation (which we term 
lexical expansion hallucination
) by anchoring the model’s attention to user-critical tokens, such as brand and model names.

(ii)
 
We apply top-
 sparsification 
(
Yang et al., 2021
; 
Xie et al., 2026
)
 through a 
lexical focusing window
 (LFW) and find it effective for product search, improving retrieval and accelerating early sparsification. As representations become sparse, the window becomes inactive and FLOPS regularization continues to refine term weights.

Overall, we present an empirical study of the challenges and practical solutions for LLM-based sparse retrieval in product search.

Experimental findings.

We evaluate PROSPER on both the Multi-CPR E-commerce dataset and a real-world dataset sampled from search engine logs.
On Multi-CPR E-commerce, PROSPER improves Hit@1000 by 10.2, 4.3, and 1.5 percentage points over BM25
(
Robertson and Zaragoza, 2009
)
, SPLADE-v2 
(
Formal et al., 2021a
)
, and an LLM-based sparse retrieval baseline 
(
Xu et al., 2025
)
, respectively.
Moreover, large-scale online A/B tests demonstrate consistent gains in key business metrics. In direct-guided settings, PROSPER improves net GMV by 1.60%, net transactions by 0.31%, and net UV by 0.24%, further validating the practical value of PROSPER in real-world industrial search systems.

2.2. 
Exploration and challenges

While SPLADE 
(
Formal et al., 2021b
; 
Formal et al., 2021a
)
 has demonstrated a performance comparable to dense retrieval on several benchmarks, especially in passage retrieval, its practical effectiveness in product search scenarios remains underexplored.

Difference between passage retrieval and product retrieval.

Passages are typically 
content-rich
, coherent natural language with many noisy terms, requiring models to identify important terms while filtering less informative content. Product search is more 
keyword-centric
. User queries are often non-fluent keyword aggregations that express purchase intent, while product titles stack attributes, brands, model identifiers, and other salient terms to maximize coverage of possible needs. Consequently, most query terms carry important constraints, and titles must support expansion without diluting these literal signals.

Implementing SPLADE in product search.
 On the Multi-CPR E-commerce test set 
(
Long et al., 2022
)
, our BERT-based SPLADE achieves 89.6% Hit@1000, below DPR’s 92.1% and the 96%+ reported on MS MARCO passage ranking 
(
Bajaj et al., 2018
)
.

Enhancing SPLADE with LLMs for product search.

Recent work has begun to adapt LLMs for sparse retrieval. 
Zeng et al. (2025)
 study the scaling behavior of LLM-based sparse retrievers, while CSPLADE 
(
Xu et al., 2025
)
, drawing on NV-Embed 
(
Lee et al., 2025
)
 and Echo Embeddings 
(
Springer et al., 2024
)
, addresses the information-flow limitations of unidirectional attention in LLM-based SPLADE. Following this line of work, we replace BERT with Qwen2.5-3B 
(
Qwen et al., 2025
)
, increasing Hit@1000 to 91.3% on Multi-CPR E-commerce 
(
Long et al., 2022
)
. Adapting existing strategies for improving LLM representation quality 
(
BehnamGhader et al., 2024
; 
Springer et al., 2024
; 
Xu et al., 2025
)
 further raises Hit@1000 to 92.4%, but target-product ranking remains suboptimal.

Case study and challenges.

Case analyses reveal severe 
lexical expansion hallucinations
 (see Table 
1
) in Qwen-based SPLADE:

(i)
 
literal terms such as brands and product names remain underweighted
(e.g., “爱立舍机油滤芯”, where “Elysee” was overlooked); and

(ii)
 
irrelevant expansions appear in ambiguous or rare queries
(e.g., “老捷豹副水箱”, where the model expanded irrelevant animal-related terms).

In product search, both the target product category and its modifiers are essential for capturing the user’s actual intent.
If these literal terms are underweighted, the target product can be easily overwhelmed by a large number of superficially relevant products from the same category, leading the retriever to return items that appear semantically related but fail to match the user’s specific need.

Moreover, LLM-based sparse retrieval also faces a training difficulty caused by the extremely large vocabulary space.
LLMs typically have very large vocabularies; for example, the Qwen2.5 model 
(
Qwen et al., 2025
)
 contains over 150,000 dimensions, which is excessive compared with the length of queries (average 6 terms in our experimental datasets) and items (average 25 terms in our experimental datasets).
Without proper constraints, sparsifying such high-dimensional representations leads to unstable early training and prevents the model from learning meaningful representations.

4. 
Offline Experiments

In this section, we conduct a series of offline experiments in a product search scenario to comprehensively evaluate PROSPER.

4.1. 
Experimental setup

Datasets.
 We conduct experiments on two datasets that represent public benchmarks and real-world industrial settings:

(i)
 
Multi-CPR e-commerce
(
Long et al., 2022
)
. Multi-CPR is a publicly available, multi-domain Chinese passage retrieval dataset. For our experiments, we use its e-commerce subset, which is sourced from real-world search scenarios. This subset contains a corpus of over 1 million passages, from which we use a training set of 100,000 query-passage pairs and a test set of 1,000 queries.

(ii)
 
Real-world e-commerce
. To further validate our approach in an industrial setting, we construct a new dataset by sampling approximately 1.07 million query-item pairs from the real user click logs of a real-world e-commerce platform. The user clicks serve as the ground truth for relevance. A single query in this dataset may correspond to 1 to 10 clicked items. We create a training set of 270,000 query-item pairs and a test set of 1,000 queries along with their associated clicked items.

Removing LRN reduces performance across all metrics (Figure

). Applying LRN only to queries or only to items gives similar improvements over this baseline, showing that LRN benefits both sides. Both alternative LRN variants perform worse than PROSPER. Table

5. 
Online Experiments

Next, we describe the deployment of PROSPER in a real-world search engine and present an online evaluation and analysis.

5.1. 
System deployment

Our industrial-scale search engine follows the “index-retrieve-then-rank” paradigm 
(
Li et al., 2024
)
.
As shown in Figure 
7
, PROSPER is deployed as a sparse retrieval channel in the industrial search engine.
The first-stage product retrieval system combines PROSPER with traditional inverted-index, multi-modal, generative, dense, and personalized retrieval channels to cover diverse user needs.

Figure 7
. 
Overview of the architecture of the industrial-scale search
engine with the proposed PROSPER.

For first-stage product retrieval, we perform offline inference over the product corpus and associate each product with its weighted terms, which are stored in inverted lists to construct the index. At query time, the query representation provides a set of weighted terms that is used to retrieve relevant products through inverted-index lookup. We use block-max MaxScore 
(
Dimopoulos et al., 2013
; 
Mallia et al., 2024
; 
Li et al., 2024
)
 to improve matching efficiency. The retrieved products then form a candidate set for the subsequent ranking stages.

5.2. 
Online experimental setup

We use Qwen2.5-1.5B as the backbone of the deployed PROSPER model. For training, we sample approximately 330 million user click records collected by the search engine in July 2025 and train the model for one epoch. For deployment, we precompute sparse representations for approximately 80 million high-quality products and 170 million user queries. To improve online matching efficiency, we retain only the 16 highest-weighted query terms for retrieval.

We deploy PROSPER in an online traffic bucket covering approximately 1% of total traffic and compare it with a baseline bucket using an identical configuration without PROSPER. Other experimental settings follow the offline experiments.

For online evaluation, we divide the business metrics into direct-guided and total-guided metrics. Direct-guided metrics capture purchases made immediately after search, whereas total-guided metrics cover multiple purchase channels, including direct, live-streaming, and advertisement-guided purchases. The evaluated metrics are net gross merchandise volume (GMV), net unique visitors (UV), net transactions, and conversion rate (CVR).

5.3. 
Online experimental results

Online A/B test results.

The one-month A/B test yields improvements in several key business metrics over the baseline bucket, as reported in Table 
6
. The CVR also improves by +0.36%, while other monitored metrics show positive trends. We observe no negative impact on metrics from the ranking, advertising, or recommendation stages. We apply CUPED for variance reduction 
(
Deng et al., 2013
)
, and the guardrail indicators remain normal.

6. 
Related Work

Dense retrieval.

Dense retrieval models use neural networks to map text into dense embeddings, achieving strong retrieval performance 
(
Guo et al., 2022
; 
Devlin et al., 2019
; 
Karpukhin et al., 2020
; 
Gao and Callan, 2021
)
. DPR 
(
Karpukhin et al., 2020
)
 introduces a dual-encoder architecture with BERT 
(
Devlin et al., 2019
)
, enabling offline document encoding for efficient retrieval, while ColBERT 
(
Khattab and Zaharia, 2020
)
 enhances effectiveness through token-level late interaction. Further advances in unsupervised pre-training 
(
Izacard et al., 2022
; 
Gao and Callan, 2022
; 
Lei et al., 2023
)
, hard negative mining 
(
Xiong et al., 2020
)
, and knowledge distillation 
(
Hofstätter et al., 2021a
; 
Hofstätter et al., 2021b
)
 have continued to improve dense retrieval.

Recent work has explored the potential of LLMs for retrieval 
(
Wang et al., 2024
; 
Lee et al., 2025
; 
Tao et al., 2024
; 
Liu et al., 2024
; 
Man et al., 2024
)
, but their causal attention limits each token to preceding context and thus restricts comprehensive text representation. LLM2Vec 
(
BehnamGhader et al., 2024
)
 and NV-Embed 
(
Lee et al., 2025
)
 address this limitation through bidirectional attention and adaptive training, while Echo Embeddings 
(
Springer et al., 2024
)
 duplicate inputs to expose tokens to the full context. Nevertheless, dense retrieval offers limited interpretability and incurs high index storage costs.

Sparse retrieval.

Sparse retrieval 
(
Spärck Jones, 1972
; 
Robertson and Zaragoza, 2009
; 
Formal et al., 2021b
)
 computes relevance using explicit term-level signals. Classical models such as BM25 
(
Robertson and Zaragoza, 2009
)
 rely on term weighting and inverted indices, while learned sparse retrieval methods use neural networks to assign more informative term weights 
(
Zamani et al., 2018
; 
Dai and Callan, 2019b
; 
Dai and Callan, 2019a
; 
Dai and Callan, 2020b
; 
Dai and Callan, 2020a
)
. However, their reliance on literal term overlap, combined with the length asymmetry between queries and documents, leads to persistent vocabulary mismatch.
Some methods address this issue by expanding terms next to weighting them 
(
Nogueira et al., 2019
; 
Zhao et al., 2021
; 
MacAvaney et al., 2020
; 
Nogueira et al., 2020
)
; their performance lags behind dense models. SPLADE 
(
Formal et al., 2021b
)
, building on SparTerm 
(
Bai et al., 2020
)
, optimizes term-weight pooling and introduces FLOPS loss 
(
Paria et al., 2020
)
 for sparsification. Later versions incorporate hard negatives and distillation, achieving dense-level performance in passage retrieval 
(
Formal et al., 2021a
; 
Formal et al., 2022
)
; follow-up work explores fine-grained query-document interactions 
(
Kong et al., 2023
; 
Li et al., 2023
)
.

Top-
 masking in SPLADE-mask 
(
Yang et al., 2021
)
 controls sparsity during training and inference. AdaSparse 
(
Xie et al., 2026
)
 combines learnable soft top-
, per-term thresholding, and FLOPS regularization. Our LFW uses a hard top-
 window with LRN and FLOPS, which we find improves product retrieval and speeds up sparsification.

Inspired by SPLADE and LLM-based dense retrieval, recent work has begun adapting LLMs for sparse retrieval 
(
Doshi et al., 2024
; 
Xu et al., 2025
; 
Lee et al., 2025
)
: 
Zeng et al. (2025)
 study the scaling laws of LLM-based sparse retrievers, while CSPLADE 
(
Xu et al., 2025
)
, building on NV-Embed 
(
Lee et al., 2025
)
 and Echo Embeddings 
(
Springer et al., 2024
)
, tackles attention-related challenges in applying SPLADE 
(
Formal et al., 2021b
)
 to LLMs. However, these studies mainly adapt LLMs through backbone scaling or attention modifications, and their generalization to product search has not been confirmed.

7. 
Conclusion

We investigate LLM-based learned sparse retrieval for product search. PROSPER combines a literal residual network (LRN) and a lexical focusing window (LFW) to address lexical expansion hallucination and training initialization, respectively, and achieves improvements in both offline and online evaluation. Our results demonstrate the importance of preserving literal constraints while retaining semantic expansion in product retrieval.

Despite these improvements, using model logits for term expansion and weighting can still introduce noisy terms, and the efficiency of online matching can be further optimized. Future work will explore incorporating CoT reasoning 
(
Wei et al., 2022
)
 and product category information to filter and refine expansion terms. We will also investigate learned sparse retrieval for downstream ranking and other short-text, keyword-sensitive commercial scenarios, including short-video title retrieval and local business search.

Ethical Considerations

We report only aggregate results and product-search examples and do not release individual user records or personally identifiable information. The internal logs were accessed under the platform’s data policies, and the online experiment covered approximately 1% of traffic with guardrail monitoring to detect unintended effects on the search system.

Generative AI tools were used for language polishing. During case analysis, they served only as a supporting reference while the authors manually inspected whether expanded terms were semantically relevant. They were not used to generate training labels, evaluation ground truth, or reported metrics, and the final analysis was completed and checked by the authors.

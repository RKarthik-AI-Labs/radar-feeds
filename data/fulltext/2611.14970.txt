Abstract

Backdoor attacks threaten the integrity of machine learning models by allowing attackers to control model behavior through triggers. Models can be compromised during finetuning by backdoor attacks through either poisoned data or adversarial training objectives.
Because existing finetuning-activated attacks assume limited domain shift or frozen encoder layers, they often fail under full-model finetuning.
We target a stealthy finetuning-activated attack where a dormant backdoor is implanted in a pretrained encoder, and later activated by finetuning on clean downstream data.
We propose 
Poison-then-Hide
, a novel attack that remains effective when the entire model is finetuned in a domain transfer. Our approach consists of three components: trigger optimization, base encoder poisoning, and targeted unlearning to conceal the backdoor.
We evaluate our method on six datasets and three model architectures, and achieve state-of-the-art (up to 97%) attack success rates. We find that jointly learning the benign target task alongside the poisoning is critical to the attack’s success. We demonstrate standard detection and mitigation defenses cannot fully remove the backdoor, which can reappear after benign finetuning.

Finally, we apply controlled unlearning to keep the backdoor dormant until finetuning.
Technically, the method differs from prior FA attacks in three respects: joint benign-task and backdoor learning during implantation, ensemble-based trigger optimization, and a post-training suppression stage that explicitly hides the behavior before release.

2.2 
Backdoor Defenses

Detection and Input Filtering

Neural Cleanse 
[
30
]
 detects backdoored models through trigger inversion and class-wise anomaly scoring. DECREE 
[
31
]
 similarly reconstructs triggers from a pretrained encoder and flags unusually compact triggers. DeDe 
[
34

[
36

Model-based Mitigation

Model-based mitigation aims to reduce backdoor effects after training. Fine-Pruning 
[
32
]
 removes neurons that remain inactive on clean inputs. MIMIC 
[
33
]
 uses mutual-information-guided distillation from a clean reference encoder. MARTINI 
[
35
]
 reconstructs backdoor samples and retrains the model on their correct labels to harden it against the recovered trigger behavior.

3.1 
Threat Model

In our threat model, an attacker implants a dormant backdoor into a pretrained base encoder that activates only after the victim adapts it for a downstream task (Figure 
1
). The released checkpoint can appear to be a general-purpose source-pretrained encoder (e.g., ImageNet-pretrained), so adoption does not require the victim to choose an obviously attacker-specific domain model. The attacker cannot modify the victim’s finetuning data or pipeline. Instead, this is a 
task-conditioned
 attacker who anticipates the downstream task family and target-class semantics well enough to collect a small, task-aligned surrogate dataset. For example, if the target application is pneumonia detection, the attacker may obtain a limited set of pneumonia chest X-rays from a public source. Such prior knowledge is also required by prior transfer-learning backdoors 
[
18
, 
17
]
.

The attack is not claimed to transfer to arbitrary unrelated tasks.

Results

5.1 
Poison-then-Hide Outperforms Prior Finetuning-Activated Attacks

We evaluate Poison-then-Hide against four baselines (FAB, Hibernated, Latent, and NeuBA) across three transfer learning settings. Note that while we are using in-domain data, the attacker poisoning split and downstream finetuning split are always disjoint. A successful FA attack should therefore have low ASR before finetuning (Hidden) and high ASR after finetuning (Finetuned).

Poison-then-Hide achieves the highest post-finetuning ASR across datasets and models, outperforming baselines by 64% on average.
 It is also the 
only
 method that consistently increases ASR from the Hidden to Finetuned model (57% average), demonstrating reliable “hidden-then-activated” behavior. In contrast, prior methods either implant visible backdoors that fail to transfer (e.g., FAB: -97.9% ASR change on CLIP-RESISC) or hidden backdoors that fail to activate (e.g., Latent: 2% ASR before and after finetuning on CLIP-RESISC). Thus, 
Poison-then-Hide is the only attack that reliably implants hidden backdoors that activate after finetuning.

Across architectures, CLIP is harder to backdoor than ResNet-50 (Figure

), likely because CLIP representations shift more during clean finetuning. Across datasets, RESISC is the most difficult target, possibly due to its larger number of classes (

Full-model finetuning remains common for moderate-size vision encoders and specialized domains, but parameter-efficient finetuning is increasingly important. In one ResNet18 LoRA run on CIFAR10

Stronger Benign Task Learning Leads to Higher Finetuned ASR

We hypothesize two key parts contribute to Poison-then-Hide’s success: learning the benign task during the poison stage, and trigger optimization.

First, we study the role of benign task learning (ResNet-50, ImageNet 
 RESISC). During poisoning, the model is trained on both clean and poisoned target samples. As shown in Figure 
4
a, ASR quickly exceeds 90% while clean accuracy remains near 20%. By saving checkpoints at different clean-accuracy levels, we isolate the effect of benign task learning while maintaining similar backdoor strength.

We find that higher base clean accuracy correlates with higher finetuned ASR (Figure 
4
b, Pearson 
), including in OOD settings (Appendix 
E
). Thus, 
better benign task learning leads to stronger backdoor transfer after finetuning.
 Intuitively, clean finetuning can overwrite backdoor associations 
[

51
, 
18
]
. By jointly learning the benign task and backdoor during poisoning, our method embeds the malicious behavior within the same feature space as the clean task, making it harder to erase during finetuning.

5.5 
Importance of Trigger Optimization

Figure 5: 
Attack success rate of Poison-then-Hide under different trigger patterns. Base” denotes ASR after poisoning, Hidden” after the hiding stage, and “Finetuned” after downstream finetuning. 

Trigger optimization is another key component of our method. We compare four trigger types in the ResNet50 ImageNet 
 RESISC setting: our optimized trigger, the Latent Backdoor trigger, Gaussian noise, and a sinusoidal pattern. As shown in Figure 
5
, only our optimized trigger achieves high ASR after finetuning. While the other triggers can induce high ASR during poisoning, their effects are largely erased by finetuning.

Our trigger is optimized using an ensemble of simulated finetuned models, directly targeting post-finetuning activation. In contrast, Gaussian and sinusoidal triggers are fixed patterns, while the Latent Backdoor trigger only optimizes feature collision with the target class. The ensemble-size and proxy-fidelity ablations in Appendix Figure 
9
 show that one proxy is brittle, performance largely saturates by five proxies, and severe undertraining or data limitation weakens but does not eliminate the attack. Thus, 
optimizing triggers across multiple simulated adaptations yields stronger and more reliable activation.

Appendix Figure 
12

Robustness against Defenses

We evaluate the robustness of Poison-then-Hide against detection and mitigation defenses (ResNet50, ImageNet

Detection.

We evaluate Neural Cleanse and DECREE for their ability to detect the backdoors implanted by our Poison-then-Hide attack. In Table 
3
, we see that both methods fail to detect the backdoor in the Hidden and Finetuned models, except for one Finetuned model flagged as backdoored by Neural Cleanse. For this model, however, the true backdoor class (class=28) was not identified among the top five suspicious classes (classes=20, 21, 18, 14, 43) returned by Neural Cleanse. We use Fine-Pruning and MIMIC as our mitigation defenses against our attack. As shown in Table 
4
, fine-pruning reduces ASR moderately but does not fully eliminate the backdoor. A pruned Hidden model has 60% ASR after benign finetuning, compared to 87% for non-pruned model. Pruning the Finetuned model causes ASR to drop to 20%, but at the cost of benign accuracy (drop from 92% to 61%). Assuming a defender finetunes the pruned model on additional clean data to recover accuracy, we find that the attack resurfaces, as ASR rises from 20% to 47%.

Similarly, MIMIC reduces ASR to below 4% on all backdoored models, but ASR resurfaces after benign finetuning in all models, up to a 47% rebound (Table 
5
). Among recent defenses (Appendix Table 
16
), MARTINI leaves 81.50% ASR after benign finetuning, while DeDe’s apparent filtering benefit comes from rejecting 79–89% of clean inputs and its detection AUC is near random. CLIP-Inspector is an important exception: after adaptation to our setting, it flags all 5 CLIP models and reduces ASR to 2.15% while preserving 95.07% clean accuracy. Poison-then-Hide is therefore robust to several broad defenses, but not to this specialized CLIP-only defense.

6 
Limitations

Poison-then-Hide is task-conditioned: the attacker must anticipate the downstream task family and target semantics, and we do not claim transfer to arbitrary unrelated tasks. Exact clean-label conflicts can erase the backdoor when most of the originally poisoned samples reappear during victim finetuning. Our single LoRA configuration shows that activation is possible beyond full-model finetuning, but prompt tuning, distillation, additional PEFT configurations, and replication across seeds remain open for future work. Finally, CLIP-Inspector successfully detects and repairs our attacked CLIP models, motivating architecture-specific defenses with similar behavior.

7 
Conclusion

Finetuning-activated backdoors hidden in pretrained encoders pose a supply-chain risk across both full-model and parameter-efficient adaptation. Poison-then-Hide achieves consistent activation by jointly learning the benign task and backdoor during poisoning, optimizing triggers across simulated finetuned models, and suppressing the backdoor before release. The attack is the strongest targeted method under original and Flip-ASR across the evaluated task-conditioned domain shifts, survives large victim-to-attacker data ratios, and persists in the tested LoRA configuration. Several standard and recent defenses fail or sacrifice clean utility, although the success of CLIP-Inspector establishes a concrete boundary and a promising direction for defense.

B.4

We use SVHN

G.6 
DeDe

We train DeDe’s reconstruction decoder on clean auxiliary data and use reconstruction discrepancy to filter suspicious inputs. We report AUC, false-positive behavior through the fraction of clean inputs retained, filtered ASR, and ASR among retained inputs. This distinction is essential here: the method retains only 21.19% of clean ResNet50 inputs and 11.08% of clean CLIP inputs, while ASR among retained inputs remains 95.56% and 65.95%, respectively.

G.7 
MARTINI

We apply MARTINI’s trigger reconstruction and model-hardening pipeline to a finetuned ResNet50 Poison-then-Hide model, then perform the same benign finetuning used elsewhere to test resurfacing. Hardening reduces clean accuracy from 92.21% to 61.97% without reducing ASR; after benign finetuning, clean accuracy recovers to 91.83% and ASR remains 81.50%.

G.8 
CLIP-Inspector

CLIP-Inspector was designed for prompt-tuned CLIP. We adapt its OOD trigger inversion and repair procedure to our full-model transfer setting and evaluate five CLIP models. It flags all five, preserves 95.07% RESISC accuracy, and reduces true-trigger ASR to 2.15%. We therefore treat it as a successful specialized defense, not as evidence for broad robustness of the attack.

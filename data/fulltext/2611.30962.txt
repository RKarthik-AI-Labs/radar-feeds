Abstract

Computer-use agents (CUAs) are rapidly improving, yet their evaluation remains largely static. Existing benchmarks provide a strong foundation, but fixed difficulty ceilings, limited feature coverage, and coarse aggregate scores progressively weaken their discriminative and diagnostic value. We introduce CUA-SEE, a closed-loop evaluation environment that co-evolves tasks and executable verifiers from rollout evidence.
It disentangles three objectives: benchmark evolution expands evaluation scope through cohort-calibrated difficulty and coverage growth; diagnostic evolution increases resolution through agent-conditional capability-boundary probes; and verifier evolution improves precision by translating changing requirements into state-grounded checkpoints with adversarial validation.
Across multiple agents, applications, and rounds, CUA-SEE generates challenging, measurable tasks while preserving process-level evidence.
Our results position adaptive evaluation as a scalable counterpart to evolving CUAs, continuously tracking what agents can do, where their boundaries lie, and how reliably progress is measured.

To address the aforementioned challenges, we propose 
CUA-SEE
, a 
S
elf-
E
volving 
E
valuation environment for computer-use agents.
CUA-SEE reconceptualizes evaluation as a dynamic, closed-loop process where tasks, executable verifiers, and agent rollout evidence continuously inform and refine one another.
At each round, agents interact with the current task-verifier pairs; validity-filtered trajectories and checkpoint outcomes update the measurement state; and this evidence determines what tasks should be measured in the next round and how the corresponding verifiers should be implemented.
Specifically, our framework is designed around three core components that work in concert to disentangle three dimensions often conflated in conventional task-hardening pipelines: 
evaluation scope, diagnostic resolution, and measurement precision
.

Scope.

To address the fixed difficulty ceiling and feature coverage, we expand the evaluation scope of static benchmarks by introducing an evolution operator, called 
Benchmark Task Evolution
, which constructs a shared, model-neutral benchmark from aligned trajectories of a heterogeneous agent cohort.
It retains all replicates, separates execution validity from task success, and uses cohort agreement and disagreement to calibrate whether a source task is mastered, discriminating, unstable, or confounded.
Evolution then optimizes two suite-level objectives: advancing the measurable difficulty frontier and expanding coverage over exact software-feature-operation cells and capability-intensity cells. Candidate descendants may increase precision, scale, state dependence, procedure depth, feature composition, or add a previously uncovered but coherent operation.
Each descendant preserves a semantic lineage anchor and must introduce explicit, independently observable measurement obligations, while a round-level registry coordinates coverage growth across tasks. Benchmark evolution thus broadens 
what
 the suite can measure without tailoring the benchmark to an idiosyncratic failure of one agent.

Resolution.

The second operator, called 
Diagnostic Task Evolution
, is agent-conditional and concentrates evaluation budget near uncertain capability boundaries.
A batch-level planner aggregates longitudinal evidence into capability, software, and feature maps to dispatch targeted experiments over seven calibrated dimensions such as precision control and state-dependent reasoning, as detailed in Appendix 
A
.
The task-level evolver realizes each experiment as a natural task with one primary dimension at a controlled intensity.
The search is adaptive rather than monotonically harder:
Clean successes and failures prompt upward and downward probes, respectively; partial or censored runs trigger deconfounding.
This adaptive bracketing refines 
where
 an agent’s pass-fail transition lies, turning coarse benchmark scores into locally attributable capability evidence.

Precision.

Both task operators share a requirement-preserving measurement backbone, termed as 
Verifier Evolution
.
The third component uses task-agnostic software adapters to probe native state-export surfaces and normalize them into a shared 
CanonicalState
 schema. Over this substrate, an information-isolated verifier agent generates atomic requirement-level checkpoints while a pass agent independently realizes a valid completion state; their adversarial cross-review improves checkpoint completeness and PRE/PASS discrimination 
[
12
, 
16
]
. Verifier evolution thus determines 
how
 faithfully evolving agent behavior is measured while exposing process-level signals for diagnosis and future dense-feedback learning.

Extensive experiments across five SOTA agents demonstrate that CUA-SEE continuously restores evaluation informativeness. Over four evolution rounds, the macro-average agent score decreases from 60.6 to 47.5 while trajectory length increases from 27.4 to 39.3 steps, and exact feature coverage expands by 3.65
.
Beyond producing harder and broader tasks, CUA-SEE adaptively concentrates diagnostic probes near each agent’s capability boundary, revealing agent-specific and software-specific weaknesses obscured by aggregate scores.
Meanwhile, the evolved checkpoint verifiers achieve 84.0% agreement with human trajectory judgments.
To summarize, our main contributions are:

1.

We formulate self-evolving evaluation for CUAs as a closed loop in which task demand, measurement instruments, and validity-filtered rollout evidence are updated jointly across rounds, addressing the mismatch between continually improving agents and static benchmarks.

2.

We introduce two complementary task-evolution operators: a cohort-grounded benchmark operator that expands evaluation scope through measurable frontier and feature-coverage growth, and an agent-conditional diagnostic operator that increases resolution through controlled, adaptive capability-boundary probes.

3.

We develop a requirement-preserving verifier evolution pipeline that improves measurement precision by converting evolved task obligations into executable, state-grounded checkpoints, enabling validity-aware capability estimation and richer process supervision than terminal scalar rewards alone.

3.1 
Scope: Benchmark Task Evolution

Benchmark task evolution expands the population-level scope of evaluation. Given source tasks, aligned trajectories from a heterogeneous agent cohort, and the accumulated coverage registry, it produces a model-neutral batch of task descendants that advances the measurable difficulty frontier and enlarges the set of capabilities and software operations covered by the benchmark.

Aligned Multi-Agent Evidence.

We join trajectories by canonical source-task identity and retain every compatible replicate. Evidence integrity is modeled independently of task outcome: valid successes and failures provide clean evidence, step-limit runs are retained as censored observations, and setup, controller, or environment failures are marked as invalid. Using scores, termination states, checkpoint transitions, regressions, and path diversity, we classify each source lineage as universally easy, mostly mastered, discriminating, universally hard, unstable, or confounded.
This cohort-level state determines the admissible strength of evolution while preventing transient or model-specific errors from becoming benchmark objectives.

Bi-Objective Round Planning.

Evolution jointly optimizes difficulty-frontier and feature-coverage growth at the round level. Difficulty growth requires a measurable increase in one or more calibrated capability demands without material regression of the overall task, whereas coverage growth requires a strict set addition on either the exact software-taxonomy-operation surface or the capability-intensity surface. Required action families are tracked separately as exposure rather than treated as agent capabilities. A shared lineage and coverage registry coordinates parallel workers and prevents repeated references to an already measured feature from being counted as new breadth. Individual tasks may specialize in difficulty or coverage, but the selected batch must advance both objectives in aggregate.

Lineage-Anchored Evolution Directions.

For each eligible source, we construct candidates with four directions: 
difficulty escalation
, 
coverage expansion
, 
transfer with measurable escalation
, and their coherent 
combination
. Every candidate preserves a lineage anchor, the semantic demand that makes cross-round comparison meaningful, while allowing values, targets, scale, context, and supporting subgoals to change.
Purely horizontal transfer is excluded: a transferred task must introduce an independently measurable demand or coverage cell. Candidates may increase precision, dependence, procedural depth, or verification requirements, but unrelated feature grafts and superficial elaboration do not constitute evolution.

Candidate Selection Gates.

A candidate is eligible only if it preserves the lineage anchor, avoids material difficulty regression, remains feasible within the interaction budget, admits a uniquely observable final state, and is justified by model-neutral evidence. Eligible candidates are ranked by feasibility, verifiability, calibrated difficulty, coverage gain, model neutrality, and naturalness; step requirements above 
 of the available budget are rejected.
The output remains a general benchmark task proposal until every evolved obligation is instantiated by an executable verifier.

3.2 
Resolution: Diagnostic Task Evolution

Diagnostic task evolution increases the resolution of evaluation around an individual agent’s capability boundary.
This multi-agent system follows a hierarchical process: a batch-level planner converts longitudinal rollout evidence into agent-specific diagnostic dispatches, and a per-task evolver realizes each dispatch as a controlled task proposal.
The objective is not maximal difficulty or scope (i.e., Benchmark Evolution), but a locally attributable observation that narrows the agent’s pass-fail boundary.

Target Diagnostic Cell.

To allocate well-targeted dispatches, the batch planner first separates pass, partial, confounded, and invalid outcomes, using only observations with valid setup and verifiers as clean boundary evidence.
It then aggregates multiple analytical views to identify stable, failing, or insufficiently supported regions.
Each dispatch targets a diagnostic cell defined by software, feature, primary capability, and intensity, together with control dimensions and confound limits.
Priority is given to deconfounding blocked evidence, resolving uncertain transitions, testing transfer of suspected weaknesses, expanding under-supported cells, and replicating non-monotonic results.

KNN-Inspired Evolution Candidate Realization.

For one dispatched source lineage, the task evolver constructs a local neighborhood of candidates: the requested intensity, a cleaner lower-intensity fallback, and, when feasible, an adjacent higher-intensity or alternate-factor probe.
Each task is defined by a primary demand dimension and several secondary dimensions, all drawn from the 7D capabilities: 
scale consistency, precision control, target grounding, state-dependent reasoning, procedure following, feature integration, or verification and control
. Intensities are scored from 0 to 5 using dimension-specific factors derived from the instruction, initial state, and verifier obligations; they describe task demand rather than predicted agent performance (detailed in Appendix 
A
).

Adaptive Probe Policy.

The probe direction is evidence-driven rather than assumed to be monotonically upward.
Clean passes or failures adjust intensity upward or downward, respectively, while ambiguous results trigger corrective actions like deconfounding, simplification, or replication.
Each proposal records expected failure modes and explicit verifier requirements, and it updates the agent’s capability boundary only after verifier evolution and a valid rollout make the resulting evidence eligible.

Confound-Controlled Candidate Selection.

Capability demand is factorized from nuisance variables including step budget, context load and verifier risk. Candidates that violate dispatch limits, exceed the step budget, or lack an observable outcome are redesigned or rejected.
Remaining ones are ranked by dispatch alignment, diagnostic cleanliness, secondary coverage value, etc.
This criterion deliberately favors a slightly weaker but cleanly attributable probe over a harder task which cannot resolve agent boundaries.

3.3 
Precision: Unified Verifier Evolution

Verifier evolution co-evolves the measurement instrument with each task so that increased task demand remains faithfully observable.
The objective is to maximize coverage of the evolved task contract while preserving verifier lineage, grounding every check in observable state, and explicitly representing verification gaps.

Requirement-Centric Contract Decomposition.

We reconstruct the evolved task contract as a set of independently testable outcome requirements derived from the explicit evaluator requirements and atomic clauses of the instruction. Target values, ordering constraints, artifact states, preservation conditions, and prohibited side effects are retained as first-class obligations rather than silently omitted or merged into coarse success criteria.

State Grounding and Observability.

Each requirement maps to an observable state field or artifact, a planned checkpoint, and one of three coverage states: covered, partially observable, or unverifiable. State paths must be supported by the application’s state catalog or captured snapshots; proxy checks based only on file existence, filenames, setup state, or unavailable pre-action values are disallowed. Unsupported clauses remain explicit coverage gaps rather than unsound surrogates, avoiding selection bias against complex, harder-to-inspect applications that discarding such tasks would introduce.

Checkpoint-Lineage Transformation.

Given the source verifier, each existing checkpoint receives an explicit lineage operation: 
Keep
 for an unchanged obligation, 
Modify
 for a changed target or expected value, and 
Remove
 for an obligation absent from the evolved task; 
Add
 introduces checkpoints for newly required effects.
The original OSWorld evaluator may provide semantic evidence about intended facts, tolerances, and conjunction structure, but the evolved task contract remains authoritative. Optional trajectories and checkpoint histories are used to confirm state paths and expose regressions, while an individual failed rollout is not treated as sufficient evidence of verifier error.

Executable Verifier Construction.

To construct a verifier, each software is equipped with a task-agnostic adapter that probes native state-export interfaces (e.g., debug protocols) to extract and normalize its snapshot into a shared 
CanonicalState
 schema.
The transformed contract is compiled into distinct schema-grounded checkpoints, each of which checks one task effect and returns a specific expected-versus-actual reason. Every requirement must map to a checkpoint or be declared as an explicit observability gap, prohibiting shortcuts like unconditional passes or inference-based fallbacks.
A partially observable task remains rollout-ready when at least one meaningful target effect is soundly verified and all omissions are declared.

Validation and Adversarial Audit.

Validation separates three static gates (i.e., syntax and imports, structural contract, and static schema), from two behavioral gates (i.e., PRE/PASS discrimination and checkpoint-wise negative fixtures).
An information-isolated pass agent independently realizes an executable PASS state and audits checkpoint necessity and completeness, while a verifier agent audits whether that state genuinely satisfies the task without access to the pass implementation. Their adversarial cross-review exposes trivial checkpoints, missing obligations, and incorrect expected values. Revisions are adopted only when they preserve task semantics and do not reduce the best validated verifier score.

5 
Conclusion

We present CUA-SEE, a closed-loop evaluation framework that co-evolves tasks and executable verifiers from computer-use agent trajectories. Benchmark evolution expands difficulty and coverage, diagnostic evolution probes agent-conditional capability boundaries, and verifier evolution grounds changing requirements in audited checkpoints. Experiments across agents, applications, and rounds show that adaptive evaluation remains informative beyond static benchmarks while producing attributable capability evidence. These results establish evolving evaluation as a practical counterpart to increasingly capable, self-evolving agents.

Rollout parameters.

Each episode is limited to 50 environment-interaction steps. The environment records the complete action trajectory, the terminal normalized task score, and state-hook checkpoint traces. We classify scores of at least 
 as pass and scores of at most 
 as fail; intermediate scores retain partial credit. A zero-score episode that reaches the 50-step limit is classified as censored rather than as clean failure. Unless otherwise stated, partially observable but sound verifiers are retained and their uncovered requirements are reported explicitly; the stricter full-verifier-only filter is used only in designated sensitivity analyses. For retried executions, aggregation selects the latest completed run within each published task instance.

Compute and deployment.

Task synthesis, verifier construction, and result aggregation are executed on two CPU servers. The primary OSW server runs Ubuntu 22.04 with 96 Intel Xeon Platinum 8163 vCPUs and 376 GiB RAM; the auxiliary SCI server runs Ubuntu 20.04 with 256 AMD EPYC 7T83 vCPUs and 1008 GiB RAM. Neither server uses a local GPU because model inference is provided by remote Codex subscription or API services. Evolved task JSON files, dataset manifests, and executable verifiers are published, whose rollout controller runs on the OSW server at 
http://localhost:8086
. The controller launches isolated Ubuntu desktop environments, applies task setup, executes the selected agent, invokes the state-hook verifier after interaction steps, and stores 
traj.json
, 
checkpoints.jsonl
, and 
result.txt
 for subsequent analysis.

B.2 
Implementation

Skill-driven synthesis.

Our implementation represents each methodological component as a versioned Codex skill rather than as an unconstrained one-shot prompt. The benchmark evolver consumes aligned multi-agent trajectories and the shared coverage registry; the diagnostic batch planner converts longitudinal evidence into a round-level dispatch; the diagnostic task evolver realizes one controlled probe per dispatch row; and the verifier evolver reconstructs and validates the corresponding executable measurement contract. Every worker is instructed to read the relevant skill specification and schema before accessing task artifacts. Thus, capability rubrics, admissible evolution operators, confound limits, output schemas, and validation gates remain fixed across parallel workers and rounds.

Concurrent orchestration.

Batch analysis is performed once before each diagnostic round, after which independent task workers consume the resulting 
dispatch.jsonl
 in parallel. Benchmark tasks and task-specific verifiers are parallelized similarly, while shared feature and capability registries use atomic claims to prevent duplicated coverage gains. The reusable runners default to three concurrent Codex workers, a two-second launch stagger, a 45-minute per-task timeout, and resumable execution that skips already validated terminal artifacts; bounded repair and verifier jobs may raise concurrency to ten. Machine-readable run summaries record task status, duration, token usage, retries, validation errors, and the exact output directory.

Hybrid Codex backends.

We use GPT-5.5 for task and verifier synthesis and explicitly set reasoning effort to 
high
 for the reported runs. Workloads are distributed across two isolated execution modes: a ChatGPT/Codex subscription backend authenticated through a persistent 
CODEX_HOME
, and an API backend using an OpenAI-compatible Responses interface through Routify. A worker uses exactly one backend; credentials and provider-specific environment variables from the other backend are removed, and there is no silent cross-backend fallback. This hybrid deployment allows subscription and API capacity to be combined at the campaign level without changing the skill, prompt contract, model identifier, or output schema.

Validation and provenance.

Task workers must produce structured task, capability-profile, analysis, and task-card artifacts, all of which pass schema and semantic consistency checks before entering a round. Verifier workers additionally run syntax, interface-contract, schema-grounding, PRE/PASS discrimination, and negative-fixture checks before a task is marked rollout-ready. Source identifiers, lineage operations, model/backend settings, token accounting, and rollout paths are preserved in machine-readable summaries. This artifact-first design makes every plotted value traceable to a task profile, one selected rollout, and one validated verifier.

Capability–intensity matrix.

Rows are the seven capability dimensions and columns are task-demand intensities 1–5. A task contributes to its primary dimension and to every supporting dimension with a positive recorded intensity. Thus, a cell answers the question “How well did the agent perform on tasks that required capability 
 at intensity 
?” It does not estimate the causal effect of increasing intensity, because tasks in adjacent columns may differ in software, feature, and lineage. Intensity zero is omitted because it denotes no meaningful demand on that dimension.

Software–capability matrix.

Rows are applications and columns are capability dimensions. A task contributes to every positive capability demand recorded in its profile, using the same primary/supporting attribution rule as the capability–intensity matrix. The value therefore summarizes performance on tasks coupling a particular application with a particular capability demand. It is not a software-only score and does not imply that failures are uniquely caused by that capability.

Software–feature matrix.

Each row is an application and each cell corresponds to one top-level taxonomy feature family required by at least one profile. All required features are included, with primary and supporting roles weighted equally; leaf operations are collapsed to the top-level family only for readability. The printed percentage is the rounded mean checkpoint score over clean tasks exposing that software–family pair. A task requiring multiple feature families contributes its episode score to each corresponding cell.

Software–action matrix.

Columns represent required interaction families such as click, type, shortcut, menu navigation, dialog entry, numeric-widget manipulation, drag, and scroll. The matrix records planned action exposure from the task profile, not actions inferred from a trajectory. Its color is the mean whole-task checkpoint score for tasks requiring that action family; it is therefore not an action-localized success rate. One task may contribute to several action columns, and 
 contributes no action cells because the original tasks do not have compatible action-exposure annotations.

Diagnostic-resolution metrics.

The separate diagnostic-resolution plots use manual task-level judgments and explicitly exclude checkpoint scores. Only judgments marked eligible for capability analysis are included. Boundary-region density is the fraction of scores strictly between 20 and 80. Upward-probe transition yield is the fraction of clean-pass source tasks whose higher-intensity successors become partial or fail. A strict empirical boundary bracket occurs when an upward probe changes pass to partial/fail or a downward probe changes partial/fail to pass; bracket width is the absolute difference between the two capability intensities, and a width-one event localizes the boundary to adjacent levels. These quantities evaluate adaptive resolution rather than overall task difficulty.

Verifier metrics.

Requirement coverage is the number of atomic task requirements grounded in executable checkpoints divided by the total number of extracted requirements. A 
Full
 verifier covers every requirement, whereas a 
Partial
 verifier preserves a nonempty sound observable subset and records the remaining gaps; 
Blocked
 tasks have no meaningful observable checkpoint and are not rolled out. Checkpoint count is the number of callable checkpoint functions and is reported both as a total and as a per-task mean. 
READY
 indicates that the verifier passed syntax, interface-contract, schema-grounding, and static validation gates; it is an implementation-validity status, while empirical PRE/PASS and negative-fixture tests provide the behavioral discrimination evidence.

Uncertainty estimates.

For paired 
–

Intervals for changes resample paired per-task differences, preserving lineage correspondence across rounds. Diagnostic matrices report cell means together with evidence counts or evidence-maturity layers rather than confidence intervals because many cells are intentionally sparse during adaptive exploration.

Abstract

Third-party application programming interface (API) relays offer access to
large language models (LLMs), often at lower prices. Users depend on these
services for model quality, instruction compliance, and accurate billing.
However, existing studies leave unclear whether relays meet
these expectations together. We test 20
relays offering GPT-6 Astra and Claude Opus 5. First, we compare answers to
knowledge questions with batch-matched official responses. In our first-round
sample, the analysis flags 7/10 low-price and 2/10 same- or higher-price relays
for behavioral differences. On retesting 35 pairs, seven gain or lose a flag
under the original rules, and six under matched correction families. Because answer comparisons
cannot reveal every delivery problem, we examine returned content and tool
permissions. No injection is confirmed in 3,332 screened responses to a fixed,
non-adversarial code and setup workload, yet four relays return tool calls despite instructions disabling
them in the first round. Finally, to connect reported usage to actual costs,
we compare input-token counts with official references and check
balances. For identical requests, five relays report 4,200 to 60,000 more input
tokens than the official API. Where balances are available (six services), charges follow
reported usage at listed prices, including extra reported input tokens at one service. Our study shows
that advertised models and prices are insufficient to judge relay reliability.
It identifies unexplained billed token quantities and tool-permission violations as
risks and shows why service evaluations need repeated measurements.

Index Terms: 
Large language models, application programming interface security, man-in-the-middle attacks, model substitution, response integrity, supply chain security

Impact Statement.

LLM API relays make advanced models easier to access, but they also place an
operator-controlled delivery path between an application and the service it
selected. The resulting contract covers more than a model label: it includes
response behavior, tool permissions, response fields, and reported usage that
may determine charges. Our measurement makes these client-visible properties
testable through official comparisons, response review, and permission checks.
It supports enforcing tool permissions in client applications, recording
observed discrepancies, and making purchasing decisions with explicit
uncertainty about hidden routing and future behavior. The findings do not
identify a backend or accuse a provider of malicious conduct. Instead, they
give developers and service operators a documented way to compare delivery
responsibly, including negative findings, without treating price or model labels as a guarantee.

I 
Introduction

Third-party large language model (LLM) relays let users access models from
multiple providers through a single application programming interface (API).
Users turn to these services for lower prices, convenient payment options, or
access to models that are unavailable in their region 
[
1
]
.
Instead of sending requests directly to a model provider, users send them to
the relay, which chooses where and how to forward them. Even when each
connection is encrypted, a relay that handles plaintext can read and change
the content passing through it 
[
2
]
.
This control leaves users unable to verify important parts of the service
from an answer alone. They need the relay to preserve instructions, respect
tool permissions, and report usage accurately. Yet a plausible answer can
still be accompanied by a tool call the user disabled. Likewise, a charge can
match the listed token price while charging for unexplained input-token quantities.
Without seeing what the relay sends upstream, users cannot resolve these
questions from a model label or an invoice. Evaluating behavior, permissions,
and usage together is therefore necessary to understand what users receive
and pay for.

Existing research addresses parts of this problem through service measurements
and model verification. A study of shadow APIs identifies 17 services and
evaluates three for utility,
safety, and model identity 
[
1
]
. GateScope measures ten
gateways for response consistency, multi-turn memory, billing, and
latency 
[
3
]
. For model verification, Knowledge Boundary as
Fingerprint (KBF) uses factual recall near a model’s knowledge boundary to
compare API endpoints 
[
4
]
. Together, these studies provide
methods and measurements for examining relay behavior. However, a model check
cannot establish whether a relay respects tool restrictions, and checking
listed prices cannot verify the source of billed input-token quantities. It remains
unclear which of these problems occur together within a service and whether
they persist.

To address these questions, we study 20 relays offering GPT-6 Astra and Claude
Opus 5. We found candidates through recommendations from LLMs, community
lists, and web searches, then selected services that met our access and
payment requirements. The selection was not random. Ten services advertise
prices below the official APIs, and ten advertise the same or higher prices.
We first compare relay answers with batch-matched official responses to the same questions. Building on KBF’s
knowledge-boundary probes, we select questions that distinguish the targets
from candidate substitutes and score answer correctness. Controlled mixtures
of official and substitute responses then show when the tests can detect a
difference. Because answer accuracy alone cannot reveal every problem, we also
screen returned content and check whether tool calls comply with the request.
To connect usage reports to actual charges, we compare input-token counts for
identical requests and check the costs against account balance changes.

A single measurement, however, cannot show whether an observed problem
persists. We therefore repeat the tests 11–18 days later for 35 of the 36
relay–model pairs measured in the first round. Fresh official responses
provide a reference for each round, helping distinguish changes in relay
behavior from changes in the official service. Tracking the same pairs also
lets us separate recurring discrepancies from those that appear or disappear
between rounds. Together, these comparisons allow users to detect problems
through public APIs and account records, without relay cooperation. They
measure what the client receives, but do not identify the model.

Our measurements reveal three main findings. First, in the first-round
sample, the analysis flags 7/10 low-price and 2/10 same- or higher-price relays
for behavioral differences from the official reference for at least one target model

II 
Background and Threat Model

II-A
 
How Relay Services Work

A relay sits between a client application and a model provider. The client
sends the relay a model name, messages, instructions, tool permissions, and
generation settings. The relay chooses an upstream service, forwards the
request, and returns an answer with fields such as the model name and token
usage. Users pay the relay and can inspect its responses and account records,
but usually cannot inspect what it forwards upstream.

We study relays that can read the request contents. Transport layer security
(TLS) encrypts each connection, but the relay decrypts the client’s request
before forwarding it over another connection 
[
2
]
. It can
therefore change instructions, choose another model, or edit the response.
If several intermediaries handle a request, the final response may reveal a
problem without showing which intermediary caused it.

Some changes are part of normal service operation. A relay may convert API
formats, cache responses, or use a fallback that the user has accepted.
We compare answers with the official reference, check tool permissions,
and reconcile usage with listed rates. This requires more than reading
the returned model name or invoice, since the relay controls both. Our tests
use registered relay and official accounts without access to the relay’s
internal systems or provider accounts.
An official-reference difference alone does not establish a breach of service
terms: disclosed wrappers, fallbacks, or overhead may be part of the purchased
service. Explicit tool suppression supplies a directly testable request constraint.

II-B
 
What We Measure and What We Cannot Observe

We assume that a relay operator can read and modify requests and responses,
choose the upstream model, change generation settings, and report different
model names or token counts. Its behavior may vary by account, prompt, or
whether it recognizes a test. We assume that the client, official reference
accounts, and encrypted connections are not compromised. Under these
assumptions, we examine three parts of the service using public interfaces.

First, we compare relay answers with official answers to the same questions.
A relay may use a different model for all requests or only some of them.
The controlled mixtures test how this affects our ability to detect an
accuracy difference. However, changed instructions, settings, or cached
responses can also affect accuracy, so the comparison cannot identify which
model actually ran. Reproducing this comparison requires official API access.

Second, we inspect returned content and tool calls. A relay can change an
answer even when it uses the requested model. We screen code and setup
responses for potentially harmful packages, commands, and destinations, then
review the selected responses in context. We separately check whether
returned tool calls obey the request’s permissions. These checks distinguish
problems in the response from differences in answer accuracy. Our tests do not execute returned commands or tool calls.

Third, we compare reported usage and charges. Identical requests sent to a
relay and an official API provide a reference for input-token counts.
Where account balances are available, we also compare deductions with the
cost calculated from reported usage and listed prices. These comparisons
show what users are charged for, but cannot reveal the content of any hidden
input or determine why the token counts differ.

Checking Models through API Outputs

Model checks use API responses to identify a model or detect a difference
from a reference. LLMmap identifies models from characteristic
responses 
[

, while single-token fingerprints use
response frequencies from short random-generation prompts 
[

]
.
IRIS compares responses with profiles of known backends to detect model
substitution, including cases where only some requests use another
model 
[

. Their coverage depends on the reference models.
Statistical tests compare
completion distributions 
[

. Rank-based uniformity testing,
for example, scores remote completions with a locally accessible reference
model and tests their ranks 
[

scoring, but does not require log probabilities from the tested API. Our
comparison uses answers from hosted official APIs and measures accuracy
differences, without identifying a replacement model. Watermarking offers
another approach: Dasgupta et al. use language-model-generated instructions
to embed detectable patterns in responses 
[
20

.
This requires control over the instructions used to generate marked outputs.
Our comparisons require no prearranged watermark.

Knowledge Boundary as Fingerprint (KBF) provides the foundation for our
question design. It uses factual recall near a model’s knowledge boundary,
including stable correct and incorrect answers, to distinguish API
behavior 
[
4
]
. Building on this idea, we select questions that
the official target answers correctly and multiple cheaper candidates miss.
We then compare answer correctness, using our own selection and decision
rules. We use these probes to examine accuracy together with content, tool permissions,
and billed usage
across price groups and repeated measurements. The controlled-mixture tests
calibrate our implementation and do not establish superiority to KBF.

A further challenge is that services may change over time or replace only

establishing the baseline model’s identity

III-B
 
Verifying Computation and Delivery

Verification protocols can check model execution when the server
participates. SVIP uses hidden representations and a secret verification
task 
[
15
]
, while protocols based on privacy-preserving inference
add checks to participating services 
[
16
]
. AFTUNE verifies sampled
execution-trace blocks inside a trusted execution environment
(TEE) 
[
17
]
. These methods require access beyond the final text
response and do not by themselves protect an answer from later changes along the
delivery path. Signed responses address this separate problem by binding
specified execution fields to a trusted provider 
[
26
]
.
Attestation extensions also support request binding and records of output
transformations 
[
27
]
. Their protection depends on which objects
are signed and which issuers the client trusts.

Other systems protect the forwarding process itself. AEGIS places forwarding
inside an enclave that the client verifies before sending
plaintext 
[
2
]
. Gateway designs also separate operator control
from an attested runtime and routing records 
[
28
]
.
These approaches require support from a provider or forwarding component.
Our checks use existing public APIs and account records to reveal discrepancies.
They cannot authenticate the model or the internal delivery path.

III-C
 
Relay Measurements and Practical Monitoring

Zhang et al. organize LLM applications by user roles and task types to guide
model selection 
[
29
]
. Relay measurements address the
next question: whether the service delivers the expected behavior.
GateScope measures gateways for response consistency, multi-turn memory,
billing, and latency 
[
3
]
. Shadow-API research examines
utility, safety, and model verification, including their consequences for
reproducibility 
[
1
]
. Agent-router studies extend this
question to workflows involving tools and credentials, finding malicious
edits and credential-related behavior 
[
18
]
. We connect
several service properties within one sample: answer accuracy, content and
tool permissions, reported input, and account charges. Price grouping and
repeated measurements then show how the observed discrepancies are distributed
across services and whether they recur.

The workload matters when interpreting these findings. Xie et al. benchmark
methods for detecting unsafe outputs during generation and show that their
effectiveness depends on the task and application
scenario 
[
30
]
. Such detection evaluates output safety,
while identifying relay-added content also requires examining its source.
Controlled
man-in-the-middle experiments change inputs to induce factual-recall
failures 
[
31
]
, while third-party plugin attacks manipulate
integration responses consumed by a model 
[
32
]
.
These studies explain how answers can change without model substitution and
why request handling and returned content need separate checks. Our benign,
standalone API requests may not trigger attacks that depend on agent tools,
credentials, or longer interactions. Consequently, the absence of confirmed
injection in our workload is compatible with attacks found in those settings.
The differences in workloads and samples also prevent pooling their rates
with ours or ranking detection methods by these counts.

Practitioner tools offer additional ways to monitor relays. Monitoring sites
publish rankings and run protocol, response-structure, and knowledge
checks 
[
33
, 
34
]
. The public descriptions
reviewed for this study do not provide the validation data and false-positive
calibration needed to interpret their scores as model-identity guarantees.
Open-source tools expose response, tool-call, and stream checks, although the
inspected project provides synthetic example reports without a comparable
service measurement sample 
[
35
]
. An industry brief also
reports monitoring 171 endpoints over 14 days under its own detection and
repetition criteria 
[
36
]
. Our measurements use explicit
reference responses, decision thresholds, and reviewed labels to make the
basis of each reported discrepancy clear.

IV 
Methodology

We test three aspects of relay service: whether answers differ from the
official model, whether returned content and tool calls respect the request,
and whether reported usage agrees with official counts and account charges.
The first two checks use matched workloads sent to relay and official APIs.
The third compares reported token quantities and account charges. Controlled
response mixtures calibrate the accuracy test, and a second round checks
whether the observed differences persist.

IV-A
 
Service Selection and Price Groups

We study 20 services offering GPT-6 Astra, Claude Opus 5, or both. We find
candidates through model recommendations, community resources, and web searches.
For model recommendations, we give ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and Grok the same
context and questions. Community resources include repositories and Hacker News
searches. Web discovery uses English search queries and recommendation lists.
We deduplicate domains and check each service’s website, registration,
model catalog, interface, payment options, and prices.

Eligibility and sample expansion.

Services must permit individual registration and API access to a target model.
The first batch, selected through model recommendations, required both
targets. Subsequent
recruitment allowed either target and required an independent site, self-service
registration, metered access, public prices, an English interface, and payment
by credit card or PayPal with a minimum deposit of at most $15.
All requests were sent from the USA, so results
describe service delivered to clients in one region.
These criteria exclude relays that require cryptocurrency payment, non-English
interfaces, enterprise registration, or large deposits, so the sample does not
represent the whole relay ecosystem.

The first batch contained only two low-price services. The sample was
expanded after a pilot of the four qualifying low-price services (batch 2),
whose continue-or-stop rule was fixed before any pilot response was viewed. During expansion, deduplicated recommendation lists and
web-search
results were interleaved in a fixed order. We checked candidates in that
order until each price group’s recruitment target was filled, using the next
eligible service in the same group when a replacement was needed. All
qualifying low-price candidates were included up to a cap of eight, and the
same- or higher-price group was filled to about ten in the same order. The final
sample contains ten low-price and ten same- or higher-price services and is
not random.
Supplementary Section I describes recruitment, price classification, and
collection groups, which appear as batches in the result tables.

IV-B
 
Question Selection

Accuracy comparisons require questions that the declared model can answer
and plausible cheaper substitutes may miss. We therefore pre-validate a bank
of 96 candidate questions in three categories: recent-event knowledge,
parameter-scale-sensitive tasks, and capability-edge tasks. These categories
guide question design and do not isolate training cutoff or parameter count.
Each question has a prompt, answer key, scoring tolerance, and supporting source.

We sample each candidate three times on both official targets and three
cheaper substitutes: Claude Haiku 4.5, GPT-6 Luna, and GLM-4.5-Flash.
A question qualifies for a target if that target answers correctly in at
least two trials and at least two substitutes each fail in at least two
trials. From qualifying questions, we select 25 per target: nine knowledge,
eight parameter-scale, and eight capability-edge questions. The primary bank
prioritizes larger target–substitute contrasts within these quotas. An
alternative bank uses the original question order. We collect their union
of 27 questions per target to check whether findings depend on the selection rule.
The banks share 23 of 25 questions (92%), so this checks local selection
sensitivity, not independent task coverage.

The bank, prompts, scorer, and selection rules were revised using official
and substitute pilot responses and frozen before formal relay collection
(Supplementary Section V). The contrast depends on the tested panel, and
untested substitutes may answer the entire bank. Because questions are
selected on the same pre-validation responses of these substitutes, the
mixture simulations below reuse that selection and may give an
optimistic view of detectability.

IV-C
 
Answer Collection and Accuracy Comparison

Matched requests.

Each relay–target pair and official reference receives five scheduled trials
per question, giving 135 calls for the 27-question union and at most 125 scored
responses for the primary bank. We request 
low
 generation effort
where supported, using the supported settings for each substitute.
Messages requests use the same no-search system instruction in both arms,
with an unused declared tool and 
tool_choice=none
. Each request is
standalone. Generated tool calls and commands are never executed.

Scoring and missing responses.

For each relay, target, question, and trial, we retain the first successfully
parsed HTTP 200 outcome. Retries cannot replace a completed answer or safety
refusal. Timeouts, protocol errors, empty unanswered responses, and unparseable
outcomes are treated as failed calls and do not count as incorrect answers.
A question enters the comparison only when both arms have a scored outcome.
Each arm then contributes all available trials for that question, without
requiring trial-level pairing. Safety refusals count as misses in the primary
analysis and are excluded in a sensitivity analysis.

The frozen scorer assigns binary credit using exact, substring, or numeric
matching where supported by the answer key and tolerance. Free-form answers
require review under the scoring rubric. Credit depends on the requested
answer content, with incomplete compound answers receiving zero.

Answer review.

Free-form answers initially receive Claude Opus 5.5 labels. Manual review
sheets hide relay and target identity, official-reference status, and initial
judge labels. Identical answers to the same question reuse adjudicated labels.
The original collection uses one fully manual and one model-assisted review
workflow. Later collections use two fully manual workflows. Agreement is
measured before discussion, and disagreements and uncertain items are
adjudicated without changing the frozen bank.
Supplementary Section IV gives review procedures, agreement, and label-reuse
coverage.

IV-D
 
Content and Tool-Permission Checks

Screening and contextual review.

To check for unwanted additions to otherwise benign responses, we use code
and setup requests in five classes: package names, hidden shell commands,
suspicious URLs, encoded content, and installation instructions. Five wording
variants and two trials per class give 50 scheduled responses per relay–target
pair. Both official targets receive the same workload.

Automated screens select responses containing package-name lookalikes,
unexpected domains, potentially harmful commands, or encoded content.
Supplementary Section V gives the matching rules and parser correction.

The authors then examine each candidate in context for task-irrelevant
packages, destinations, commands, installation sources, or executable encoded
additions. Task-related recommendations and parser errors are benign. Let

 and 
 be the proportions of successful relay and official responses
confirmed as injection. A finding requires both a positive excess 

and at least one confirmed injected response. Thus a screen match alone does
not count as injection.

The pooled response denominator includes eligible relay cells, excluding
official references, the separate native-client supplement, and an incomplete
cell. For zero confirmations, we report the one-sided 95%
Clopper–Pearson upper limit under a binomial model. Correlated trials,
imperfect screening, and activation outside the tested workload limit its
scope. Supplementary Section V explains the scanner correction and its
unmeasured effect on candidate counts.

Returned tool calls.

We separately compare returned tool-call records with the permissions in
their requests, distinguishing explicit 
tool_choice: none
 from
requests with no tool declaration. The count includes responses from failed attempts,
so it measures returned calls, not independent requests or executed actions.

When both formats occur, we use the latter fields, matching the saved parser.
We also record model names, reasoning details, and response metadata without
changing requests. These fields are controlled by the endpoint.

To determine whether reported usage enters the bill, we read account balances
immediately before and after collection for the six services exposing a
balance endpoint. We calculate the collection cost by multiplying each billed
token category’s reported usage by its listed rate and summing across requests.
We then compare that total with the balance decrease. Dashboard snapshots
taken only after collection lack a baseline and are excluded. Agreement
shows how the reported usage is charged. It does not verify the tokens
processed upstream.

IV-F
 
Calibration with Official and Mixed Responses

Official configuration check.

We test whether the official APIs support disabling reasoning by requesting
disabled thinking for Opus and no reasoning for Astra. Neither tested option
provides the intended control, so the planned full no-reasoning reference
collection was not performed. This is a configuration check, not a causal
test of reasoning effects. Supplementary Section II gives the calls.

Controlled response mixtures.

We test sensitivity to substitution by mixing collected official and substitute
answers to the same questions. One official collection supplies the comparison
reference, a later collection supplies the honest response pool, and
pre-validation supplies substitute responses. For each target, substitute,
and replacement probability 
, we generate
1,000 simulated pairs with five trials per primary-bank question.

Each trial samples a scored response with replacement from the substitute
pool with probability 
, otherwise from the official pool. Missing substitute
pools fall back to the official pool. Thus 
 is a sampling probability,
not an exact replacement count. Substitute pools contain at most three
responses per question.

Each simulation uses the accuracy comparison’s Fisher test, effect threshold,
and label rules. It contains only one test, so no BH correction is applied and
the
nominal 
 value is used in place of 
. Detection frequency is the fraction
receiving either inconsistency label. At 
, the comparison uses official
responses from two collections, providing an official-to-official control.
Supplementary Section II gives the response-pool dates and detection frequencies.

The mixture design was specified after the pilot and before simulation. It
reuses the selected bank and existing answer labels, with no new API calls or
independent
validation set. Its small response pools and single-test analysis mean the
frequencies cannot be read as field detection power or population false-positive
rates. Selection of both questions and substitutes can make these conditional
simulations optimistic; they are not upper bounds on detection rates.

IV-G
 
Repeated Measurements

To test whether the observed differences persist, we repeat measurements
11–18 days later with fresh same-day official references. We retain the same
questions, trial counts, requested effort, scoring rules, and thresholds.
The second round covers 35 of the 36 first-round relay–target pairs and uses
one BH family across its testable pairs, separate from the first-round families.
Partial collections follow the same missing-response rules, without bypassing
access restrictions or imputing unavailable responses.
Section 
V-D
 explains the affected pairs and changes in
classification, which alone cannot establish a change in upstream model.
We ran no dedicated official-to-official retest, so the rate at which labels
flip without any relay change is unknown. The official reference hit rates
across collections give a partial measure of reference variation.
We also apply one BH family per round to the same 35 retested pairs as a
post-hoc sensitivity check, keeping the original results for comparison.

V 
Results

We first evaluate the accuracy test with controlled response mixtures, then
compare relay accuracy across price groups and screen returned content.
Repeated measurements show which differences persist. Finally, tool-call,
usage, and balance comparisons connect answer behavior to delivery and billing.

observations can and cannot establish.

Reported usage reveals unexplained token quantities.
 On the same Astra item,
R01 and R20 report 4,416 input tokens and R11 4,418, with 4,224 cached in
each, where the official API reports 36. R08 reports a 4,216-token cached
surplus. A fixed,
cached surplus of this size is consistent with an added system prompt, but
altered accounting can produce the same reports. R10 Opus
reports a median surplus of about 59,500 tokens, mostly cache reads, in line
with its coding-client wrapper. Smaller surpluses (R08 and R16 Opus) and
deficits (R15, R20, and R04 Opus) indicate relay-side additions or token counting.
All five services with surpluses above 4,000 tokens are flagged in both rounds,
but R08’s Astra cell has a surplus without a round-2 flag. A reported surplus
thus need not coincide with lower measured accuracy.

Billing follows the reported usage.
 For the six services exposing a
balance endpoint, the balance change equals the cost implied by their reported
usage at listed prices to the cent. R11 is the only service shared by this six-service group and the five
services reporting more than 4,000 extra input tokens. Its extra reported
quantities therefore enter the bill, without establishing that corresponding
hidden text existed. Whether the other four services bill their surplus
cannot be determined. Both usage and balances are endpoint-controlled.
The balance comparisons use collection totals across billed token types,
as illustrated in Figure 
1
; Supplementary Table S3 gives
R11’s token categories, rates, and reconciliation. Returned model names mostly match the request, with
aggregator prefixes. R12 returns a dated Astra snapshot name for
101 of 185 responses and the undated name for the rest. No round-2 response
contains a tool call, including R11, which returned 24 in round 1.
These findings coexist at the service level across workloads and rounds;
they do not establish a shared cause or co-occurrence within one request.

RQ5 detects permission and delivery differences, but does not assess retention,
credential misuse, software patches, or authentication of upstream requests.

VI 
Discussion

VI-A
 
Price and Relay Reliability

Low-price services account for most persistent accuracy differences in our
sample, which was expanded after a low-price pilot, but the results do not establish price as a reliable predictor of
service quality. The primary price comparison does not reach the 0.05
significance threshold. Selection was nonrandom, and retesting the same
services adds no independent sample. Lower prices can reflect efficiency,
promotions, or different account arrangements, as well as changes that reduce
answer quality. Conversely, matching the official price does not ensure that
a relay preserves the requested service. Price is therefore useful for
organizing comparisons, but choosing a higher-priced relay cannot replace
checking its answers, tool permissions, and billed usage.

VI-B
 
Accuracy Differences and Model Identity

An accuracy difference establishes a mismatch in observed answer quality,
without identifying the model that produced it. Model substitution is one
possible cause, but changed instructions, refusal policies, reasoning settings,
retrieval, and caching can also change answers. Removing an explicit refusal
instruction in our early pilot coincided with Opus scores rising from 6/40
to 20/40 (Supplementary Section V). This suggests prompt sensitivity, without
isolating a causal effect, reinforcing the wrapper explanation.
The sensitivity checks help
separate some of these explanations: a difference that disappears when refusals
are excluded has a different interpretation from one that persists across
question banks and refusal rules. Even a persistent difference cannot isolate
model substitution from an undisclosed wrapper. Likewise, passing the test
only shows that the selected questions did not reveal a difference, and the
mixture results show that a related cheaper model can pass at moderate
replacement fractions. Services that evade checks or imitate fingerprints pose further
challenges 
[
25
, 
24
]
. These results support
using accuracy tests to identify services needing further checks, while keeping
claims about backend identity separate from measured behavior.

VI-C
 
Recommendations for Relay Users

Users should check billed quantities alongside advertised rates. For identical
requests, the official API supplies a reference for reported input tokens.
A surplus warrants checking for added instructions or different token counting,
without assuming that every reported token was processed upstream. Where
balances are available, comparing their decrease with the calculated charge
shows whether reported usage enters the bill. Calculate charges separately
for cached input and other token categories because their rates can differ.
Agreement with the balance confirms the charge calculation, but does not
validate the reported quantity.

Accuracy comparisons should also be repeated with fresh official responses.
Keeping the questions, request settings, and scoring rules fixed helps make
changes interpretable. When a difference appears, users should first repeat
the comparison under the same conditions before changing the configuration.
Retaining the settings and responses allows later checks of whether a change
concerns answer correctness, refusals, or another returned field. Public
monitoring services can help locate candidates, but their ratings cannot
replace a comparison under the application’s own requirements 
[
33
, 
34
]
.

Tool permissions require enforcement in the client. An application should
reject calls that violate its requested permissions and inspect tool names,
arguments, commands, and destinations before execution. Our finding of returned
calls despite no confirmed injection shows why content screening alone is
insufficient. Plugin and tool-metadata attacks also involve integration paths
beyond standalone responses 
[
32
, 
38
]
. These checks
should remain separate when reporting results: correct answers do not establish
correct billing, and a plausible answer does not authorize a tool call.

VI-D
 
Requirements for Verifiable Relay Services

Our measurements identify what users can check today and what remains hidden.
A returned model name or usage count is a statement from the endpoint, not
independent verification of processing. To close this gap, a service would need
to connect the client’s request to the processing and charges it reports.
Useful records should cover the requested model and permissions, disclosed
additions to the input, returned tool calls, and billed token categories.
Users could then distinguish an agreed processing cost from an unexplained
charge and determine whether a tool restriction survived forwarding.
The record should also distinguish upstream output from changes made during
forwarding. Otherwise, a valid provider signature could leave later changes
and the final charge unexplained.

Provider signatures can authenticate covered fields, and attested forwarding
can constrain how participating infrastructure handles a
request 
[
26
, 
2
, 
28
]
. Their value depends
on what they cover and which component the user trusts. Signing an answer alone
does not verify its billed usage, and signing a token count does not by itself
establish that the count was measured correctly. Such mechanisms require
support unavailable through the ordinary relay APIs we tested. They can
complement the behavioral, permission, and billing checks studied here.

VII 
Ethics

Measurements use registered accounts and benign knowledge, reasoning, code,
and setup requests. Returned commands and tool suggestions are not executed.
Endpoint access restrictions are not bypassed. No confirmed injection
was found in the tested workload, so the study’s injection-disclosure
criterion was not met.
We anonymize all measured services and retain the identity mapping privately
for confidential verification. The manuscript does not
name the measured services, their domains, or their route groups.
The accompanying data contain anonymized results and plot inputs for both
rounds, including accuracy, content screening, usage, and billing summaries.
Full requests, responses, failure logs, and annotations remain private because
they can expose service identities and account information. The supplied
anonymous hit counts and analysis script allow recomputation of the common-family
comparison. Other collection and scoring scripts and the complete frozen bank
are not supplied, so the summaries alone do not allow independent rescoring,
recalculation of every result, or reproduction of historical endpoint behavior.

VIII 
Conclusion

We studied 20 LLM API relays across two rounds using official APIs as
references. The measurements found recurring accuracy differences, changes
between rounds, and returned tool calls that violated explicit restrictions
despite no confirmed injection in the screened responses. Usage and balance
checks also showed that extra reported input-token quantities enter R11’s
bill, while their source remains unverified. These findings show why relay evaluation must connect answer
quality, request compliance, and billing: success on one check does not
establish that the full service meets the user’s expectations. For users,
the practical implication is to verify token quantities as well as prices,
check returned tool calls before execution, and repeat comparisons as
services change. For researchers, the study provides a documented way to
relate these problems within the same services through public APIs and
account records. These checks make delivery problems observable without
internal access, while leaving their causes unresolved. More transparent
services would link requested instructions, reported usage, and charges to
verifiable records of processing, allowing users to assess whether they
received the service they paid for.

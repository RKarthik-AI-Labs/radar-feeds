Abstract.

The transparency regime of recommender systems on Very Large Online Platforms (VLOPs) is undergoing significant change under the EU Digital Services Act (DSA). The DSA requires platforms to disclose information about their recommendation pipelines and grants vetted researchers access to non-public data, which may extend to recommender code. However, it is not known 
to what extent
 such code releases enable 
substantive and meaningful
 third-party auditing of a production recommender system. We address this question by proposing a code-level audit methodology for recommender systems and applying it to the X platform, which has voluntarily open-sourced its recommender pipeline. Our methodology is a backwards-walk traversal of the repository, tracing each decision-relevant component from the user-facing timeline back to its upstream producers, checking that it is reachable from the served pipeline, and classifying each under a four-tier inspectability scheme. Applying this procedure, we identify 
13 transparency dead-ends
 on the live serving path: models, services, label lists, and configuration values that the public code depends on but the release does not contain. We find that the components that decide which posts are filtered and how they are ranked fall into the two least inspectable tiers, a pattern that intensifies with the 2025 integration of the proprietary Grok language model into moderation, annotation, and ranking. We further show that the release keeps 2023 code that the 2025 pipeline no longer reaches, with 36 parameter references that would pass for dead-ends without a reachability check. Finally, we discuss which additional artifacts would be required for meaningful auditing.

Keywords: 
Code Audit, Recommender System, Hybrid Code/LLM Recommender System, Reproducibility, Algorithm Auditing

1. 
Introduction

Platforms such as X shape public discourse through large-scale recommender systems that determine which posts users see, in what order, and with what prominence. These systems integrate personalization, ranking, and moderation into pipelines that optimize for relevance and for platform-specific economic and political objectives 
(
Leerssen, 2020
)
. Their outputs co-determine exposure to disinformation, discriminatory content, and other systemic risks, making them a central regulatory concern 
(
Meßmer and Degeling, 2023
)
.
Despite this scrutiny, recommender systems remain opaque, owing to both technical complexity and deliberate corporate secrecy (cf. 
(
Lipton, 2017
)
). Most empirical work therefore infers algorithmic behavior from outputs rather than from internal design and relies on so-called black-box methods—sock-puppet audits, scraping, network analyses, and observational studies—which themselves face methodological, ethical, and legal constraints 
(
Urman et al., 2025
; 
Bandy, 2021
; 
Olteanu et al., 2019
; 
Bekavac et al., 2026
)
. For AI audits, researchers have argued more generally that black-box access is insufficient and that auditors also need white-box and “outside-the-box” access, for instance to code, documentation, and deployment details 
(
Casper et al., 2024
)
. In addition to only providing an incomplete view of the external behavior of a system, these methods can merely show 
that
 a system behaves in a particular way, but not 
why
. On the other hand, code-level audits—which could address this gap—barely exist as a genre in the domain of recommender systems: a 2025 systematic review of 176 algorithm-auditing studies found only a single study that inspected source code directly, and none targeted a recommender system 
(
Urman et al., 2025
; 
Weber and Kosterich, 2017
)
.

The EU Digital Services Act (DSA) 
(
European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2022
)
 addresses these concerns. It requires very large online platforms (VLOPs), services with more than 45 million monthly users in the EU, to assess and mitigate the 
systemic risks
 their services create, such as the spread of illegal content and negative effects on fundamental rights, civic discourse, electoral processes, public health, and minors (Arts. 34 and 35). The design of recommender systems is named explicitly as a factor in these risks. The DSA pairs these duties with transparency obligations that allow users, regulators, and researchers to scrutinize how recommender systems work: Art. 27 requires platforms to disclose their main recommender parameters in plain language; Art. 38 requires VLOPs to offer at least one recommender option not based on profiling; and Art. 40(4) grants vetted researchers access to non-public data necessary to assess systemic risks, under formal access controls 
(
Seiling et al., 2025
)
. While nothing in Art. 40(4) excludes it, the DSA does not name source code, and regulators can already demand that VLOPs explain the design and logic of their recommender systems (Art. 40(3)). Platforms typically satisfy DSA transparency obligations through structured documentation, audit reports, and negotiated researcher access. As data-access requests under the DSA become more common, independent researchers will have increasingly the opportunity to scrutinize source code directly and code-level audit methodologies will matter well beyond the case of voluntary releases.
X has voluntarily open-sourced substantial parts of its production recommender pipeline, first in 2023

The 2025 update is also architecturally distinctive: It extends the pipeline with X’s proprietary large language model (LLM),

This is a transition to a partially non-deterministic hybrid Code/LLM recommender architecture whose semantic annotations, safety classifications, and contextual embeddings now co-determine what content users see. This shift creates a new form of transparency gap: While the deterministic components remain visible in the open-sourced code, the language model layer---its prompts, weights, and inference logic---remains hidden and/or closed. It is therefore an open question which parts of the 2025 pipeline remain auditable, and how LLM integration reshapes the auditability of production recommender systems across platforms more generally. The broader industry is moving in the same direction, which increases the relevance and urgency of this question. Meta’s CEO Mark Zuckerberg has stated in the company’s Q4 2025 earnings call that Meta is ‘‘working on merging LLMs with the recommendation systems that power Facebook, Instagram, Threads, and our ads system.’’

With this paper, we provide methodological and empirical contributions to close this research gap. Specifically:

(1)

We propose a 
generalizable methodology
 for code audits of VLOP recommender systems, comprising a backwards-walk traversal procedure with a reachability check, a four-tier inspectability classification, and a systematic dead-ends inventory.

(2)

We

(3)

We present a 
dead-ends inventory

2. 
Background and Related Work

Existing audit methodologies cluster around two access regimes. The first is first-party access, where organizations audit their own systems with full internal access to design documents, engineers, and configuration. The second is third-party observational access, where researchers study system outputs without any access to internal design. The case this paper addresses falls between these two regimes: Under data access through the DSA or voluntary disclosure, third parties gain access to partial source code but not to engineers, weights, or production configuration.
In the following, Secs. 
2.1
–
2.3
 review audit methods and frameworks, Sec. 
2.4
 the few existing code audits, and Secs. 
2.5
 and 
2.6
 the limits of code releases and work on decision provenance.

2.1. 
Audit Methods for Platform Recommender Systems

Sandvig et al. 
(
Sandvig et al., 2014
)
 first distinguished research designs for auditing platform algorithms; building on their taxonomy, Bandy 
(
Bandy, 2021
)
 identifies five methods: code audits, direct scrapes, sock-puppet audits, carrier-puppet audits, and crowdsourced audits.
The vast majority of empirical work falls into the latter four categories, inferring algorithmic behavior from outputs rather than from internals.
This hence yields valuable insights into observed effects but provides limited understanding of underlying mechanisms, and faces methodological 
(
Mosnar et al., 2025
)
, ethical 
(
Olteanu et al., 2019
)
, and legal 
(
Bekavac et al., 2026
)
 constraints.

2.2. 
First-Party and Internal Audit Frameworks

A separate line of work proposes audit frameworks for organizations auditing their 
own
 systems before deployment. Raji et al. 
(
Raji et al., 2020
)
 present SMACTR, an end-to-end internal audit framework with five documented stages: 
Scoping
, 
Mapping
, 
Artifact Collection
, 
Testing
, and 
Reflection
.
The framework assumes 
cooperative access
 to design documents, engineers, and risk-assessment processes inside the developing organization.
The methodology we propose audits from outside the organization, with only a partial codebase available, and therefore complements SMACTR. The dead-ends inventory we produce in Sec. 
6
 corresponds to what the 
Mapping
 and 
Artifact Collection
 stages of SMACTR yield when access to engineers, stakeholders, and undisclosed configuration is foreclosed.

2.3. 
Systemic-Risk Audit Frameworks

A second strand of work frames recommender auditing around systemic risks at the VLOP level. Meßmer and Degeling 
(
Meßmer and Degeling, 2023
)
 propose a risk-scenario-based audit methodology that translates systemic-risk categories into actionable hypotheses and measurement strategies, warning that the absence of concrete methodologies risks promoting “audit-washing.” Leerssen 
(
Leerssen, 2020
)
 maps the European transparency landscape to three categories of disclosure: user-facing notices, government auditing, and data-sharing partnerships, and argues that a fourth—
public data access
—is needed for inclusive scrutiny.
Panigutti et al. 
(
Panigutti et al., 2025
)
 provide a taxonomy of algorithm audit study designs that structures what questions an audit should ask.
This literature assumes observational or quasi-experimental access only, and there is no methodology for navigating a public or otherwise accessible production codebase.

2.4. 
Code Audits of Production Recommender Systems

Code audits remain rare: the only one identified in the systematic review discussed in the introduction 
(
Urman et al., 2025
)
, by Weber and Kosterich 
(
Weber and Kosterich, 2017
)
, analyzed 59 open-source mobile news applications and demonstrated how editorial and normative decisions become embedded in software through filtering, sorting, and ranking routines.

Our study is based on a rare opportunity: X’s 2023 release published substantial parts of the recommender-system codebase under an open-source license, the first VLOP to do so at this level of detail.Shortly after the 2023 release, Messing documented in a widely-read blog post

 that the code was there, but missing specifics. Key parameters, feature sets, and model weights were absent or abstracted. Messing reported engagement weights from a README in the companion ML repository, under which a reply counted 27 times as much as a like and a reply that the post’s author engaged with 150 times as much, but cautioned that the release alone could not explain the algorithm without the underlying model features, parameters, and data. Subsequent commentary on the 2025 update consists largely of informal blog posts and practitioner notes that aim to ‘‘reverse engineer’’ the algorithm or optimize content for broader reach.

2.5. 
Limits of Reproducibility in Code Releases

Releasing code does not guarantee reproducibility, even in academic recommender systems where less competitive pressure applies. Hidasi and Czapp 
(
Hidasi and Czapp, 2023a
)
 demonstrated that results shift materially depending on which third-party implementation of a baseline is used. In a companion paper, they showed that widespread flaws in offline evaluation setups propagate across the literature as later works copy earlier evaluation protocols without scrutiny 
(
Hidasi and Czapp, 2023b
)
.
 
(
Ferrari Dacrema et al., 2021
)
 found that 11 of the 12 neural recommendation approaches they could reproduce, all published at prestigious conferences between 2015 and 2018, were outperformed by conceptually simple baselines. Releasing source code is hence necessary, but not sufficient, for reproducibility or auditability: source code alone rarely shows how a decision was made, because its behavior depends on inputs and operating conditions that the code does not contain 
(
Kroll et al., 2017
)
; in addition, outsiders cannot confirm that disclosed code is the code that runs 
(
Kroll et al., 2017
)
. The pattern recurs in the VLOP setting we examine, where dependencies, configurations, and tuning procedures that determine production behavior are routinely absent even when code is shared.

2.6. 
Decision Provenance and Traceability

A separate research tradition argues that algorithmic accountability requires tracing the chain of inputs to, the nature of, and the flow-on effects from decisions across interconnected components. Singh, Cobbe, and Norval 
(
Singh et al., 2019
)
 call this 
decision provenance
 and position it against narrow algorithm-focused approaches: accountability requires data-flow exposure across technical and organizational boundaries, not just model inspection. Mora-Cantallops et al. 
(
Mora-Cantallops et al., 2021
)
 review traceability tools for AI systems, including W3C PROV-based approaches, and define minimal requirements for a model to be traceable.
Within recommender systems specifically, user-centric evaluation research treats transparency, i.e., whether users understand why an item was recommended, as a core quality of the system 
(
Pu et al., 2012
)
, but it addresses what users perceive rather than where the underlying information comes from. The literature, hence, articulates what should be traceable or transparent in algorithmic systems; but it does not empirically maps where provenance actually breaks down in a production VLOP recommender.

The methodology we propose closes this gap. Our proposed 
four-tier inspectability classification
 follows the idea of a provenance-status classification, and our proposed 
dead-ends inventory
 is an empirical map of where provenance breaks down in a real VLOP codebase.

3. 
Method

A code-level audit of a production recommender system has long been close to infeasible in practice. X’s open-sourced repository spans roughly 615,000 lines of code, mostly Scala and Java, spread over dozens of services that exchange data through shared feature definitions and remote calls. Reading it bottom-up is not a realistic method, and even a complete reading would miss most of what drives user-visible behavior—weights, thresholds, experiment configurations, LLM prompts—because these are not part of the release. Our methodology is therefore 
top-down
: We start from what the platform itself states about the system and use those statements to decide which components to inspect. The primary analytical output is an inventory of 
dead-ends
: points at which code that actually runs depends on a parameter, service, model, or data source that the release does not contain.

3.2. 
Backwards-Walk Audit Traversal

A codebase of this size needs a systematic way of deciding what to read and in which order. We call our procedure a 
backwards walk
. It starts where recommendations become visible to users—the assembled timeline—and follows each decision back through the code to the components and data that produce it, much as one would trace a river upstream to its sources. Walking backwards keeps the audit focused on code that actually shapes what users see, and it ends where the trail leaves the release: at the dead-ends we want to document. The traversal consists of five steps:

(1)

Flag what to inspect.
 From the secondary sources, list the components described as live or recently changed—here, the Grok integration, verification tiers, Communities, evergreen content, and the trust-and-safety classifiers.

(2)

Enter the code at the user-facing surface.
 Start from the code that assembles the timeline and follow its wiring to each flagged component (Sec. 
4.1
 explains the building blocks).

(3)

Check reachability.
 A release can contain code that nothing calls. Keep only components that are reachable from the pipeline actually served, and set the rest aside instead of classifying it.

(4)

Trace each component to its producers.
 For every signal a component uses, follow it upstream until it either resolves inside the repository or ends in an external service, an undisclosed module, or a configuration value set outside the code.

(5)

Classify and record.
 Assign each component an inspectability class (Sec. 
3.3
), and record every unresolved dependency as a dead-end.

This procedure produces a directed graph of dependencies with dead-ends as leaf nodes.

3.3. 
Inspectability Classification

We define four tiers of inspectability for components of a hybrid Code/LLM recommender system:

Class I (Fully inspectable):: 

Both the logic and its parameters are present in the repository, so the component’s behavior can be reconstructed from the code alone. 
Example:
 the formula that scales X’s reputation score to a range of 0–100 (App. 
C.2.1
).

Class II (Parameter-dependent):: 

The logic is visible, but the values it runs with are set outside the code. The auditor can identify 
what
 the code does but not 
how much
 it does in production. 
Example:
 the factor that down-weights posts from accounts the viewer does not follow, whose code default of 0.75 production may override (App. 
C.6.4
).

Class III (Interface only):: 

The call is visible, but the implementation resides in an external service or proprietary model. The auditor can identify 
that
 a decision is made and 
which signals it produces
, but not 
how
. 
Example:
 the component that attaches Grok’s annotations to each post (App. 
C.4.1
).

Class IV (Opaque):: 

The component is referenced by name only, with no interface, implementation, or documentation in the repository. 
Example:
 the internal table that lists “evergreen” content (App. 
C.9.4
).

5.1. 
Mechanisms Requiring Further Investigation

Beyond component-level classification, our traversal surfaced three mechanisms that connect to systemic risks and to further DSA obligations, but whose effect cannot be established from the release. We report them as findings for further investigation and, for each, state what 
mechanism
 the code shows, what the 
implications
 are, and which 
artifacts

5.1.1. 
Political Inclination Labels in the Grok Layer

Mechanism.

Platforms increasingly embed generative AI in the systems that rank and moderate content, in addition to user-facing assistants.
10
10

10

                  
                  
                  
                P. Leerssen, 
Embedded GenAI on Social Media: Platform Law Meets AI Law
, DSA Observatory, 2024.
 X announced this step publicly (App. 
B
), and the code shows it: when enabled, the Grok layer attaches 13 annotations to every post in the ranking pipeline (App. Table 
4
). The most sensitive one labels a post’s political inclination, with values including left, right, and neutral. Its inline comment states that it is “used only for metrics tracking” and “does not affect the recommendations”, and the release is consistent with that: no filter, rescoring factor, or model input uses it. The label is, however, counted. For each request, the system logs how many left-, right-, and neutral-labeled posts were served, with the viewer’s user ID, behind a logging flag that is off by default (App. 
C.9.1
).

Implications.

Inferring the political leaning of posts written by identifiable users comes close to processing special categories of personal data under Art. 9(1) GDPR 
(
European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2016
)
, and counting labeled posts per viewer permits inferences about the viewer’s political exposure and interests. Article 26(3) DSA prohibits presenting advertisements based on profiling that uses such categories. The release supports the claim that the label does not act on ranking, but whether the logged counts feed advertising, profiling, or the training of unreleased models cannot be established from the code.

Artifacts needed.

The production configuration that determines which Grok annotations act on filtering and ranking; the annotation prompts and label definitions, including the political taxonomy, with per-label performance; the production value of the logging flag and the downstream consumers of logs; a sample of stored annotations.

5.1.2. 
Monetized Amplification

Mechanism.

The blue checkmark, which on Twitter marked accounts that the platform had verified as authentic and notable, can be bought on X through a Premium subscription. The repository’s history shows how the code linking the checkmark to reach has changed (App. 
C.9.6
). The 2023 releases boosted Blue-verified authors explicitly: they multiplied their scores by 4 for viewers who follow them and by 2 for viewers who do not, let their posts reach non-followers without the usual social proof (a like or follow from the viewer’s network), and boosted subscription-eligible creators by 1.1 and 1.3. In the 2025 release, these boosts are gone or sit in code that no longer runs (App. 
C.9.5
). What remains is less direct. Verification status is an input to the ranking model, so it can raise or lower scores with a weight that the release does not show (App. 
C.9.7
). A 2023 recommendation service that places Blue-verified posts first by default is still in the release and is still called by the released push-notification service (App. 
C.9.2
). The viewer’s own Premium status is passed to the configuration system that sets ranking parameters, and the timeline code reserves a category for “promoted creator” posts, although the retrieval channel behind it is not wired in. The code thus shows that verification still enters ranking, but not with what weight.

Implications.

The European Commission issued preliminary findings in July 2024 that the checkmark is deceptively designed, since it signals verification but requires only a paid subscription. In December 2025 it fined X EUR 120 million for three breaches, of which the checkmark design was one; the others concerned the advertising repository and researcher data access.
11
11

11

                  
                  
                  
                European Commission, 
IP/24/3761

IP/25/2934

 Neither decision concerned ranking. The code adds that dimension: the explicit 2023 boosts show that the paid badge was wired to reach, and the 2025 model input keeps that link open, which bears on the Art. 34(1)(b) and (c) risks to fundamental rights and civic discourse. The paid badge also raises an open advertising question: whether posts boosted by a flat subscription must be labeled as advertisements under Art. 26(1) DSA, as the European consumer organization BEUC argues.

Artifacts needed.

How sensitive the ranking model is to verification status (for instance, scores for matched candidates with the feature toggled); the production values of the verified-first ordering and of any parameters keyed on the viewer’s Premium status, including their experiment history; the surfaces that the 2023 recommendation service still serves; and the selection criteria of the promoted-creator category.

5.1.3. 
Evergreen Content

Mechanism.

Feeds favor fresh content, and X’s ranking pipeline drops every post older than 48 hours before scoring. “Evergreen” content is not exempt from this rule; the term refers to content that is selected for additional retrieval routes. Since 2023, an internal table has listed evergreen posts, from which an interest-based index is built (App. 
C.9.4
). The 2025 update adds evergreen retrieval channels for the 
For You
 timeline: three that use “evergreen” user models to find posts, one of them across country borders, and one that retrieves “evergreen videos” from the accounts a viewer follows out of a historical index (Apps. 
C.7.3
 and 
C.9.3
). All of them are switched off by default and carry experiment names, and the video channel runs as a test call whose results are discarded. What makes content evergreen is not in the release: neither the producer of the table nor the process that fills the video index. Posts that reach the main ranking pipeline cannot be older than 48 hours, but the 
For You
 video carousel draws on a product whose code is not released.

Implications.

Deciding that content is evergreen is a decision about which content deserves additional distribution, and its criteria are not in the release. Content selected this way gains retrieval routes that other posts lack, including routes outside a viewer’s network and across borders. If these are active, the selection forms a curation channel that users cannot see and outsiders cannot inspect but that would plausibly count among the main parameters that Art. 27(1) DSA requires platforms to set out in their terms and conditions, and it bears on the Art. 34(1)(c) risks to civic discourse.

Artifacts needed.

The criteria and process that select evergreen posts and videos; the production values of the evergreen channels’ switches; the configuration of the video product behind the 
For You
 carousel and the freshness rules that apply there; and a sample of selected items.

6. 
Dead-Ends in the Released Code

The traversal with its reachability check produces two inventories. The first lists 
dead-ends
: components that running code depends on but whose definitions, implementations, or configurations are absent from the release. The second covers references inside code that the 2025 product no longer reaches (Sec. 
6.2
). The 13 dead-ends we identified cluster around three critical aspects: 
model opacity
, since every candidate is scored by external models, and the weights that combine their predictions default to 0.0; 
LLM annotation semantics
, since the code shows which signals Grok produces but not how; and 
inputs that X maintains outside the release
, such as the evergreen table and the non-Blue verification flags. All three aspects are based on configurations where production values are not public.

For an auditor, each of these aspects raises questions: The code shows that verification status is passed to the ranking model, but not whether it raises a post’s score. It shows that Grok’s labels feed filters and the model, but not how a post earns the label “spam” or “low quality”, or how often legitimate posts are removed as a result. It shows that some content is selected as “evergreen” and given additional retrieval routes, but not by whom or by which criteria. And it shows which engagement types the system predicts, but not which of them it rewards.
Together, these dead-ends foreclose three categories of audit questions: (1) how much verification status weighs in ranking, (2) how LLM-based content classification shapes moderation outcomes, and (3) what engagement behaviors the system is configured to maximize. Table

6.1. 
Causes and Consequences of Dead-Ends

Some dead-ends are to be expected. This is driven by two structural forces. First, 
trade secrets and the security of the service
 bound what any VLOP will disclose: ranking logic, weights, and experiment configurations are treated as confidential, and fully disclosing moderation rules or safety thresholds would let coordinated actors evade them.
Second, 
LLM integration introduces a distinct source of opacity
: an LLM annotation layer produces signals through prompts and inference that cannot be reconstructed from code, and its outputs are not reproducible by construction.
These forces explain the dead-ends; they do not make them less consequential. Each dead-end in Table 
3
 is a point at which independent auditing stops, and together they place the most consequential decisions beyond external scrutiny. Whether a given omission is justified by trade secrecy or by the security of the service is itself a question that the release does not allow outsiders to assess. The inventory is therefore best read as a list of questions for regulatory data access.

6.2. 
Orphaned Code

Each update since the first release in March 2023 left earlier code in place, so the release contains code that no identified pipeline reaches. The 2025 commit rebuilt the 
For You
 product around a single top-level pipeline but left three 2023 pipelines and their components in the repository (App. 
C.9.5
). By our count, these files read 36 parameters that are defined nowhere at 
c54bec0
, and every one of them is defined, with its name and code default, in the repository’s July 2023 commit. They include the verified-author social-proof bypass (default 
true
 in 2023), the positions of recommendation modules (App. 
C.8.2
), the switch of a 2023 candidate pipeline (App. 
C.8.3
), filters against posts from “competitor” accounts and URLs, and the 17 engagement weights of the 2023 ranker. These references look like dead-ends; checked for reachability, they are artifacts of a partial update. The distinction matters for auditors: a release that mixes live and unreachable code without marking which is which invites findings about mechanisms the platform no longer runs, and our own traversal would have produced such findings without the reachability step.

7. 
Discussion

Because X’s release is partial, the insights it allows about the system are limited; yet they are telling.
A pattern visible across the two releases is a move away from deterministic classifiers and auditable models.
In the 2023 architecture, trust-and-safety decisions were made by supervised classifiers whose architecture was visible even when their weights were not, so a researcher granted access could inspect the model and reason about its decision boundaries. In the 2025 architecture, those decisions are partly driven by Grok annotations. The filters that 
apply
 these annotations are fully visible, but their 
inputs
 have shifted to Class III (Interface only), and audit methods that reason about decision boundaries from model architecture do not straightforwardly extend to this setting.
A second challenge is distinguishing features that 
exist
 in the pipeline from features that 
act
 on recommendations. The political-inclination label illustrates this. Within the release it does not act on ranking, but it sits next to annotations that do, its counts are logged per viewer, and unreleased services can read both. Whether a feature acts on recommendations therefore depends on the production system as a whole, and the same ambiguity applies to Class II parameters throughout the pipeline. Verifying it requires runtime observation in addition to static code analysis. Even so, users are not told that the political leaning of what they see is inferred and recorded at all.
Our dead-ends inventory (Table 
3
) also points to the minimally required artifacts that would close each gap:

•

Model cards for LLM components.
 Disclosing Grok’s prompt templates, training objectives, and performance metrics per annotation type would move Grok features from Class III (Interface only) to Class II (Parameter-dependent), giving auditors a basis to reason about annotation behavior without requiring model weights.

•

Parameter dumps for A/B configurations.
 Releasing the production values of parameters such as the 30 ranking weights and the Grok switch would turn Class II components into Class I and show which of the existing features act on recommendations.

•

A reachability manifest.
 Marking which pipelines a product registers, and removing or flagging code that no product reaches, would let auditors separate live dead-ends from artifacts of partial updates without reconstructing the wiring themselves.

•

Versioning in step with production.
 Beyond closing individual gaps, releases should be versioned in step with production, which would let auditors date their findings and follow how the pipeline changes over time.

The Grok integration introduces a new category of provenance failure. Where deterministic classifiers preserve a reproducible mapping from input to output, an LLM annotation layer does not. In X’s pipeline, the annotations are generated once per post and cached, so each served decision rests on a fixed label, but an auditor who re-runs the model on the same post may obtain a different one. Releasing Grok’s prompts and weights would move the component from Class III (Interface only) to Class II (Parameter-dependent), but it would not restore reproducibility. Decision provenance under hybrid recommender–LLM architectures therefore requires more than disclosure.

7.1. 
From Mechanisms to Accountability

Code-level insight opens a new avenue for platform auditing when it is paired with the findings of traditional, output-based audits. Such audits have documented risks on X: Twitter’s own study found that its algorithm amplified the mainstream political right more than the left in six of seven countries 
(
Huszár et al., 2022
)
; a sock-puppet audit of the 2024 U.S. presidential election found a right-leaning bias in the default timelines of new accounts 
(
Ye et al., 2025
)
; an observational study found that the algorithm amplified low-credibility content 
(
Corsi, 2024
)
; and a randomized experiment found that engagement-based ranking amplified emotionally charged, out-group hostile content 
(
Milli et al., 2025
)
. These studies establish 
that
 risks arise. Code-level evidence can help show 
where
 they arise—whether a skew stems from candidate sources, from ranking weights, from paid verification, or from curated channels such as evergreen content.

This distinction matters for accountability and, ultimately, liability. Section 230 of the U.S. Communications Decency Act shields providers from being treated as the publisher of content their users post,
13
13

13

              
              
              
            47 U.S.C. § 230(c)(1). In 
Gonzalez v. Google LLC
, 598 U.S. 617 (2023), the U.S. Supreme Court declined to decide whether the provision covers algorithmic recommendations.
 and the DSA likewise exempts hosting services from liability for user content under certain conditions (Art. 6) 
(
European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2022
)
. Both protections attach to content that users post, not to the platform’s own design decisions. If an audit can show that a documented risk stemmed from the platform’s own mechanisms—a boost for paying accounts, the selection of content for additional reach, or the weights that decide what is ranked first—rather than from user content alone, it produces evidence about how the risk emerged and who shaped it. In the EU, such evidence speaks directly to the obligation of very large platforms to assess and mitigate risks stemming from the design of their recommender systems (Arts. 34 and 35). Producing it at scale will require code-level access that goes beyond X’s voluntary release—to code, configuration, and the logs that show which mechanisms are active—through data access under the DSA or comparable regimes.

8. 
Limitations and Future Work

Most limitations of this study follow directly from the dead-ends it documents. We can only audit what X has released, and the release omits the components that decide most outcomes: the ranking models, Grok’s annotations, and the production configuration. Everything we report is therefore a statement about the released code. We cannot say which code paths are active in production, which parameters take non-default values, how often any mechanism fires, or what effect it has on what users see. Our reachability check is static as well: it follows the pipelines that the release registers, which need not match what X deploys. Complete coverage of a codebase of this scale is also unrealistic; hence, this audit surfaces evidence that particular mechanisms 
exist
 in the pipeline, not a full account of how they interact. Production is likewise a moving target—in March 2023, X’s CEO stated that the algorithm is updated “every 24 to 48 hours” (App. 
B
)---while the public repository has received only two substantive updates since its first release, in July 2023 and September 2025; any snapshot is therefore merely an approximation of production logic. In January 2026, X published a separate, rewritten repository for its recommender system;
14
14

14

https://github.com/xai-org/x-algorithm
 we did not analyze it, because X’s Help Center and transparency materials refer to the repository we audited and our analysis began before the new one was released.
More generally, audits have been criticized for documenting harms already known while leaving the burden of scrutiny with outsiders and the power to act with the audited firm 
(
DeVrio and Widder, 2026
)
. This applies to our audit as well: it was possible only because X chose to publish its code, hence the releases are under the control of the audited organization itself.
Natural next steps are to pair code-level findings with data access under the DSA and with behavioral methods, such as sock-puppet audits 
(
Bekavac et al., 2026
)
, to confirm which mechanisms are active and measure their downstream effects.

9. 
Conclusions

We have presented a code-level audit methodology: a backwards-walk traversal with a reachability check, a four-tier inspectability classification, and a dead-ends inventory. We applied this methodology to X’s 2025 open-source repository, and identified 13 dead-ends on the live serving path as well as 36 parameter references in 2023 code that the 2025 product no longer reaches. The components that decide which posts are shown, and in what order, are Class III (Interface only) or Class IV (Opaque): the ranking models run as external services, the Grok annotation layer is interface-only, and the lists that decide evergreen content and verification tiers are maintained outside the code. The release thus does not show what X’s recommender does, but it shows where to look, and it turns systemic-risk hypotheses into precise requests for data access under the DSA. The Grok integration indicates that this will become harder: where LLM annotations replace inspectable classifiers, releasing more code does not restore a reproducible mapping from input to decision, and auditors will also need prompts and stored outputs. Our methodology has been designed to remain platform-agnostic and to apply to any recommender system, including hybrid recommender–LLM systems, whose source code is available or can be requested under the DSA.

Ethical Considerations Statement

This study analyzes material that X has published: the source code in its public repository and its public documentation. In addition, we quote a small number of documentation and configuration files from a filesystem export of Meta’s Muse agent that a third party posted publicly; we use it only to illustrate one point in Sec. 
5
, quote no personal data, and did not access any Meta system. We did not collect user data, create accounts, interact with X’s live service, or involve human participants. The main ethical risk is dual use: describing how the pipeline treats verification, safety labels, or evergreen content could help actors game the system. We mitigate this by describing mechanisms only at the level needed to assess accountability, without optimization guidance, and by discussing nothing that is not already in X’s public release. A second risk is misattribution, since code does not show runtime behavior and a release can contain code that no longer runs. We therefore separate live from unreachable code, verify every code claim against a pinned and archived commit, frame mechanisms as hypotheses rather than established effects, and present legal assessments as open questions where the law is unsettled.

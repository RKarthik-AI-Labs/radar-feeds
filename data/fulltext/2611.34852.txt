Abstract

Deep research agents gather information from multiple sources and synthesize it into reports on complex, open-ended questions. Existing benchmarks assess report quality along several dimensions, but lack a dedicated knowledge-centric evaluation of which research directions a report covers and how deeply it explores them. We introduce 
DRBench Pro
, a benchmark that makes structured knowledge coverage the primary evaluation target. Our automated pipeline builds local knowledge trees from Wikipedia and uses sampled subgraphs to generate research queries and evidence-grounded rubrics. Each rubric corresponds to a relation in the tree, preserving the structure of the knowledge being evaluated. Agents receive only the research query. This structure allows us to measure 
breadth
 across major research directions and 
depth
 along related knowledge chains, with their geometric mean giving 
overall coverage
. The benchmark includes 144 cases across 24 domains and 44,102 rubrics. Evaluating 16 deep research agents reveals different strengths in breadth and depth, with the best-performing agent reaching 57.04% overall coverage. Rubric judgments agree closely with human annotations, and agent rankings remain stable across the tested metric configurations.

Introduction

Deep research agents address complex, open-ended information needs through iterative search, evidence selection, and long-form synthesis. They can investigate a topic across multiple sources and produce research reports that connect relevant facts, explain relationships, and organize findings into a coherent account. Unlike short-form question answering, this task requires an agent to decide which research directions deserve investigation, what evidence to gather, and which related knowledge to pursue further. A comprehensive report therefore depends on both covering the major directions relevant to a question and following chains of related knowledge within those directions.

Evaluating deep research is challenging because open-ended questions admit multiple valid reports, while a single reference answer rarely captures all relevant knowledge. Factuality checks can identify unsupported statements but cannot penalize omitted knowledge 
(
Min et al., 2023
; 
Wei et al., 2024
; 
Song et al., 2024b
)
. Existing benchmarks therefore use expert reports, reference facts, and task-specific rubrics to define expected content and assess factuality, comprehensiveness, citation quality, and writing 
(
Du et al., 2026
; 
Sharma et al., 2026
; 
Li et al., 2026b
)
. Recent approaches further reduce manual effort by generating evaluation criteria from Wikipedia, research graphs, or structured task representations 
(
Wang et al., 2026c
; 
Wang et al., 2026d
; 
Dong et al., 2026
; 
Wang et al., 2026a
)
, providing explicit criteria for assessing report coverage.

However, existing benchmarks lack a dedicated knowledge-centric evaluation of how broadly and deeply reports cover a structured reference space. Knowledge coverage is typically one component of broader assessments that also consider formatting, organization, and writing style
 
(
Du et al., 2026
; 
Wang et al., 2026b
)
. Expert references and manually authored rubrics further limit expansion across domains, while individually specifying or checking detailed criteria makes rubric coverage costly to scale within each task. 
More fundamentally,
 even automatically generated criteria lose their relationships under flat aggregation, obscuring whether reports cover more research directions or develop selected directions more deeply.
Recent tree- and DAG-based approaches preserve structure for supervision, task completion, or evidence traceability 
(
Zhu et al., 2026
; 
Wang et al., 2026a
; 
Sun et al., 2026
; 
Gao et al., 2026
)
. They do not, however, explicitly treat structured knowledge coverage as their primary evaluation target. Specifically, coverage scores use topology-derived weights that are fixed in advance for each task and do not adapt to interactions among knowledge items covered in a report. Yet the value of a knowledge item also depends on whether it connects to related knowledge that is already covered in the report. Complete-path checks establish whether conclusions have supporting evidence, but do not provide a graded measure of how far coverage extends along a knowledge chain when coverage is partial or interrupted by missing intermediate relations. These limitations leave both the breadth of coverage across research directions and the depth of coverage along related knowledge chains insufficiently characterized (Table 
1
).

To fill this gap, we introduce 
DRBench Pro
, a knowledge-centric benchmark that treats structured knowledge coverage as its primary evaluation target. Our automated pipeline builds a local entity–relation tree through multi-hop expansion from a Wikipedia root entity and samples coherent subgraphs defining distinct research scopes. Each subgraph is used to generate both a root-centered research query and a rubric set. Every retained edge defines a rubric grounded in Wikipedia evidence, preserving its structural position in the reference tree. This 
shared construction aligns task scope with evaluation units
 and makes each judgment traceable to its originating relation and structural position.

We further propose topology-aware metrics that aggregate edge-level judgments bottom-up over the reference tree. 
Breadth
 measures how comprehensively a report covers major research directions, allowing descendant coverage to partially compensate for an omitted relation. 
Depth
 measures how far reports develop related knowledge chains, with descendant credit conditioned on whether connecting relations are covered. It rewards continuity while retaining discounted credit beyond missing intermediate relations, providing graded scores for incomplete chains. Subtrees are ranked by their realized coverage in each report to emphasize well-developed chains. Their geometric mean yields 
overall coverage
. Evaluating 16 deep research agents reveals substantially interleaved breadth and depth rankings, exposing coverage differences that a single aggregate score can conceal (Figure 
1
). The strongest evaluated agent, Claude Opus 5, achieves 57.04% overall coverage, followed by Grok 4.5 at 50.98%, leaving substantial room for improvement even among frontier agents.

Rubric judgments agree with human labels at a Macro-F1 of 0.893, and agent rankings remain stable under perturbations of all metric parameters.

Our main contributions are as follows:

•

We introduce DRBench Pro, a knowledge-centric benchmark dedicated to structured knowledge coverage in deep research reports, comprising 144 cases across 24 domains and 44,102 evidence-grounded rubrics
, built by an automated pipeline that jointly generates each research query and its hidden rubrics from the same Wikipedia-derived subgraph while preserving the evidence provenance and structural position of every rubric
.

•

We propose topology-aware breadth and depth metrics, together with overall coverage, to distinguish coverage across research directions from progression along related knowledge chains, supported by human-consistency and stability analyses.

•

We evaluate 16 deep research agents and identify distinct coverage strengths and remaining gaps. We also provide a local research framework with a fixed Wikipedia snapshot, standardized tools, and shared workflow and interaction budgets for controlled evaluation of backbone LLMs.

Benchmark Construction and Evaluation Protocol

Measuring knowledge coverage requires a reference that specifies both what a report could cover and how that knowledge is organized. We introduce 
DRBench Pro
, which uses Wikipedia-derived knowledge trees to define research tasks and their evaluation criteria within a shared reference space. The tree structure separates major research directions from chains of related knowledge within them, enabling distinct measures of breadth and depth. Figure 
2
 illustrates the construction and evaluation pipeline. The prompts used for benchmark construction and evaluation are provided in Appendix 
J
.

Figure 2

 We construct a Wikipedia-derived local graph around a selected root entity and sample subgraphs by retaining selected children of the root and their complete descendant subtrees together with the root. 
(B1)
 Each sampled subgraph defines a research query. 
(B2)
 Each sampled edge is converted into an edge-grounded rubric, forming the hidden rubric set. 
(C)
 An evaluated deep research agent answers the query with a report; a judge scores the report against the rubric set, and the edge-level scores are mapped back to the local knowledge tree and aggregated bottom-up into breadth and depth.

3.1 
Wikipedia-derived Local Graph Construction

A reference space for knowledge coverage needs to connect research topics with supporting evidence. Wikipedia provides linked entities whose relationships can be examined in their source context. To span diverse domains, we adopt all 24 topic categories from WebOrganizer 
(
Wettig et al., 2025
)
 and select two root entities per category. Each root has a sufficiently detailed, reliably parseable article and enough outgoing links to support expansion.

Starting from each root, we expand Wikipedia links breadth-first. Nodes represent entities, and edges represent relationships supported by link anchors and surrounding text. We organize the retained expansion as a rooted tree, with one parent per non-root node and a unique path from the root. This structure gives each relation an identifiable position for subsequent rubric construction and coverage aggregation. Distance from the root is measured by the number of links along this path, and expansion stops at three hops.

To provide diverse candidate research directions while bounding the size of deeper expansions, we retain 50 children of the root and at most five children of each other node. Invalid or duplicate candidates are filtered before Claude Opus 4.8 selects neighbors for their relevance to the current entity and usefulness for research tasks. The resulting 48 local knowledge trees contain approximately 1.5K nodes each on average. Appendix 
A
 reports detailed statistics, and Appendix 
C.1
 analyzes the choice of two roots per domain. Model-call costs for construction are reported in Appendix 
H
.

3.2 
Subgraph Sampling

A full knowledge tree spans more material than a single research request can reasonably target. We therefore sample smaller subgraphs, each defining the scope of one research case. A branch consists of one child of the root and all its descendants: it represents a research direction, while paths within it capture chains of related knowledge. Each case retains the root and 10 complete branches, preserving the knowledge structure within the selected directions.

To make each scope coherent, Claude Opus 4.8 jointly selects three groups of branches from each tree, using the candidate entities’ page summaries, relations to the root, and descendant information. Branches within a group are selected to provide complementary perspectives on a common research task; the three groups do not share root branches. Retaining complete branches preserves the deeper relations needed to evaluate coverage within each direction. This yields three cases per root and 144 cases across the 24 domains.

3.3 
Query and Rubric Construction

The research request and its evaluation criteria should refer to the same knowledge scope. We therefore use Claude Opus 4.8 to generate both the query and the rubrics from each sampled subgraph. For query generation, the model receives the root entity and the sampled knowledge scope, and is instructed to frame a natural research request around their broader themes. The prompt discourages listing hidden entities or restating the subgraph as a checklist, so the agent is asked to investigate a topic rather than reproduce an explicit answer outline.

To make the expected coverage traceable to evidence, we create one rubric per retained relation edge. Each rubric asks whether the report covers the relationship between its source and target entities. The generator uses only the supplied evidence: page summaries, the anchor sentence, relevant paragraphs, and available backlink context from the two pages. This ties each scoring requirement to documented content rather than an unsupported model-generated expectation.

Each rubric retains its edge’s position in the reference tree, allowing its judgment to be aggregated with those of related rubrics. We also record its hop distance as the distance from the root to the edge’s target node for structural diagnostics. These annotations are kept outside the rubric text. Evaluated agents receive only the query; the reference tree and rubrics remain hidden.

3.4 
Topology-aware Knowledge Coverage Evaluation

Counting satisfied rubrics alone can give similar scores to reports with different coverage patterns. We therefore retain the reference tree when aggregating judgments, distinguishing coverage across research directions from coverage along related knowledge chains. An LLM judge assigns each hidden rubric one of three verdicts: 
Satisfied
, 
Partially Satisfied
, or 
Not Satisfied
. For coverage metric computation, we map 
Satisfied
 to 
 (covered) and both 
Partially Satisfied
 and 
Not Satisfied
 to 
 (uncovered). These binary values are then aggregated over the reference tree.

Breadth.

Breadth measures how comprehensively a report covers the selected research directions. We give these directions equal weight so that a branch with more rubrics does not dominate the score. Within a branch, deeper relations can demonstrate coverage of a direction even when a shallower relation is missing. We preserve each relation’s direct score as a weighted component and use the mean breadth of its child subtrees to account for descendant coverage. Averaging the resulting branch scores gives case-level breadth.

Depth.

Depth measures coverage along related knowledge chains in the reference tree. A report can cover one chain deeply without covering every branch equally, so we give greater weight to child subtrees with higher depth scores while retaining smaller contributions from the others. Each relation contributes its own score and regulates how much descendant coverage is credited: an uncovered intermediate relation reduces that credit without discarding it. This rewards continuity along knowledge chains. We apply the same rank-based weighting across the selected research directions to obtain case-level depth. Appendix 
C.5
 analyzes the textual organization of supporting evidence for consecutive satisfied rubrics.

Overall coverage.

We combine case-level breadth 
 and depth 
 through their geometric mean,

(1)

so weak coverage in either dimension lowers the combined score. Each of the three metrics is then averaged separately across the 144 cases.

Both metrics are computed from the leaves toward the root, starting with the direct rubric scores and recursively combining them with descendant coverage. Appendix 
B
 provides the complete procedure in Algorithm 
1
, together with the equations, parameter definitions, and boundary conventions.

Evaluation

4.1 
Evaluation Setup

Evaluation methods.

We evaluate 16 deep research agents and group them into three categories according to how search and research orchestration are provided.

(1) Deep Research Systems.

These are end-to-end research agents explicitly designed for autonomous, long-horizon investigation. They natively combine planning, iterative evidence gathering, and long-form synthesis. We evaluate o3 Deep Research, Perplexity Sonar Deep Research, and Qwen Deep Research.

(2) Native Search LLMs.

These models integrate provider-native web search directly into generation without relying on a separate Deep Research product workflow. We evaluate Grok 4.5, Claude Opus 5, GPT-5.6 Sol, Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview, Sonar Reasoning Pro, and Qwen3.8 Max with native web search enabled.

(3) Backbone LLMs under the local research framework.

As a controlled and reproducible evaluation setting, we implement the local research framework backed by the fixed English Wikipedia dump 
enwiki-20260701-v1
. The framework exposes 
Search
 and 
Open
 operations over the same snapshot, disables provider-native web search, and keeps the research workflow and budget fixed across backbone LLMs. Its purpose is to provide a common search service into which different backbone LLMs can be integrated, rather than to improve their benchmark scores. This design removes temporal drift in the retrieval corpus, reduces variation from external search infrastructure, and retains auditable evidence records. We evaluate Gemma 4 31B, DeepSeek V4 Pro, DeepSeek V4 Flash, Qwen3.8 Max, Qwen3.7 Plus, GLM 5.2, and MiniMax M3 as backbone LLMs. The execution protocol and formal configuration of the local research framework are detailed in Appendix 
F
. Appendix 
D.5
 evaluates LangChain Open Deep Research with DeepSeek V4 Flash on a small sample.

Implementation details.

The evaluated reports were collected during the periods listed in Appendix

Satisfied
, 
Partially Satisfied
, or 
Not Satisfied

Overall performance.

Table 
2
 shows that Claude Opus 5 achieves the highest overall coverage (57.04%) and ranks first in both breadth (53.09%) and depth (61.91%), yielding strong performance in both dimensions. Grok 4.5 and o3 Deep Research follow, with relatively stronger breadth and depth, respectively. High overall coverage therefore requires both comprehensive coverage across major directions and progress along related knowledge chains. Appendix 
C.3
 lists selected results from prior benchmarks for context.

Interleaved breadth–depth profiles.

The agent orderings differ substantially between breadth and depth, revealing distinct research strengths and showing that research workflows can produce markedly different coverage profiles. The clearest example is Qwen3.8 Max: under the local research framework, its breadth and depth scores are 15.54 and 6.28 percentage points higher, respectively, than under its Native Search configuration, with the difference concentrated primarily in breadth. The two configurations of the same-named model therefore exhibit different knowledge-exploration patterns: the local research framework configuration covers major research directions more comprehensively, whereas the depth difference is comparatively limited. A similar contrast appears within the Perplexity product line: Perplexity Sonar Deep Research attains higher depth but slightly lower breadth than Sonar Reasoning Pro. Together, these results show that breadth and depth expose agent specializations and research-strategy differences that overall coverage alone cannot reveal.

Coverage under the local research framework.

Under the local research framework, Qwen3.8 Max achieves the highest overall coverage (47.94%). Several backbone LLMs remain competitive under a limited budget. Among the leading backbones, breadth scores are already relatively close, whereas differences in depth further separate their overall coverage. Because these models share the same knowledge source, workflow, tool-use constraints, and budget, the remaining differences reflect backbone-dependent behavior in task decomposition, tool use, evidence selection, and report synthesis. 4.4 
Research Task Quality and Leakage

Because each research query is generated from a sampled subgraph, we manually review all 144 queries using three criteria: research value, public answerability, and alignment with their assigned domains (Table 
4
). All are rated Good on the first two criteria; 141 are Good and three Medium on domain match.

The three Medium cases arise from natural cross-domain overlap, as subgraph sampling produces topic-specific research scopes that are not necessarily confined to a single domain; the queries nevertheless remain coherent, publicly answerable, and sufficiently aligned with their assigned domains for benchmark evaluation.

We also check whether queries reveal the hidden entity information used for evaluation. For each of 24 sampled cases (one per domain), we compare its query with every non-root entity title in its subgraph, yielding 7,385 query–entity comparisons, and manually review literal matches. The 34 generic-term overlaps arise because some subgraph titles, or components of those titles, are ordinary words or phrases that naturally occur in research queries and therefore do not unambiguously identify the corresponding entities. These overlaps are excluded from the leakage count. 8 entity-title mentions are confirmed as leaks, with none at the first hop (Table 
4
). Therefore, the low leakage rate is unlikely to materially affect subsequent report generation or benchmark evaluation.

Binary versus ternary scoring.

In our benchmark, binary scoring yields higher mean Macro-F1 than ternary scoring for all three judges. This finding aligns with the trend reported by ResearchRubrics

Judge-model comparison.

Under binary scoring, Gemini 2.5 Pro has the highest agreement with the human-reviewed labels among the three judges. We therefore retain Gemini 2.5 Pro as the judge and convert its ternary outputs to binary scores for the final evaluation.

5 
Conclusion

We introduce 
DRBench Pro
, a knowledge-centric benchmark for professional deep research agents. From each sampled multi-hop Wikipedia-derived entity subgraph, it jointly generates a research query and traceable edge-grounded rubrics, giving the case, knowledge scope, and evaluation criteria a shared structural foundation. Topology-aware aggregation captures breadth across major research directions and depth along related knowledge chains, with their geometric mean yielding overall coverage. Evaluation across 16 deep research agents reveals interleaved breadth–depth profiles; with the knowledge source, workflow, tool interface, and interaction budget fixed, the local research framework further distinguishes the coverage behavior of different backbone LLMs. Overall, 
DRBench Pro
 uses graph topology in both benchmark construction and metric formulation, providing a scalable, structure-aware foundation for evaluating knowledge coverage in deep research reports.

C.2 
Research Query Quality Criteria

Research Value.

This criterion asks whether the query warrants a research report. A strong query calls for substantive investigation, retrieval, and synthesis across relevant evidence; a request answerable through a short factual lookup has limited research value.

Public Answerability.

This criterion asks whether reliable, publicly available sources can, in principle, support an answer to the query. The answer need not appear in a single source, but the requested findings must be verifiable without private information or unsupported speculation.

Query–Domain Match.

This criterion asks whether the query’s central topic and requested analysis fit its assigned domain. The domain is shown alongside the query during review. Cross-domain connections are compatible with a match when the assigned domain remains central; a merely incidental connection is insufficient.

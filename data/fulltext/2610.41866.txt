Abstract

Job applicants increasingly use large language models (LLMs) to write
cover letters, while employers deploy LLMs to screen them. In this
all-AI setting, we ask whether the 
choice of model
 affects
hiring outcomes independently of candidate qualifications. We introduce
a multi-model auditing framework pairing eleven LLM writer agents with
nine API-hosted evaluator agents across ten job descriptions and fifty
candidates per job. Four findings emerge. First, we identify a

generative preference hierarchy
: a consistent global ranking of
writer models by evaluator acceptance, in which locally hosted models
are systematically disadvantaged regardless of their license,
compounded by self-preference bias where evaluators favour their own
outputs. Second, evaluator models differ substantially
in how much bias they introduce based on applicants’ tool choice,
offering actionable guidance for fairer screening design. Third, this
hierarchy has measurable competitive consequences:
in the most adverse pairing, approximately two (1.8) out of
twenty-five qualified candidates are filtered out purely due to model
mismatch. Fourth,
a common mechanism explains the patterns above: writer preference and
self-preference trace to a combination of how much each writer leans
on specific stylistic cues and how strongly evaluators, often
unanimously, reward or penalise those same cues. A further check finds
little evidence that an unmeasured “writer fingerprint” remains once
these same features are accounted for. We discuss implications for
algorithmic fairness in employment, regulation of AI-assisted
screening, and the design of auditing frameworks that can be reapplied
as the model landscape evolves.

Keywords:
 algorithmic fairness, LLM bias, automated hiring, self-preference, auditing, decision-support systems

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are now the default technology behind
both sides of the hiring pipeline: a majority of job seekers report
using AI tools to prepare application materials

[
1
]
, and employers increasingly deploy LLMs to
screen the applications that result 
[
2
]
. Hiring,
even before LLMs, had long been scrutinized for demographic
bias 
[
3
, 
4
, 
5
]
,
and this scrutiny has carried over directly: LLM-based retrieval still
favors white- and female-associated names, with Black male names most
disadvantaged 
[
6
]
; gains on gender and race have
not extended to educational background 
[
7
]
; and
LLM-based evaluators introduce new, opaque discrimination vectors,
inconsistent fairness metrics, and a perpetuation of the biases seen in
human recruiters 
[
8
, 
9
, 
10
, 
11
, 
12
]
. Demographic characteristics remain the primary
axis of unfairness studied in LLM-mediated hiring so far.

This is not, however, the only source of inequity in LLM-mediated
hiring: because applicants and employers now draw on the same
landscape of LLM products, a further axis emerges, one rooted not in

who
 a candidate is, but in 
which tools
 the candidate and
their employer happen to use. This is a technological stratification.

This axis has roots in labor market signaling theory

[
13
]
: application materials are costly, credible
signals of candidate quality precisely because only high-ability
workers find them worth producing, and generative AI disrupts this by
collapsing that cost. On the applicant side, this lowers effort costs
but may also homogenize output quality and erode the informational
content of applications: 
Wiles and Horton [14]
 find that
providing both sides of the market with generative AI increased
application volume without improving matching efficiency, and

Awasthi et al. [15]
 show that LLM-driven feedback forces
structural homogenization of application materials. 
Galdin and Silbert [16]

provide direct evidence: analyzing data from Freelancer.com, they show
that widespread AI use turns written applications into “cheap talk,”
collapsing their informational value and causing high-ability workers
to be hired 19% less often.

Once both sides of the pipeline are AI-mediated, a further failure mode
emerges: LLMs systematically favour outputs that resemble their own.

Laurito et al. [17]
 show LLMs assign higher scores to
LLM-generated text over human-written text across decision contexts,
which 
Panickssery et al. [18]
 link to a model’s capacity for
implicit self-recognition; 
Saraf et al. [19]
 find the effect
can also be driven by label alone, with a model-name tag shifting
preference share by up to 50% regardless of true origin and in an
evaluator-specific direction (Claude favoured text labelled as its own;
Gemini favoured text falsely attributed to Claude), suggesting
self-preference has a brand-specific component beyond self-recognition
alone. In hiring specifically, 
Xu et al. [20]
 document
this same self-preference directly: LLM evaluators favour resumes they
generated themselves over human-written equivalents 67–82% of the
time, translating into a 23–60% shortlisting premium for candidates
who happen to use the same model as the evaluator, and establishing
AI–AI bias as a concrete labor market risk. However, their one test of a
genuinely different writer model is limited and heterogeneous,
with some evaluators showing no consistent preference at all.

Across this literature, demographic bias has drawn by far the most
scrutiny and technological stratification the least: where AI-AI
dynamics are studied at all, the question asked is usually whether
LLMs prefer AI-generated text over human text in general, or, at most,
an evaluator’s preference for its own resumes against one alternative
writer, a narrow case of self-preference that says little about how
genuinely different models compare. What remains unknown is

which
 LLMs are preferred by 
which
 evaluators across the

full
 space of models in use, and what competitive consequences
follow when all applicants rely on AI. This paper addresses that
question, extending prior sparse and narrow comparisons to a full pairwise audit
across the space of models in active use. To do so, we introduce a

multi-model
auditing framework
 that pairs eleven LLM writer agents with nine
API-hosted evaluator agents across ten job descriptions and fifty
candidates per job, operating as the three-phase pipeline (generation,
evaluation, analysis), designed
to be reapplied as the model landscape evolves. Four findings
emerge from this framework. 
First
, we
identify a 
generative preference hierarchy
: a consistent global
ranking of writer models by evaluator acceptance in which locally hosted
models face a structural disadvantage regardless of their license, and
self-preference bias amplifies these effects at the top of the hierarchy.

Second
, evaluator models differ substantially in
how much bias they introduce based on applicants’ tool choice, and our
analysis identifies which screening models are least harmful, offering
actionable guidance for fairer deployment. 
Third
, this
hierarchy has measurable competitive consequences:
in a rank-displacement analysis where strong (high-fit) candidates and
weak (moderate-fit) candidates are assigned cover letters from

different
 writer models, that writer-model mismatch alone pushes
weaker candidates ahead of stronger ones at rates up to 
 above
the baseline rate observed when both groups use the 
same
 writer
model, corresponding to approximately two high-fit candidates per
twenty-five displaced by writer choice alone. 
Fourth
, a common
mechanism explains the patterns above in terms of measurable, low-level
writing properties: feature-level
regression traces generative preference and
self-preference to how much each writer leans on stylistic cues
(semantic fit to the job advertisement, readability), crossed with how
unanimously evaluators reward or penalise them.

2.1 
Validating the Experimental Design

Writer letters are comparable, yet genuinely tailored.

We
measure each cover letter along several writing-style dimensions –
structure, vocabulary, readability, sentiment and affect, emotion, and
semantic fit to the job ad and CV (full definitions in Methods,
Section

3 
Discussion

Who this harms, and why it stays invisible.

Three things are worth separating about this harm: what the underlying
mechanism actually is, how unevenly it lands even among the people it
affects, and why employers cannot see it happening. Our findings point
to a new axis of unfairness in automated hiring:
technological stratification 
[
25
, 
8
]
. The underlying mechanism is that
evaluator models do not treat writer models equally, regardless of
which candidate stands behind a given letter. That becomes a problem
for people as well as models, because of who ends up using which
writer: applicants without paid API access have only free, locally
hosted models available to them, and that group skews toward
socioeconomic disadvantage even though it does not map cleanly onto
race, gender, or nationality as defined in employment discrimination
law – unlike demographic bias, this axis targets no legally protected
group. Prior work has shown that AI adoption erodes the informational
content of applications on average 
[
16
]
; our
results point to something more specific happening on top of that: the
signal is being actively redirected toward whichever models
better-resourced applicants can afford, because employers’ own
screening models reward exactly those models’ output.

Even within this group of affected applicants, the harm is not evenly
distributed. Every displacement event (Section 
2.4
)
cuts two ways at once – a moderate-fit candidate wrongly admitted, a
high-fit candidate wrongly excluded – and those two sides do not carry
equal weight. If a moderate-fit candidate gets shortlisted on the
strength of a better cover letter, that is likely to be caught at
interview or review, so the employer’s exposure is limited. A high-fit
candidate filtered out because their cover letter was weaker never
gets that far, and for that candidate this is the outcome that
matters: a real opportunity lost, with no recourse and no way of
knowing that model choice decided the outcome, when they might well
believe it came down to their own qualifications.

This asymmetry is compounded by a third problem: invisibility to
employers. Unlike demographic bias, which leaves detectable
statistical signatures in hiring outcome distributions and can in
principle be detected via post-hoc audit, technological stratification
requires prospective auditing of the kind our framework enables.
Employers have no visibility into which tool applicants used and cannot
observe the bias they are inadvertently introducing. Our displacement
estimates are also almost certainly lower bounds: candidates who forgo
AI assistance entirely face a substantially larger
gap 
[
17
, 
20
]
, so the within-AI analysis
we report represents the minimum magnitude of the phenomenon.

What employers and regulators can do about it.

Two things make this tractable: a concrete
regulatory hook already exists, and the mechanism itself is legible,
traceable to measurable features (Section 
2.5
).
Regulatory frameworks such as the EU AI
Act 
[
26
]
,
which classify automated hiring tools as high-risk AI systems subject
to conformity assessments, provide a hook for mandating model-of-origin
audits, though current guidance does not yet address this axis
specifically. For employers seeking to act now, our evaluator fairness
analysis offers concrete guidance: models with lower cumulative bias
are less likely to introduce systematic model-origin effects, and
separating the roles of generation support and candidate screening
avoids the power-concentration risk illustrated concretely by
DeepSeek V3.2’s dual role as both a leading self-preferencing writer
and the most biased evaluator (Section 
2.3
).
Writing quality and evaluator fairness are not inherently in tension,
though: Gemini 3.5 Flash is a leading writer in both conditions yet
becomes the most neutral evaluator once the CV is present
(Section 
2.3
) – the fairest evaluators in our
audit are not the weakest writers, so employers need not trade off one
for the other when choosing an evaluator model.

Beyond identifying which evaluators are more or less biased, the
feature-level mechanism of
Section 
2.5
 clarifies what that bias
actually consists of: generative preference traces to measurable,
low-level writing
properties (structure, readability, and semantic fit to the job
advertisement), and a further check (Appendix 
C.3
) finds
little room for an unmeasured writer identity on top of those same
features. Little of the effect looks like some irreducible
reaction to a model’s identity as a “brand.” Practically, this is
grounds for cautious optimism: because the mechanism is legible,
mitigation does not require reverse-engineering model internals, only
monitoring a tractable set of surface features.

Limitations.

Four limitations are worth being explicit about: how low absolute
scores in the CV-only condition should be read, an ecological-validity
gap in how CV-job pairings were assigned, what our fixed anonymization
choice does and does not let us say,
and a methodological caveat
specific to the variance-decomposition analysis.

Absolute scores are low in the CV-only condition specifically – mean
scores around 4 for high-fit candidates and around 2 for moderate-fit
candidates, both below the rubric’s own bar for a genuinely qualified
applicant – because it is the sparsest signal we evaluate, a bare CV
with no persuasive framing; it is not a ceiling on how highly any
evaluator scores these candidates. In the combined condition, high-fit
candidates average 
, and GPT-4o-mini alone rates them 
,
squarely in the rubric’s “strong match” band. Our central claim does
not depend on the absolute level in any case: it is a within-candidate
comparison, asking whether the same candidate’s score moves when only
the writer model changes, and that comparison is well-defined
regardless of how strict or lenient an evaluator is on average. What it
does not establish is that the effect generalises to candidates a human
recruiter would independently call excellent; validating our candidate
pool against human judgment is a natural next step we leave to future
work.

Our CVs and job descriptions are each real, but the pairing between
them is not: no candidate in our pool actually applied for the job
their cover letter was generated against. Real applicants self-select
into jobs they already believe themselves a plausible fit for, which
our cosine-similarity-constructed candidate pool does not replicate,
and a writer model generating a letter for a pairing no real applicant
chose may not fully capture how a genuine, motivated application would
be framed. Our results measure model behaviour under a controlled,
researcher-assigned pairing, not necessarily under the self-selected
pairings a real applicant pool would exhibit.

All CVs are anonymized to remove gender markers, with a fixed
gender-neutral name used throughout 
[
9
]
;
we therefore cannot speak to whether gendered names would change
evaluator behaviour, only to the writer-model effect isolated when they
do not vary.

The variance-decomposition analysis of
Appendix 
C.3
 carries two further caveats. A
raw-score variance decomposition and a rank-based fairness metric can
diverge sharply, particularly in noisier evaluation conditions where
scores cluster near decision boundaries, so ranking-level harm to
candidates should not be inferred from a variance audit alone. It also
only controls for each evaluator’s own strictness (a fixed intercept),
not for how widely each evaluator spreads scores; since 
 is
computed on pooled residuals, evaluators with wider score spread
implicitly carry more weight in what counts as explained variance, so
those results are best read as representative of the higher-spread
evaluators rather than uniformly true across all nine.

The specific model hierarchy we report will shift as models are updated
or deprecated, and our primary contribution is therefore methodological:
the framework itself is the durable deliverable.
We recommend that organisations conducting impact assessments of AI
hiring tools incorporate periodic replication of this audit as standard
practice, analogous to the periodic demographic bias audits now required
in several jurisdictions. Future work should examine generalisability
across languages and labour markets, whether instruction-tuned
neutral-writing strategies can mitigate generative preference bias, and
whether mandatory disclosure of AI use in applications changes evaluator
behaviour.

Ethical considerations

This study uses only publicly available CV and job description datasets;
no personal data were collected. All candidate profiles were anonymized
prior to analysis. Models were accessed via public APIs under their
respective terms of service.

The findings could in principle inform strategic model selection by
applicants seeking to game screening systems. We consider this risk
modest: model preferences shift as models are updated, and our primary
contribution is a detection framework. It tracks a moving target; it
does not hand applicants a stable gaming recipe. Surfacing generative
preference bias publicly is more likely to
prompt corrective action by employers and regulators than to enable
durable individual gaming. Publicly identifying which evaluator models
are least biased also creates an accountability mechanism that serves
candidates, employers, and regulators alike.

A potential adverse impact is discouraging AI screening adoption,
including tools that may on balance reduce demographic bias relative to
unassisted human screening. Our findings do not imply AI screening is
worse than human screening overall; they identify a specific, currently
invisible and unregulated bias axis that can be mitigated through
thoughtful evaluator selection and periodic auditing.

4 
Conclusion

We built a reusable auditing framework that isolates generative
preference bias from candidate quality: eleven writer models (nine
API-hosted, two locally hosted) produce cover letters for fifty
candidates across ten job postings, and nine API-hosted evaluator
models score every candidate under three conditions – CV only, cover
letter only, and CV plus cover letter – for 414,000 evaluations in
total (Figure 
1
). Candidate quality is fixed by an
external, evaluator-independent criterion, so every evaluator is judged
against the same ground truth and writer identity is the only thing
that varies within a candidate.

When both applicants and employers use AI in recruitment, the

which
 matters as much as the 
whether
. Four findings
follow from this design. First, a persistent generative preference
hierarchy means that equally qualified candidates face different
screening outcomes depending solely on which language model wrote
their cover letter, a bias invisible to the employer and unrecoverable
for the candidate; locally hosted models, run by those without means
to pay for API access, are systematically disadvantaged regardless of
their underlying licence.
Second, evaluator models differ substantially in how much of this bias
they introduce, giving employers an actionable lever even before the
underlying hierarchy itself can be fixed. Third, this hierarchy has
measurable
competitive consequences: writer-model mismatch alone displaces
qualified candidates at a rate comparable to the noise floor itself.
Fourth, how much writers
lean on stylistic cues, crossed with
how evaluators reward or penalise them, accounts for almost
all of the preference hierarchy, and a further check finds little
unaccounted-for “writer fingerprint” in evaluator scores: the bias
is legible.
As AI-mediated hiring
scales globally, model-of-origin bias warrants the same regulatory
scrutiny as the demographic biases that have dominated the algorithmic
hiring literature.

5 
Methods

To study generative preference bias in the within-AI setting, we need
a framework that (a) controls for candidate quality, so that any
observed differences in hiring outcomes can be attributed to model
choice, (b) separates the effects of writer model
from evaluator model, and (c) is replicable as the model landscape
changes. We design our framework around these requirements, operating
as the three-phase pipeline summarised in Figure 
1

(generation, evaluation, analysis); what follows fills in the
implementation detail behind each phase. We
use two publicly available datasets, one of CVs and one of job
descriptions 
[
21
, 
22
]
, and select ten job
descriptions that range widely in industry and seniority, from Primary
School Teacher to Chief Financial Officer. For each job we curate a pool of fifty candidate
CVs by computing cosine similarity between CV embeddings and the job
description using the all-MiniLM-L6-v2 sentence
encoder 
[
27
]
. The top 25 candidates by
similarity are designated 
high-fit
 candidates; the remaining 25
are 
moderate-fit
 candidates (ranks 26–50 within the pool),
drawn from a middle tier of the distribution.
This is a deliberate design choice: early test runs showed that LLM
evaluators trivially filter out irrelevant profiles. Using candidates
with some relevant background instead ensures the experiment tests
nuanced distinctions; a bottom-tier pool would only test the capacity
to reject obvious noise.

Cosine similarity between a CV and a job description is a coarse proxy
for occupational fit — it can be thrown off by superficial vocabulary
overlap or miss substantive but differently-worded qualifications — but
it has a property none of the nine evaluator models can offer on their
own: it is a single, external, evaluator-independent criterion, so
every evaluator is judged against the exact same high-fit/moderate-fit
boundary (each evaluator’s own
CV-only judgment would draw that line somewhat differently on its own — by 6% to
14% of candidates, Table 
A2
 in
Appendix 
A
). A fixed, model-agnostic criterion is what makes
the cross-evaluator comparisons throughout the rest of the paper
meaningful: “high-fit” refers to the same fifty candidates regardless
of whose scores are being read. Despite being coarse, it also turns out
to track what evaluators themselves perceive as quality well:
Appendix 
A.4
 shows a large, consistent CV-only scoring
gap between the two tiers (

highest inter-evaluator agreement of any condition (

itself and each evaluator’s own independent CV-only ranking (86–94%
of candidates assigned to the same tier either way, per evaluator;
Table 
A2
) — evidence that the stratification
captures a real, substantial, and independently-verified quality
difference.

The one analysis that deliberately departs from this fixed criterion is
the rank-displacement analysis of Section 
2.4
,
which asks a self-referential question — does cover-letter quality
overturn an evaluator’s 
own
 internal ranking of candidates? —
and so needs each evaluator’s own CV-only ranking as its baseline. The
shared cosine-similarity split would not work here: using an external
criterion there would conflate genuine writer-driven displacement with
an evaluator simply disagreeing with that external criterion to begin
with.

Length and structure.

Word count, sentence count, paragraph count, and comma count are literal
surface counts extracted from the letter text. Mean word count ranges
from 288 words (DeepSeek-R1-8B) to 370 words (Gemini 3.5 Flash), with
most models clustering in the 340–370 range, close to the 350–400
word target given in the prompt. Sentence count spans a narrower range,
14.4 (DeepSeek-R1-8B) to 17.9 (Claude Haiku 4.5) sentences per letter.
Paragraph count separates the two Gemini models (6.75 paragraphs, the
fewest of any writer) from GPT-5 and Claude Haiku 4.5 (8.6–8.7, the
most): the Gemini models favour fewer, longer paragraphs, where the
others break similar content into more, shorter ones. Comma count is
the one clear outlier: GPT-5 averages 44.1 commas per letter, close to
double the next-highest writer (DeepSeek V4 Flash, 30.0) and more than
double the lowest (GPT-4o-mini, 18.4): substantially more
syntactically complex, clause-heavy sentences than any other model.
Verbosity and structure are stable per-model traits, quantified
precisely in the tier-adaptation sanity check below: they reflect a
fixed model style.

Language complexity.

Average word length (characters per word) and type-token ratio (TTR, capturing the fraction of words in the letter that are unique: 0 means every word
appears at least twice while 1 means none do) proxy vocabulary richness; Flesch Reading

(roughly: 90–100 simple text, 60–70 plain English understood by most
adults, 30–50 fairly difficult/academic, 0–30 very difficult/dense;
the score can dip slightly negative for extremely dense text). All
eleven writers fall in the 6–21 range on Flesch: dense, formal,
professional prose throughout, well below the “plain English” band.
Within that narrow band, Claude Haiku 4.5 writes both the longest
average words (6.20 characters) and the least readable letters (Flesch

characters) and the most readable letters of the eleven (Flesch

Figure A2: 
Mean pairwise cosine similarity between cover-letter

Within-job similarity (0.68–0.78) is far higher than cross-job
similarity (0.46–0.54) for every writer. This validates the
experimental setup: writers genuinely condition the letter’s content
on the specific job description, which is what the auditing framework
requires for job-to-job and writer-to-writer comparisons to be
meaningful in the first place. GPT-5-mini has the smallest
within/cross-job gap (its output varies least from job to job), while
GPT-5 has the lowest within-job similarity (it preserves the most
letter-to-letter individuality even among candidates applying to the
same job).

Abstract.

Copyright analysis focuses on the similarity between two works. However, generative AI models learn from training data through functional relations beyond similarity.
Training Data Attribution (TDA) estimates the influence of each training instance on a generated output and may serve as a basis for remuneration.
However, prior studies find global influence, where a few training instances rank as the most influential for almost every generation.
Used directly, TDA scores would distribute part of every remuneration to these instances regardless of what was generated.
To address this, we propose a new approach in which TDA scores are decomposed into three levels of influence, namely 
global
 indicating the influence of a training data to a trained model, 
group
 for its influence on a group of generated outputs (that could be clustered together), and 
specific
 for its influence on a single generated output. Our study finds that, across several music generative models and TDA methods, 
global
- and 
group
-level influence is non-negligible in every case.
Based on these observations, we propose the term 
functional dependency
 for the relation of training data to a trained model or to its outputs.
This notion both encompasses and distinguishes dependencies of different scopes, allowing TDA scores to be interpreted as distinct forms of contribution in legal, economic and ethical discussions on remuneration.

In this work, we decompose TDA scores acquired from a model into three levels of dependency, which are 
global
, 
group
 and 
specific
, by subtracting the averaged influence at each level over its corresponding scope.
The 
global
 level is the average of a training instance’s scores over all generated outputs.
It represents the dependency of the model itself on the training instance, rather than that of any specific generated output.
The 
group
 level is the average over each group of generated outputs, where generated outputs are grouped by a criterion such as 
-means clustering with each column of TDA scores as a feature vector after the 
global
 level is subtracted.
This level represents a style as the model itself defines it, shared among generated outputs whose TDA scores follow similar patterns across training instances, rather than a genre or style defined from the data.
Lastly, the 
specific
 level is what remains after the 
global
 and 
group
 levels are removed.
It represents the dependency of one specific generated output on the training instance.
The decomposition thus splits each score into three levels that differ in scope, namely the model’s global behavior, a style within the model and a specific generated output.

2. 
Background

2.1. 
Remuneration for Generative AI and Functional Dependency as Structural Perspective

2.1.1. 
Similarity and Functional Dependency

While copyright infringement focuses on substantial similarity 
(
Lee et al., 2025
)
, generative AI brings new challenges for remuneration as it generates outputs influenced by many training instances rather than direct copying.
Furthermore, substantial similarity is challenging to specify algorithmically 
(
Wei et al., 2025
)
, and it is insufficient for capturing the functional dependencies between training data and generated outputs through generative AI 
(
Brando, 2025
)
.
Prior work on copyright and LLMs distinguishes a 
behavioral
 perspective, which evaluates whether generated outputs are substantially similar to training data, from a 
structural
 perspective, which focuses on how a model is trained 
(
Wei et al., 2025
)
.
Our analysis of functional dependencies is inspired by the structural perspective, since it examines what a trained model depends on rather than the similarity of its outputs.

Legal scholars have proposed that providers of generative AI systems may owe a general payment for training on copyrighted works 
(
Geiger and Iaia, 2024
; 
Geiger, 2024
)
 or a lump sum based on their revenue 
(
Senftleben, 2023
)
.
In recent EU policy discussions, author societies have proposed remuneration for authors when their works have been used and have influenced a generated output 
(
Akritidou, 2026
)
. These discussions also note that the factors establishing such influence of the original works on the output remain to be specified 
(
Akritidou, 2026
)
.
An attribution method that measures the influence of each training instance on a generated output is one way to establish such influence.
Remuneration based on such influence thus requires understanding the functional dependency between training data and generated outputs, which persists in a model trained on the data 
(
Brando, 2025
)
.

Prior work on TDA also supports the view that the influence of training instances is not aligned with their similarity to the generated output, and that some training instances that are similar to the generated output even have a negative impact 
(
Koh and Liang, 2017
; 
Deng et al., 2023
)
.
In image classification, the most influential training instances for a test image are not necessarily of the same class as the test image 
(
Koh and Liang, 2017
)
.
In music generation, the similarity of training instances to a generated output aligns poorly with their influence on average, although the two agree on the most influential training instances, and some training instances that are similar to the generated output have a negative impact 
(
Deng et al., 2023
)
. These findings suggest that training instances relate to generated outputs through functional dependencies beyond similarity.

2.1.2. 
Attribution in Music Generation

Recent work on attribution in music generation has begun to relate a generated output to the musical works behind it.
Generated outputs have been compared with training instances through the similarity of embeddings from a separate audio encoder, with listening tests as a check 
(
Barnett et al., 2024
)
.
This approach captures the aspects of musical similarity that the encoder is sensitive to, rather than the causal influence of training instances on the generated output 
(
Han et al., 2026
)
.
Another approach proposes attribution-based music generative systems, where users select reference songs at generation time and the system attributes the generated output to these selected songs 
(
Morreale et al., 2025
)
.
A royalty framework proposes to apply TDA to a music generative model and to distribute royalties according to the resulting scores 
(
Deng et al., 2023
)
.

2.2. 
Training Data Attribution

2.2.1. 
Influence Estimation and Evaluation

TDA is one possible approach to estimating the functional dependency between training instances and generated outputs, estimating the causal relation between them.
It estimates this dependency as the influence of each training instance on a generated output, through the counterfactual effects that quantify how a generated output would change if a training instance had not been in the training data 
(
Koh and Liang, 2017
; 
Hammoudeh and Lowd, 2024
)
.
Estimating these effects directly requires retraining the model once for each training instance, which is called leave-one-out retraining.
This is infeasible for large training datasets or models due to the computational cost 
(
Koh and Liang, 2017
; 
Choi et al., 2025
)
.
Gradient-based TDA methods estimate the influence of training instances without retraining, by comparing the gradient of the loss on a training instance with the gradient of a generated output 
(
Charpiat et al., 2019
; 
Hanawa et al., 2021
; 
Pruthi et al., 2020
; 
Park et al., 2023
; 
Choe et al., 2025
; 
Hu et al., 2025
)
. As a result of TDA, we obtain one score for every training instance with respect to a specific generated output. The TDA scores are usually verified by comparing them with the causal effects measured by retraining the model after removing the training instances 
(
Park et al., 2023
; 
Deng et al., 2024
)
.
The causal effects measured by removing a single training instance can be noisy and vary across retraining runs 
(
Hammoudeh and Lowd, 2024
; 
Basu et al., 2021
; 
Bae et al., 2024
)
. Evaluations therefore also remove a set of high-scoring training instances at once and retrain 
(
Park et al., 2023
; 
Basu et al., 2021
; 
Bae et al., 2024
)
.
The retrained model is then compared with the model retrained after randomly removing the same number of training instances 
(
Chen et al., 2026
; 
Bae et al., 2024
)
.
Through this comparison, it can be observed whether the high-scoring training instances identified by the TDA method have a larger effect on the model’s behavior than a random set.

2.2.2. 
Global Influence in Training Data Attribution

Several studies on TDA have reported that a certain subset of the training instances tends to keep appearing as influential across almost every generated output, referred to as global influence 
(
Barshan et al., 2020
; 
Hammoudeh and Lowd, 2022
; 
Hammoudeh and Lowd, 2024
)
.
This global influence reflects the model’s global behavior rather than any one generated output 
(
Barshan et al., 2020
; 
Hammoudeh and Lowd, 2022
)
.
In terms of functional dependency, global influence corresponds to the dependency of the model itself on these training instances.
Gradient-based TDA methods usually include dot products of gradients in their influence computation, and prior work attributes global influence to the large gradient norms of certain training instances, which lets their global influence dominate the TDA scores 
(
Hanawa et al., 2021
; 
Hammoudeh and Lowd, 2022
; 
Hammoudeh and Lowd, 2024
)
.
Previous approaches focus on identifying and mitigating the effect of globally influential training instances to obtain more individualized TDA scores for specific generated outputs.
Some replace the dot product of gradients with their cosine similarity or apply normalization to gradients, which mitigates the effect of the gradient norm 
(
Hanawa et al., 2021
; 
Hammoudeh and Lowd, 2022
; 
Akyurek et al., 2022
; 
Xia et al., 2024
; 
Barshan et al., 2020
; 
Choe et al., 2025
)
.
Other methods find the gradient vector directions that many generated outputs have in common and use them to re-weight scores 
(
Chang et al., 2025
)
.
These findings suggest that TDA scores may mix different levels of dependency, such as the dependency of the model itself and that of a specific generated output. Using TDA scores as a basis for remuneration therefore requires decomposing them into different levels of influence, each representing one scope of dependency. This supports transparency about which dependency the remuneration reflects.

A diffusion-based model generates an output over iterative denoising steps which lead to the attribution of its generated output changing with the timestep

For example, the prompt “folk, singersongwriter. instruments: classicalguitar.” has the genre folk.
We draw 8 prompts for each of 12 genres and generate one output from each prompt with each of 
 random seeds.

6. 
Conclusion

We proposed a decomposition of TDA scores to support the analysis of what a remuneration based on each level would reflect.
Across four music generative models and six TDA methods and three variants, the scores contain a 
global
 and a 
group
 level.
In the deletion test, each level has an effect on the model’s behavior within its scope.
This means that a single TDA score combines influence on the model itself, on a group of generated outputs and on a single generated output. The 
global
 level corresponds to the dependency of the model itself, which is closer in scope to a general payment for training on copyrighted works 
(
Geiger and Iaia, 2024
)
 than to a remuneration for any one specific generated output.
The 
group
 level is formed by the model and does not coincide with genre or simple audio features.
However, we do not identify what the TDA score clusters correspond to, and identifying what they represent remains future work.
The 
specific
 level changes with the criterion that defines the 
group
 level, so a remuneration based on it depends on that criterion.

Our work lays the groundwork for interpreting each level as contribution, which requires further discussion that involves legal, economic and ethical perspectives.
With our work, we aim to bridge the legal and socio-economic research on copyright and attribution with technical estimates of the influence of training data at each scope of dependency.
By separating the levels, our decomposition turns a single TDA score into distinct forms of influence that each of these discussions can examine.
This makes TDA a more transparent basis for remuneration in generative AI.

A.1.

Table
 
7
 lists the fine-tuning recipe.
All four models, two sizes each of MusicGen 
(
Copet et al., 2023
)
 and InspireMusic 
(
Zhang et al., 2025
)
, are fully fine-tuned on the same 30,000 ten-second segments with the same optimizer, schedule, effective batch size and number of steps.
MusicGen-L and InspireMusic-1.5B split each batch of 16 into smaller batches only to fit in memory, steps are accumulated to match as 16.
Each model is fine-tuned with three seeds, and the seed also fixes the data order.
For the deletion test, the removed training instances are taken out of this order without reshuffling, so the full fine-tuning, the random deletion and the targeted deletion with the same seed share the order of the remaining instances.

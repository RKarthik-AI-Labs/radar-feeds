Abstract

When user queries are incomplete, large language models should ask targeted questions before committing to an answer, a capability we call proactive elicitation, yet evaluation on real user-assistant queries across seven LLMs (8B to frontier) shows that fewer than 11% of underspecified queries trigger a follow-up question. To benchmark and train this capability, we introduce VagueBench, where each seed query is annotated with structured intent and detail units then degraded into intent-vague and fully-vague variants via controlled removal of linguistic cues, yielding known hidden information sets for evaluating both when and what to ask. We propose DialogGRPO, an offline reinforcement learning method that organizes multi-turn elicitation as a dialogue-state directed acyclic graph, where candidate questions at each node receive dense turn-level rewards combining immediate information gain with lookahead value through group-relative comparison, enabling fine-grained credit assignment without runtime simulation. DialogGRPO elicits missing information on over 88% of vague queries with substantial gains in both recovery efficiency and answer coverage, without over-asking on complete queries, outperforms prompting and test-time-scaling baselines, and generalizes zero-shot to MultiWOZ 2.4 and AmbigQA.

1 
Introduction

Today’s large language models (LLMs) default to 
answering
, not 
asking
. When user queries are incomplete or underspecified, models silently infer unstated intent and constraints instead of asking targeted questions to resolve the gap (Figure 
1
). Yet real-world user queries are rarely self-contained: information gaps are pervasive in natural human communication 
(
Taylor, 1968
)
, and users routinely issue underspecified queries, overestimating the model’s ability to infer unstated needs: task accuracy drops by 39% when instructions are fragmented 
(
Laban et al., 2025
)
, and medical diagnostic accuracy plummets from 94.9% to 34.5% when symptoms are underspecified
(
Bean et al., 2026
)
.

Our analysis of 5,000 real-world queries from a commercial AI assistant further quantifies this gap: 
over half of user queries lack critical information, yet models from 8B to over 120B ask clarification in fewer than 11% of such cases
, with no clear improvement from scaling.
These findings motivate 
proactive elicitation
: identifying missing information and asking targeted questions 
before
 committing to an answer.

This capability is challenging because the information gap arises from both sides of the conversation. On the 
user side
, users often leave critical constraints implicit, assuming the model shares context it does not have, a cognitive bias known as the 
curse of knowledge
 
(
Camerer et al., 1989
)
.
On the 
model side
, post-training pipelines dominated by single-turn supervision and rewards for direct answers implicitly penalize pausing to ask. We formalize this gap at two levels: 
(1) Intent ambiguity
, where the user’s overarching goal is unclear—e.g., whether “help me work on this text” means translation or summarization; and 
(2) Detail absence
, where the goal is clear but critical parameters are missing—e.g., budget or use case in “recommend a laptop”. These two levels require different elicitation strategies and evaluation criteria.

Recent work has explored LLM clarification behavior 
(
Shaikh et al., 2025
; 
Zhang et al., 2025
; 
Wu et al., 2025
; 
Andukuri et al., 2024
)
, but remains limited along three axes. 
Scope:
 prior work mostly targets 
disambiguation
, resolving queries with multiple valid interpretations 
(
Min et al., 2020
; 
Zhang et al., 2024
)
, rather than the broader case where essential information was 
never expressed
 at all. 
Data:
 benchmarks are confined to narrow domains 
(
Gan et al., 2024
)
 or synthetic ambiguity 
(
Luo et al., 2025
)
, with no training-ready resource grounded in real conversations. 
Training:
 methods rely on online user simulators or trajectory-level feedback 
(
Wu et al., 2025
; 
Huang et al., 2025
; 
Andukuri et al., 2024
; 
Zhang et al., 2025
)
, limiting fine-grained, per-step credit assignment.

To address these gaps, we propose a unified framework for evaluating and training proactive elicitation, grounded in real user–model conversations.
On the evaluation side, we introduce 
VagueBench
, a benchmark built from real open-domain queries through 
controlled degradation
: intent-bearing linguistic cues are systematically weakened and task details removed, yielding vague variants whose hidden information set is precisely known. A simulated user holds the complete query and reveals a hidden information unit only when the model asks a semantically matching question, providing a controlled, reproducible testbed for multi-turn elicitation. We measure three complementary aspects: 
decision appropriateness
, whether the model asks rather than prematurely answers; 
elicitation efficiency
, the number of hidden information units recovered per turn; and 
answer coverage
, the F1 between the final response and the gold information set.
On the training side, we propose 
DialogGRPO
, an offline reinforcement learning framework that models multi-turn elicitation as a dialogue-state directed acyclic graph (DAG). Each node groups alternative elicitation actions for local GRPO 
(
Shao et al., 2024
)
 comparison, and each edge receives a dense reward combining immediate information gain with long-term lookahead value, enabling the model to learn 
when
 and 
what
 to ask without the prohibitive cost of runtime user simulation.

Our contributions are threefold:

•

Benchmark.
 We introduce VagueBench, a benchmark built
from real open-domain queries with controlled vagueness and known
hidden information for evaluating proactive elicitation capabilities
of LLMs.

•

Method.
 We propose DialogGRPO, which models multi-turn elicitation as a dialogue-state DAG and applies group-relative advantage estimation at each state for dense offline credit assignment.

•

Findings.
 Evaluation on VagueBench across open-source and
closed-source LLMs (8B to frontier) reveals systematic elicitation
suppression at all scales. DialogGRPO outperforms prompt-engineering and test-time-scaling baselines on all metrics, and zero-shot transfers to MultiWOZ 2.4 and AmbigQA.

2 
Related Work

2.1 
Proactive Elicitation

Clarification and mixed-initiative interaction are well studied in conversational search and task-oriented dialogue
(
Budzianowski et al., 2018
; 
Ye et al., 2022
; 
Gan et al., 2024
)
, where systems ask follow-up questions to resolve facets, slots, or preferences 
(
Aliannejadi et al., 2019
; 
Aliannejadi et al., 2020
)
. For LLM assistants, the challenge is broader: open-ended requests often omit intent or constraints, yet instruction-tuned models tend to answer directly and fill gaps by assumption 
(
Yang et al., 2025
; 
Laban et al., 2025
)
. Recent LLM work explores clarification via prompting, grounding analysis, and collaborative agents 
(
Shaikh et al., 2025
; 
Wu et al., 2025
)
, but mainly targets multiple valid interpretations. We instead study open-domain elicitation of unspoken intent and task details.

2.2 
Vagueness Benchmarks

Existing benchmarks capture different forms of underspecification. Qulac and ClariQ evaluate clarification in conversational search 
(
Aliannejadi et al., 2019
; 
Aliannejadi et al., 2020
)
; AmbigQA and related datasets target questions with multiple valid interpretations 
(
Min et al., 2020
; 
Zhang et al., 2024
)
; recent LLM benchmarks further test clarification in task-oriented dialogue, tool use, or synthetic ambiguity settings 
(
Zhang et al., 2024
; 
Gan et al., 2024
; 
Luo et al., 2025
)
. However, they assume a predefined domain, a fixed slot schema, or
a small set of alternative interpretations, providing limited coverage
of real open-domain vagueness where intent and task details may have
never been stated. VagueBench fills this gap with real assistant queries, unrestricted intents, and a multi-turn evaluation protocol.

2.3 
Training Elicitation Policies

Prior methods improve clarification behavior through prompting, supervised fine-tuning, preference learning, simulated users, or downstream task rewards 
(
Chen et al., 2025
; 
Andukuri et al., 2024
; 
Zhang et al., 2025
; 
Huang et al., 2025
)
. Table 
1
 compares these methods by whether they target open-domain interactions, use real user queries, avoid online user simulation, and provide turn-level rewards
(
Wu et al., 2025
; 
Shani et al., 2024
)
. While effective, existing approaches often depend on synthetic interactions, domain-specific settings, or coarse dialogue-level feedback. DialogGRPO instead precomputes a dialogue-state DAG and rewards each asking turn using both acquired information and downstream answer value.

4 
DialogGRPO

Training proactive elicitation faces two coupled challenges.
First, 
sparse credit assignment
: multi-turn rewards arrive only at the trajectory’s end, leaving each intermediate question without direct feedback on whether it was worth asking.
Second, 
simulation cost
: on-policy rollouts require invoking an LLM-based user simulator at every gradient step, which is prohibitively expensive when each dialogue spans multiple turns.

DialogGRPO addresses both through a unified offline pipeline organized in three stages (Figure 
3
).
We first construct a 
dialogue state DAG
 (§
4.1
) that maps out candidate elicitation questions and their consequences using an external LLM, so that the outgoing edges of any node form a natural GRPO comparison group.
We then assign each edge a 
turn-level reward
 (§
4.2
) that combines immediate information gain with lookahead value estimated from the pre-built graph, converting the single end-of-trajectory signal into dense per-action feedback.
Finally, we train the policy via 
advantage-weighted learning

(§
4.3
) over (prompt, candidate, advantage) tuples
extracted from the DAG, requiring no runtime simulation.

Across datasets.

6 
Conclusion

We introduce VagueBench, a benchmark exposing that elicitation suppression in LLMs persists across model families and scales, and DialogGRPO, an offline RL method that recovers this latent ability via dialogue-state DAG construction and dense turn-level credit assignment. DialogGRPO teaches models to ask better questions, not merely more, reaching 88–91% Ask% on vague queries while preserving calibration on complete ones, and generalizes zero-shot to MultiWOZ 2.4 and AmbigQA.

Limitations

VagueBench is built from a single commercial assistant’s traffic, which limits its coverage of conversational scenarios, languages, and modalities.
Our evaluation relies on a simulated user that cooperatively releases information on matching questions, abstracting away real human behaviors such as partial answers, sudden topic switches, noise, or refusal to elaborate, so the reported gains likely represent an upper bound on what can be achieved with cooperative interlocutors.
Open domain dialogue spaces are unbounded, so the DAG covers only a sparse set of trajectories. Local contrastive coverage and state merging reduce this gap, but interactions outside the training distribution remain a limitation.
Large preference spaces also make exhaustive questioning impractical. In shopping, a few targeted questions can narrow the options, but preferences may emerge or change during the interaction. VagueBench fixes the hidden preference set for reproducibility and does not capture this dynamic. Asking frequency is controlled by reward hyperparameters and should be calibrated to application needs.

Ethical Considerations

We have conducted this work with a strong commitment to ethical and responsible research practices. The raw queries used to construct VagueBench originate from a commercial AI assistant that offers tiered data-sharing preferences, only interactions from users who explicitly opted into research use were included. All queries were stripped of personally identifiable information prior to our access, and a dedicated unsafe-content filter removed harmful or sensitive material during the rule-based filtering stage (§
3.1
). The released benchmark contains only post-filtering, annotation-enriched seeds with no metadata linking back to individual sessions. For the human validation study, two graduate student annotators were recruited and compensated at $7.50 USD per hour; given the flexibility of the remote task and the economic context of the participants’ residential region, this rate exceeds the local average hourly wage, ensuring fair remuneration. Participation was voluntary, and annotators provided informed consent for the use of their labels. Proactive elicitation is inherently dual-use: it reduces unfounded assumptions and prevents costly errors in high-stakes domains, but could also be steered into over-soliciting personal information unnecessary for the task. Our reward formulation mitigates this by crediting only information units causally required for the final answer; nevertheless, we recommend that production deployments constrain what the model may solicit, audit questions touching sensitive attributes, and give users a clear option to decline. We also note that VagueBench covers English-only queries from a single platform, limiting its linguistic and cultural generalizability. All data released are for research purposes and recommend that any production use be paired with appropriate guardrails.

Appendix C 
Simulated User

This appendix specifies the simulated user 
 
(
Shi et al., 2019
; 
Hong et al., 2023
; 
Faltings et al., 2023
; 
Hu et al., 2023
)
introduced in §
3.3
. 
 is the core component of the VagueBench evaluation protocol: it plays the role of a real user who holds the hidden information and decides, on each turn, which units to release in response to the model’s question. Two properties anchor its design: 
consistency
—identical questions elicit responses of the same kind—and 
minimal disclosure
—only precisely-matched units are released, with no volunteered extras.

C.1 
Behaviour Rules

The behaviour of 
 is governed by five rules over the hidden intent set 
 and detail set 
.

R1. Information release.

When the model’s question semantically matches the trigger keywords of some hidden intent 
 or detail 
, 
 releases that unit in short, natural language. For example, the question 
“Which area would you like to dine in?”
 matches the trigger of 
 and 
 replies 
“near University Avenue in downtown Palo Alto”
; an intent-side match is symmetric, e.g. 
“Are you looking for restaurant recommendations or something else?”
 matches the 
recommend_restaurant
 intent and 
 replies 
“yes, recommend a few restaurants”
. Matching is judged at the semantic level by a strong LLM rather than by exact keyword overlap, so phrasings such as 
“roughly how much do you want to spend?”
 still match a budget trigger listing 
budget / price / spending
.

R2. Deflection.

If the question matches no hidden unit, 
 returns a deflective reply drawn at random from a predefined pool: 
“I haven’t decided yet”
, 
“I’m not sure about that”
, 
“you decide for me”
, 
“no particular preference”
, and similar. Deflections are short, natural, and carry no cues that the model could use to infer the hidden set.

R3. No proactive disclosure.

 never volunteers a unit that was not asked about. Questions that approach several units but precisely match only one—for example 
“any requirements for your meal?”
 matching only 
scenario
—release only the matched unit, regardless of how close the others appear.

R4. Release once.

Once released, a unit is removed from 
 or 
. Subsequent questions that re-hit the same unit are answered with 
“I mentioned that earlier”
 or by repeating the previous value; no new credit is granted at evaluation.

R5. Multi-unit matches.

When a single question matches multiple hidden units—for example 
“which city and what occasion?”
 hitting both 
city
 and 
scenario
—
 releases all matched units in one reply. This rule ensures that elicitation efficiency is not artificially deflated by simulator-side fragmentation.

C.2 
Direct-Answer Behaviour

If the model commits to a final answer at the initial turn without asking, the dialogue terminates immediately and the evaluation proceeds without further simulator input. On information-complete queries this is the expected behaviour and requires no simulator participation. On Intent-Vague and Fully-Vague queries, the absence of a question means the model answered under missing information, and the resulting answer is naturally penalised by Cov. F1, which compares the response against the gold information set. Whether a model turn counts as 
ask
 or 
answer
 is judged by the LLM-based behaviour judge (Prompt 3 in Appendix 
J
).

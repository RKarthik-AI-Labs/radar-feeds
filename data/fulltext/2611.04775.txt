Abstract.

Recommender systems are ubiquitous on the Web, making
understandable explanations essential for users.
Although large language models (LLMs) increasingly
generate natural-language justifications, evaluating
their factual grounding and personalized relevance
remains challenging. Statement-level ranking offers
an alternative by selecting concise, review-derived
explanations, enabling fine-grained and reproducible
evaluation using established ranking metrics.
However, existing statement ranking methods largely rely on collaborative
representations, while semantic enhancements often fail
to outperform simple user-history popularity baselines.
Moreover, directly adapting language models for
personalized statement ranking remains largely unexplored.
In this work, we introduce 
LMStaR
 (Language Models for
Statement Ranking), a fully text-based framework that
formulates personalized explanation ranking as dense
retrieval. A query encoder represents the user’s
interaction history and target-item metadata, while
a statement encoder maps candidate
explanations into a shared embedding space.
Both encoders are jointly adapted through multi-positive
contrastive learning to learn personalized relevance.
Experiments on Amazon Beauty, Amazon Toys, and Yelp
show that 
LMStaR
 achieves the highest NDCG@1
across all three domains, outperforming 
CLER

by 1.06–8.25 points (+11.8–65.9%).
It also leads top-five ranking on both Amazon datasets,
although 
CLER
 remains stronger on Yelp.
Task-specific adaptation improves all five evaluated
backbones and transfers across domains without
target-domain fine-tuning. Counterfactual analyses
further reveal the importance of user-specific histories. These findings highlight the
potential of task-adapted language models for
personalized explanation ranking.
Code, data, and supplementary materials are available at

https://github.com/BenKabongo25/language-models-statement-ranking
.

1. 
Introduction

Recommender systems play a central role on Web platforms,
helping users discover products, services, and content.
As increasingly sophisticated models underpin these
recommendations, providing understandable justifications
is important both for users assessing suggested items
and for designers seeking to diagnose system
behavior 
(
Zhang et al., 2020
)
.
Textual explanations are particularly appealing because
they convey justifications in natural, human-understandable
language. Existing approaches have evolved from predefined
templates 
(
Li et al., 2020
)
 to neural
generation 
(
Li et al., 2021b
)
 and, more recently,
LLM-based generation 
(
Li et al., 2023c
; 
Ma et al., 2024a
)
.
However, these generative methods may produce generic
or unsupported rationales 
(
Kabongo and Guigue, 2026
)
.
Evaluating their factual grounding with semantic similarity
metrics 
(
Zhang* et al., 2020
)
 or LLM-based
judges 
(
Sheng et al., 2025
)
, as well as assessing
the relevance of individual explanatory factors, remains
challenging.
Such concerns extend beyond recommendation, motivating
evidence-grounded approaches in question
answering 
(
Stolfo, 2024
)
 and retrieval-based
tool selection in LLM agents 
(
Qin et al., 2025
)
.
In explainable recommendation, a complementary paradigm
is 
statement-level ranking
: rather than generating
free-form explanations, approaches such as

EXTRA
 
(
Li et al., 2021a
)
 and

StaR
 
(
Kabongo et al., 2026
)
 rank concise,
review-derived explanatory statements and return the
top-
 for each user–item interaction.
This formulation enables individual explanatory aspects
to be prioritized and supports reproducible evaluation
using established ranking metrics 
(
Järvelin and Kekäläinen, 2002
)
.

Yet ranking explanations does not inherently solve their
personalization. Under 
item-level
 evaluation, candidates
are restricted to the target item’s statements, making
the challenge to identify which aspects best match a
particular user. Prior work shows that simple user-history
popularity signals remain competitive with sophisticated
rankers in this setting 
(
Kabongo et al., 2026
)
.
Existing approaches range from neighborhood-based
collaborative filtering and factorization methods 
(
Li et al., 2021a
)
 to

BPER
 and its variants 
(
Li et al., 2023b
)
. To leverage textual semantics,

BPER+
 
(
Li et al., 2023b
)
 incorporates BERT representations, while

SE-BPER
 and 
SE-BPER+
 exploit contextualized
statement embeddings to initialize user, item, and
statement factors 
(
Xu et al., 2022
)
.
These approaches retain an identifier-based collaborative
formulation, predominantly optimized with Bayesian
Personalized Ranking 
(
Rendle et al., 2012
)
.
More recently, 
CLER
 
(
Escarda-Fernández et al., 2026
)

introduces contrastive learning through an NT-BXent
objective, substantially improving collaborative ranking
without explicitly modeling textual semantics.
While effective, these approaches leave the direct,
task-specific alignment of textual user histories and
candidate explanations largely unexplored.

Meanwhile, neural information retrieval has progressed
from dual-encoder architectures such as
Sentence-BERT 
(
Reimers and Gurevych, 2019
)
 and
DPR 
(
Karpukhin et al., 2020
)
 to adapting large
language models as text encoders and rankers.
Approaches such as RepLLaMA 
(
Ma et al., 2024b
)
, LLM2Vec
(
BehnamGhader et al., 2024
)
,
and Qwen3-Embedding 
(
Zhang et al., 2025
)
 demonstrate the effectiveness
of task-adapted language-model representations for
retrieval.
By independently encoding queries and documents,
bi-encoders enable similarity-based ranking with
precomputable candidate representations, while contrastive
learning aligns embeddings with task-specific relevance.
Related advances in recommendation, including
UniSRec 
(
Hou et al., 2022
)
 and RecFormer 
(
Li et al., 2023a
)
, exploit textual
item representations and interaction sequences.
These advances motivate exploring language models
as explanation rankers within a fully text-based
framework for personalized statement ranking.

In this work, we introduce 
L
anguage 
M
odels for 
Sta
tement

R
anking (
LMStaR
), a fully text-based framework that
formulates personalized explanation ranking as a dense
retrieval problem. To our knowledge, 
LMStaR
 is
the first approach to jointly adapt language-model
encoders for personalized statement ranking.
Rather than learning representations tied to user and
item identifiers, our approach directly models the
textual context of each user–item interaction.
Specifically, a query encoder represents the user’s
interaction history and target-item metadata, while
a separate statement encoder independently maps
candidate explanations into a shared embedding space.
Both encoders are jointly optimized using a multi-positive
contrastive objective, contrasting relevant statements
against item-level negatives to learn personalized relevance.

We construct three statement-ranking datasets from user
reviews in Amazon Beauty, Amazon Toys 
(
Ni et al., 2019
)
,
and Yelp, using a refined 
StaR

pipeline 
(
Kabongo et al., 2026
)
 that emphasizes statements
describing matches between user preferences and item
characteristics, and strengthens reranker-based verification
of statement cluster merges.
We compare against popularity-based 
(
Kabongo et al., 2026
)
,
neighborhood-based 
(
Li et al., 2021a
)
,
factorization 
(
Li et al., 2021a
; 
Li et al., 2023b
)
,
semantic-enhanced 
(
Li et al., 2023b
; 
Xu et al., 2022
)
,
contrastive collaborative 
(
Escarda-Fernández et al., 2026
)
,
and zero-shot retrieval 
(
Zhang et al., 2025
)
 baselines.
We further investigate five pretrained backbones,
LoRA 
(
Hu et al., 2022
)
 versus full fine-tuning,
and cross-domain transfer without target-domain adaptation.
To characterize the role of user context, we conduct
controlled analyses of history quantity and selection,
performance across history-length groups, and
counterfactual query perturbations.

Our results show that 
LMStaR
 achieves the
highest NDCG@1 across all three domains, outperforming

CLER
 by 3.50 (+31.2%), 8.25 (+65.9%),
and 1.06 (+11.8%) points on Beauty, Toys, and Yelp,
respectively. At 
, it also improves NDCG by
2.66 (+13.4%) and 7.79 (+34.2%) points on Beauty
and Toys, while trailing 
CLER
 by 1.10
(
%) points on Yelp.
Beyond overall effectiveness, task-specific adaptation
improves all evaluated backbones across domains,
with smaller adapted models outperforming larger
zero-shot alternatives. LoRA further exceeds full
fine-tuning by 0.84–2.66 NDCG@5 points across the
six tested configurations.
These gains also transfer across domains: without
target-domain fine-tuning, adapted models outperform
zero-shot retrieval and conventional ranking baselines
in all six transfer settings.
Finally, history analyses reveal substantial gains from
the first few recent interactions, while replacing the
user’s history with another user’s reduces NDCG@5 points across the three domains.
In contrast, perturbing target-item metadata has a
comparatively limited effect, highlighting the importance
of user-specific context under item-level ranking.

Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We introduce 
LMStaR
, a fully text-based
dual-encoder framework that formulates personalized
statement ranking as dense retrieval and jointly
adapts language-model representations through
multi-positive contrastive learning.

•

We demonstrate the effectiveness and robustness of

LMStaR
 across three recommendation domains,
examining ranking performance against established
baselines, five pretrained backbones, fine-tuning
strategies, and cross-domain transferability.

•

We systematically investigate the effects of user
history quantity, selection, and length, together
with counterfactual query perturbations, revealing
the importance of user-specific interaction histories
and the comparatively limited influence of target-item
metadata under item-level ranking.

We evaluate item-level statement ranking using Precision@

4.1.4. 
Model Details

We evaluate several pretrained language-model backbones for

4.3.4. 
Counterfactual Query Analysis

Specifically, we remove the user history (a) or target-item
metadata (b), replace the target item with one from the same
category (c) or a random item (d), or substitute another user’s
history matched on the number of past interactions (e).
Probes (a) and (b) introduce empty fields unseen during training,
whereas (c)–(e) preserve valid input fields.

LMStaR
 strongly relies on user-specific
interaction histories for personalized statement ranking.

Replacing the user’s history with that of another user (e) reduces NDCG@5 by 6.73 points (30.0%) on Beauty,
13.20 points (43.2%) on Toys, and 5.65 points (36.7%) on Yelp.
Removing the history entirely (a) produces similarly large
degradations of 6.09, 12.30, and 4.84 points, respectively,
although this configuration is unseen during training.
Notably, with another user’s history (e), 
LMStaR
 falls
below 
UserPop
 and its own zero-shot backbone on all
three datasets (Table 
1
).

Target-item metadata has a comparatively limited
influence under item-level ranking.

In contrast, replacing the target item with a random item (d)
reduces NDCG@5 by only 0.97 points (4.3%) on Beauty,
0.41 points (1.3%) on Toys, and 0.36 points (2.3%) on Yelp.
The much smaller sensitivity to item modifications may
partly reflect the item-level evaluation setting, where
candidate statements are already restricted to the
actual target item.

5. 
Conclusion

We introduced 
LMStaR
, a text-based dual-encoder
framework that ranks explanatory statements using a query
combining user history and target-item metadata, with both
encoders adapted through LoRA and multi-positive contrastive
learning. On Beauty, Toys, and Yelp, 
LMStaR
 achieves
the highest NDCG@1, and leads the top-five metrics on both
Amazon datasets, while 
CLER
 remains stronger at

 on Yelp. Task-specific adaptation improves all five
evaluated backbones, with smaller adapted models outperforming
larger zero-shot alternatives; LoRA also surpasses full
fine-tuning for the two tested backbones. Adaptation transfers
across domains without target-domain fine-tuning.
History analyses reveal substantial gains from a few recent
interactions and, critically, a strong dependence on the
user’s own history. Together, these findings establish
task-adapted language-model representations as a promising
approach to personalized explanation ranking.

6. 
Discussion

The contrast between first-rank relevance and top-five
coverage on Yelp raises an important question: a single
query vector may struggle to represent multiple explanatory
aspects, while 
CLER
’s collaborative signals may
provide complementary evidence. Neither mechanism is
established by our experiments, but both motivate hybrid
or multi-vector ranking approaches. Counterfactual probes
also show much greater sensitivity to user-history
substitutions than to changes in the target-item description.
This does not imply that item information is irrelevant:
item-level evaluation already restricts candidates to
the true target item, potentially reducing the marginal
contribution of its metadata.
The evaluation also has limitations. Binary relevance
is derived from automatically extracted and clustered
review statements; unobserved statements may still be
relevant, and ranking metrics do not establish perceived
usefulness or faithfulness. Dataset construction relies
on Qwen-family models, potentially introducing shared
biases, although gains with EmbeddingGemma show that
adaptation is not restricted to Qwen backbones.
Importantly, item-level ranking targets
personalization more directly than global
retrieval, but candidate pools may
include statements from held-out reviews unavailable at
recommendation time, and test items are restricted to
those observed during training. More realistic evaluations
should consider temporally available candidate pools,
sparsely reviewed or unseen items, and human assessments
of explanation quality.

Appendix A 
Datasets

We conduct experiments on three real-world recommendation datasets: 
Amazon Beauty
 (
Beauty
), 
Amazon Toys
 (
Toys
), and 
Yelp
. We first describe the construction of statement-level datasets from user reviews through statement extraction and clustering, followed by filtering and data splitting. Table 
5
 summarizes the resulting dataset statistics.

Dataset Construction.

We build upon the statement extraction and clustering pipeline introduced by 
StaR
 
(
Kabongo et al., 2026
)
, with two refinements aimed at improving the explanatory relevance and semantic consistency of the resulting statements.
First, we revise the extraction prompt to explicitly identify statements that explain the match between a user’s preferences and an item’s characteristics, rather than merely describing item attributes. Second, we strengthen the final clustering stage with reranker-based verification to reduce erroneous merges between semantically distinct clusters. Specifically, we select 
 representative statements from each cluster to capture its semantic diversity in the embedding space. For each pair of candidate clusters, we evaluate all cross-cluster pairs of representatives and merge the clusters only if the reranker identifies every pair as semantically equivalent.
We use Qwen3.8-27B 
(
Qwen Team, 2026
)

(
Zhang et al., 2025
)
4

 for semantic representations, and Qwen3-Reranker-0.6B

)

Filtering and Data Splitting.

After statement extraction and clustering, we discard interactions without valid explanatory statements and apply iterative 
-core filtering, retaining only users and items with at least five interactions. We then perform a user-wise chronological split, holding out each user’s most recent interaction for testing and the second most recent for validation. All earlier interactions form the training set. To ensure comparability with collaborative filtering baselines, we exclude test interactions involving items absent from the training set, thereby avoiding item cold-start evaluation. All methods are evaluated using the same data splits and item-level candidate statement sets.

Appendix D 
Implementation Details

D.1. 
LMStaR Training Configuration

Unless otherwise specified, we use the same training
configuration across backbones and datasets, as summarized
in Table 
7
.
The query and statement encoders are initialized as two
independent copies of the same pretrained backbone and
adapted using LoRA on the query, key, value, and output
attention projections.
We extract the last non-padding token representation and
apply 
 normalization to both embeddings.
The query includes the task instruction, target-item
metadata, and the user’s interaction history, ordered from
most recent to oldest and including all available statements
for each interaction. We retain the longest history prefix
fitting the query token budget, including the target-item
metadata. Statement inputs are limited to 256 tokens.
Training uses a multi-positive InfoNCE objective with
32 uniformly sampled item-level negatives per interaction.
Checkpoints are evaluated every 50 optimization steps and
selected according to validation NDCG@5, with early stopping
after five evaluations without improvement.

D.2. 
Backbone-Specific Settings

We evaluate five pretrained models:
EmbeddingGemma-300M 
(
Vera et al., 2025
)
, Qwen3-0.6B 
(
Yang et al., 2025
)
, and Qwen3-Embedding (0.6B, 4B, 8B) 
(
Zhang et al., 2025
)
.
In particular, EmbeddingGemma-300M uses bidirectional attention, its
native query and statement prefixes
(
task: search result | query:
 and

title: none | text:
), and a 2,048-token query
limit corresponding to its maximum context length.
The Qwen3-based models use causal attention and a
4,096-token query limit.
For the 4B and 8B backbones, the physical batch size is
reduced from 128 to 64 and gradient accumulation is
increased from two to four steps, preserving an effective

All experiments use NVIDIA H200 GPUs (141 GB each),

D.3. 
Baselines

We follow the original baseline implementations, preserving
their architectures, objectives, and training procedures
wherever possible. The main adaptation concerns negative
sampling, which we align with our item-level ranking setting:
for methods requiring negative statements, these are sampled
exclusively from the target item’s statement pool, excluding
positives associated with the corresponding user–item pair.
Only statements observed in the training split are eligible
as training negatives. At evaluation time, all methods rank
the same complete set of candidate statements associated
with the target item.
Each baseline is evaluated on every dataset using three
random seeds (40, 41, and 42), and we report the mean
performance across these runs. Statistical baselines are
executed on CPUs, purely collaborative models (including

CLER
) on a single NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU, and
semantics-enhanced models on a single NVIDIA H200 GPU.
Detailed implementation settings and hyperparameter
configurations are provided in the accompanying code.

Appendix E 
Extended Related Work

Our work lies at the intersection of text-based explainable recommendation and neural retrieval. We review existing approaches to generating and ranking explanations and advances in dense representation learning.

Text-based Explainable Recommendation

Text-based explainable recommendation has evolved from template-based methods 
(
Li et al., 2020
)
 to neural generation using RNNs 
(
Dong et al., 2017
; 
Li et al., 2017
)
, Transformers 
(
Li et al., 2021b
)
, and more recently pretrained language models and LLMs 
(
Li et al., 2023c
; 
Ma et al., 2024a
; 
Li et al., 2025
)
. Despite advances in fluency, evaluation remains challenging: lexical metrics reward surface overlap 
(
Papineni et al., 2002
; 
Lin, 2004
)
, semantic metrics do not ensure factual consistency 
(
Zhang* et al., 2020
; 
Sellam et al., 2020
; 
Kabongo and Guigue, 2026
)
, and LLM-based judges can be biased and difficult to reproduce 
(
Fu et al., 2023
; 
Sheng et al., 2025
)
. Moreover, paragraph-level evaluation obscures the relevance of individual explanatory factors.
To address these limitations, prior work 
(
Li et al., 2021a
; 
Kabongo et al., 2026
)
 shifts from free-form generation to ranking short explanatory passages, called 
statements
, selecting the top-
 candidates as explanations. This enables standardized evaluation using established ranking metrics 
(
Järvelin and Kekäläinen, 2002
)
. Meaningful evaluation further requires statements to be explanatory, atomic, and semantically unique 
(
Kabongo et al., 2026
)
.

Statement Ranking Methods

Existing approaches range from statistical baselines to collaborative and semantically enhanced models. Popularity baselines 
(
Kabongo et al., 2026
)
 rank statements by their frequency in user histories (
UserPop
), item histories (
ItemPop
), or the training corpus (
GlobalPop
). Collaborative approaches exploit user–item–statement interactions: 
RUCF
 and 
RICF
 aggregate evidence from Jaccard-based neighborhoods, while 
CD
 and 
PITF
 rely on ternary and pairwise latent factorization, respectively 
(
Li et al., 2021a
)
. 
BPER
 
(
Li et al., 2023b
)
 combines separately learned user–statement and item–statement scores.
To incorporate textual semantics, 
BPER+
 
(
Li et al., 2023b
)
 combines latent factors with BERT 
(
Devlin et al., 2019
)
 embeddings, whereas 
SE-BPER
 and 
SE-BPER+
 initialize latent factors from contextualized statement representations, with the latter introducing task-specific fine-tuning 
(
Xu et al., 2022
)
. While these models rely on Bayesian Personalized Ranking (BPR) objective 
(
Rendle et al., 2012
)
, 
CLER
 
(
Escarda-Fernández et al., 2026
)
 adopts a contrastive NT-BXent loss while retaining ID-based representations.

Explanation ranking distinguishes 
global-level
 ranking over all statements from 
item-level
 ranking restricted to the target item’s statements 
(
Kabongo et al., 2026
; 
Li et al., 2021a
)
. While the former resembles large-scale retrieval, the latter more directly assesses personalization. Results on the 
StaR
 benchmark 
(
Kabongo et al., 2026
)
 show that popularity baselines remain competitive globally, while 
UserPop
 matches or outperforms several state-of-the-art models at the item level, motivating our focus on this setting.

Statement Ranking Datasets

Real-world recommendation datasets contain free-form reviews rather than structured explanatory statements. 
EXTRA
 
(
Li et al., 2021a
)
 constructs explanation-ranking datasets by extracting review sentences and grouping near-duplicates through lexical 
-gram similarity and locality-sensitive hashing 
(
Anand and Jeffrey David, 2011
)
. However, lexical matching may retain non-explanatory content and overlook semantic paraphrases. 
StaR
 
(
Kabongo et al., 2026
)
 addresses these limitations through LLM-based statement extraction and a three-stage semantic clustering pipeline combining approximate nearest-neighbor retrieval, reranker-based filtering, and graph-based refinement. We build upon this pipeline with two refinements: an extraction prompt emphasizing the user–item match and stricter reranker-based verification of cluster merges. These changes aim to improve explanatory relevance and semantic consistency.

Neural Retrieval Models

Neural retrieval represents queries and candidates in a shared embedding space, enabling efficient similarity-based ranking with precomputable candidate representations. Early dual-encoder approaches, including DSSM 
(
Huang et al., 2013
)
, Sentence-BERT 
(
Reimers and Gurevych, 2019
)
, and DPR 
(
Karpukhin et al., 2020
)
, established this paradigm. Subsequent work improved dense retrieval through contrastive learning and hard negative mining 
(
Xiong et al., 2021
)
, large-scale weak supervision 
(
Wang et al., 2022
)
, and instruction-conditioned representations 
(
Su et al., 2023
)
. More recently, RepLLaMA 
(
Ma et al., 2024b
)

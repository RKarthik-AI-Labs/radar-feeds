Abstract.

Generative search engines increasingly shape how users discover products, services, and commercial entities by generating answers from retrieved web content. This creates an opportunity to influence which brands appear in these answers by modifying the content available to the retrieval system. In this work, we study an adversarial generative engine optimization (GEO) setting in which a predefined brand is added to an article through a single contextually integrated mention. The challenge is to make the mention fit the surrounding article without making the content overtly promotional, while keeping it relevant enough to be retrieved and to influence the generated answer. To study this, we introduce

mplicit 
B

IBIBench

We compare IBI with Direct Prompting and Best-of-N with ranking under an equivalent budget, evaluating insertion quality, target-chunk retrieval, and downstream brand propagation. Compared with the baselines, IBI produces insertions that better preserve semantics and disrupt perplexity less, while improving Hit@k across all evaluated cutoffs, query regimes, and placement settings. 1. 
Introduction

For decades, businesses and publishers have competed for online visibility through Search Engine Optimization (SEO), which aims to improve the ranking of webpages in search results 
(
Sharma et al., 2019
; 
Google, 2024
)
. LLM-based search systems, or generative engines, alter this interaction by retrieving information from multiple sources and synthesizing them into direct answers 
(
Kirsten et al., 2026
)
. This shift has motivated Generative Engine Optimization (GEO), which studies how web content can be optimized for greater visibility, citation, and representation in generated responses 
(
Aggarwal et al., 2024
)
. As these systems increasingly mediate product and service discovery, visibility within generated responses acquires commercial value.

Prior adversarial GEO work has primarily studied manipulation of source or product visibility through ranking-oriented or optimized content. We study a different setting, illustrated in Figure 
1
, in which a predefined target brand is introduced into an otherwise informational article through a single contextually integrated mention. The insertion contains no model-directed instruction, explicit endorsement, call to action, or repeated keyword pattern. The attacker can publish or modify web content but does not know the future queries or control the downstream retriever or generator. We ask: can a single minimally disruptive and non-promotional brand mention remain natural to readers while achieving retrieval exposure and downstream propagation under brand-agnostic queries?

Figure 1. 

Overview of retrieval-mediated brand propagation. A single target-brand mention inserted into an informational article can be retrieved and propagated into the generated response to a brand-agnostic query.

This setting creates a tension between subtlety and downstream influence. The inserted mention should preserve the article’s meaning and style without reading as advertising, while remaining relevant enough to reach the retrieved context. A single-pass prompting approach must jointly determine placement, phrasing, and promotional tone without explicitly evaluating alternative placements or revising failed candidates.

Figure 2. 

Overview of the IBI framework. The Placement Planner identifies candidate insertion locations, the Rewriter produces minimal brand insertions, the Verifier evaluates candidate quality and requests revisions when necessary, and the Coordinator selects the best valid insertion.

To address this trade-off, we introduce 
Implicit Brand Injection (IBI)
, a multi-agent framework that separates placement planning, sentence rewriting, verification, and candidate selection (Figure 
2
). Given an article, a predefined synthetic brand, and an insertion setting, IBI identifies candidate locations, minimally rewrites one sentence, and verifies the resulting edit for semantic preservation, contextual coherence, naturalness, promotional safety, and minimal modification. Each treated article contains exactly one mention of the target brand.

We further construct 
IBIBench
, a benchmark derived from 1,000 informational web articles spanning 12 domains and 60 synthetic brands. Synthetic brands reduce confounding from prior model familiarity, popularity, and historical exposure. Each article is paired with both an 
endemic
 brand from the same domain and a 
non-endemic
 brand connected through a plausible secondary relationship. Brand assignments are fixed before insertion.

We compare IBI against Direct Prompting (DP) and a call-matched Best-of-N (BoN) baseline designed to control for additional inference-time sampling and candidate selection. We evaluate insertion quality, target-chunk retrieval, and downstream brand propagation under article-conditioned and broader category-level queries. Retrieval is evaluated using two dense retrievers, while propagation is evaluated across five response generators.

IBI produces more semantically preserving and less perplexity-disruptive insertions than both baselines. Its advantage also persists beyond additional sampling: compared with call-matched BoN, IBI achieves higher Brand Mention Rate and Conditional Propagation across all 20 generator–query–placement conditions. IBI reaches 
82.2% Hit@10
 and 
0.74 MRR@10
 under article-conditioned endemic queries, with downstream Brand Mention Rate and Conditional Propagation reaching 
62.7%
 and 
75.9%
, respectively.

The 
primary contributions
 of this paper are:

(1)

We formulate adversarial GEO as a retrieval-mediated entity-propagation problem in which a single implicit brand mention can reach downstream generative responses.

(2)

We introduce 
Implicit Brand Injection
, a multi-agent framework for planning, rewriting, verifying, and selecting minimally disruptive brand insertions.

(3)

We construct 
IBIBench
, covering 1,000 articles, 12 diverse domains, 60 synthetic brands, and endemic and non-endemic insertion settings.

(4)

2. 
Background and Related Work

Generative Engine Optimization (GEO) extends traditional Search Engine Optimization (SEO) from improving webpage rankings to improving the visibility, citation, or representation of content in LLM-generated responses 
(
Sharma et al., 2019
; 
Google, 2024
; 
Aggarwal et al., 2024
)
. Generative search introduces multiple points at which visibility can be affected, including retrieval, ranking, context selection, and answer generation 
(
Chen et al., 2026
)
. This motivates evaluating not only whether modified content is retrieved, but also whether information contained within it propagates to the generated response.

Recent work has studied adversarial manipulation of LLM-based search and recommendation systems. Some approaches embed explicit instructions in retrieved content to influence source or product rankings 
(
Pfrommer et al., 2024
; 
Nestaas et al., 2025
)
. Others optimize less conspicuous content. StealthRank generates adversarial text sequences designed to improve target rankings while preserving fluency 
(
Tang et al., 2025
)
, while CORE appends optimized string, reasoning, or review-based content to influence product rankings in generated responses 
(
Jin et al., 2026
)
. These methods primarily study ranking manipulation when the target content is already available to the downstream ranker or generator. Recent work on the risks of GEO similarly highlights this assumption and frames GEO as both an advertising and security surface 
(
Wen et al., 2026
)
.

Retrieval-augmented generation also exposes the underlying knowledge corpus to data-level manipulation. PoisonedRAG injects optimized passages to induce attacker-chosen answers for target questions 
(
Zou et al., 2024
)
, while Poison-RAG modifies item metadata to manipulate retrieval and downstream recommendations 
(
Nazary et al., 2025
)
. These attacks generally optimize against a known query or desired output. Defenses such as SCI-Defense instead seek to identify manipulation through perplexity, semantic integrity, and cross-candidate comparisons 
(
Yu et al., 2026
)
.

IBI studies a complementary threat model. The attacker inserts a single target-brand mention into ordinary informational content without model-directed instructions, knowledge of future queries, or access to the downstream retriever or generator. Rather than assuming that manipulated content is already present in the model context, we evaluate the complete path from sentence-level insertion to retrieval exposure and downstream brand propagation.

3. 
Methodology

3.1. 
Threat Model

Attacker’s objective.

The attacker seeks to increase the visibility of a predefined target brand across plausible information-seeking queries while satisfying the single-sentence, minimal-modification, and non-promotionality constraints defined in Section 
3.2
.

Attacker’s knowledge.

The attacker knows only broad properties of the source article, such as its topic, intended audience, and likely information needs. Future queries are unknown. We assume a black-box downstream system in which the attacker has no access to the retriever, ranking function, language-model parameters, system prompt, or generation procedure.

Attacker’s capabilities.

The attacker can publish or modify a publicly accessible article and select the target brand to introduce. We condition our analysis on the modified article entering the generative engine’s retrieval corpus. The attacker does not control crawling, indexing, ranking, or whether the article is ultimately included in a deployed system.

3.3. 
Implicit Brand Injection

We introduce 
Implicit Brand Injection (IBI)
, a multi-agent framework for constructing treated articles under the intervention defined in Section 
3.2
. Given a clean article 
, target brand 
, and placement setting 
, IBI produces a verified replacement for exactly one sentence while preserving the remainder of the article. The framework separates placement planning, rewriting, verification, and candidate selection, as summarized in Figure 
2
.

Placement Planner.

The Placement Planner identifies candidate locations where the target brand can be introduced without altering the article’s broader structure or informational purpose. It receives the clean article, brand profile, placement setting, and semantic bridge defined during article–brand pairing, and returns a set of candidate strategies

Each strategy specifies a target sentence and the contextual role through which the brand may be introduced. Endemic strategies connect the brand to the article’s primary topic or audience need, while non-endemic strategies use a plausible cross-domain bridge such as a secondary need, adjacent activity, shared audience, or workflow. The planner does not modify the article or introduce new claims about the brand.

Content Rewriter.

For each selected strategy 
, the Content Rewriter receives the target sentence and its immediate context and produces

The rewrite must preserve the informational role of 
, contain exactly one mention of the target brand, and remain coherent with the surrounding sentences. No other sentence may be modified. The rewriter is instructed to avoid recommendations, endorsements, calls to action, superiority claims, unsupported functionality, URLs, and fabricated user behavior, keeping the brand incidental to the sentence.

Verifier.

The Verifier evaluates each candidate together with the original sentence and local context:

It assesses semantic preservation, narrative coherence, insertion naturalness, promotional safety, and minimal modification. Candidates that fail the required criteria are returned to the Rewriter with structured feedback. If repeated revisions fail, the framework proceeds to another placement strategy.

Coordinator and deterministic validation.

The Coordinator manages candidate generation and revision and selects among verifier-approved candidates according to the predefined selection priorities. The selected candidate then undergoes deterministic checks enforcing exactly one occurrence of the target brand, preservation of all text outside the selected sentence, and the absence of additional brands or URLs. Candidates that fail these checks are rejected.

For each successful construction, IBI stores the clean and treated sentences, their immediate context, the assigned brand, and the insertion count. The treated article is reconstructed by replacing only the selected sentence. Prompt templates for all four agents are provided in Appendix 
E.3
.

4. 
Benchmark: IBIBench

We construct IBIBench, a controlled benchmark for studying whether a brand mention inserted into informational web content is retrieved and subsequently propagated into generative-engine responses. Each benchmark instance specifies a clean article, a target brand, and an insertion setting. The resulting treated article differs from its clean counterpart in exactly one sentence, enabling controlled comparison of the inserted mention.

Source Article Collection.

The source corpus contains 1,000 informational web articles spanning 12 domains: arts and creativity, beauty and fashion, education, finance, fitness, food and cooking, health, home and garden, parenting and family, productivity, technology, and travel. We retain articles containing at least 300 words and exclude listicles, product roundups, comparison pages, highly promotional documents, and articles with excessive existing brand references. The retained articles are converted to plain text, assigned a domain label and article identifier, and preserved as the clean corpus.

Synthetic Brands.

We construct 60 synthetic brands, with five associated with each domain. Each brand has a unique name, domain, short description, and intended function. Synthetic brands avoid ethical concerns associated with manipulating real companies and reduce confounding from existing popularity, reputation, search frequency, and prior model exposure.

Article–Brand Pair Construction.

Each clean article is paired with two target brands. In the 
endemic
 setting, the brand belongs to the same domain as the article and is connected to its primary topic or activity. In the 
non-endemic
 setting, the brand belongs to a different domain and is connected through a plausible secondary relationship, such as an adjacent task, secondary need, shared audience, or situational use case.

Brand assignments and their semantic bridges are fixed before insertion. The insertion method therefore cannot select a different brand or relationship based on ease of placement. Applying both settings to all 1,000 source articles yields 2,000 article–brand pairs, evenly divided between endemic and non-endemic settings. Table 
2
 reports their domain-level distribution.

Treated Article Construction.

For each article–brand pair, a construction method replaces exactly one source sentence with a modified sentence containing one occurrence of the assigned target brand. All remaining article text is unchanged. This matched structure is used throughout the insertion-quality, retrieval, and propagation experiments.

5. 
Evaluation

5.1. 
Experimental Design

We evaluate IBI at three stages: (i) insertion quality, (ii) retrieval of the injected brand-containing passage, and (iii) propagation of the target brand into the generated response. Experiments are conducted separately for endemic and non-endemic placements. We compare IBI against Direct Prompting (DP) and a call-matched Best-of-N (BoN) baseline under identical article–brand assignments.

Dataset.

All experiments use IBIBench. Comparisons between DP, BoN, and IBI use matched article–brand pairs so that each method receives the same source article, target brand, placement setting, and semantic bridge.

RAG Setup.

We evaluate downstream propagation using a standard retrieval-augmented generation pipeline.

•

Retrieval corpus:

Articles are split into 256-token chunks with an overlap of 40 tokens. For each evaluated condition, the corresponding treated article replaces its clean counterpart while the remainder of the corpus is unchanged.

•

Retriever:

We use 
BAAI/bge-base-en-v1.5
 as the primary dense retriever. Queries and document chunks are embedded independently, and retrieval uses inner-product similarity with a FAISS 
IndexFlatIP
 index. We retrieve the top-
 chunks for each query.

•

Response generators:

We evaluate GPT-5-mini, GPT-OSS-20B, Qwen3-8B, Gemma2-9B, and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. Each model receives the same response-generation prompt and retrieved context for a given experimental condition.

Query Generation.

We evaluate retrieval and downstream propagation under two query regimes: 
article-conditioned
 and 
category-level
. Queries are generated independently of the treated articles and never receive the target brand, modified sentence, placement setting, or semantic bridge.

Article-conditioned queries are generated using only the corresponding clean source article and represent information needs closely aligned with that document. Category-level queries are generated from the collection of clean articles within each domain and capture broader information needs that may be relevant to multiple documents. Queries containing the assigned synthetic brand or information introduced only by a treated sentence are excluded. The resulting query sets are held fixed across DP, BoN, and IBI.

Query generation uses 
GPT-5-mini
, with 1,000 article-conditioned queries and 1,000 category-level queries, yielding 2,000 evaluation queries in total. Full prompts are provided in Appendix 
C
.

5.2. 
Baselines

We compare IBI against two prompting baselines. 
Direct Prompting (DP)
 performs the insertion in a single GPT-4o-mini call. It receives the clean article, target brand, placement setting, and semantic bridge, and directly produces a one-sentence modification without explicit placement planning, candidate selection, iterative verification, or strategy fallback.

To control for additional inference-time computation, we also evaluate a call-matched 
 Best-of-N (BoN)
 baseline. BoN uses the same average six-call budget as IBI, generating five independent candidate insertions followed by one ranking call that selects the strongest candidate. Unlike IBI, BoN does not use explicit placement planning or iterative verifier feedback.

All methods receive the same article–brand inputs and insertion constraints. Comparisons are performed on the matched intersection of article–brand pairs for which all methods produce valid outputs. Prompt templates are provided in Appendix 
E.1
.

5.3. 
Evaluation Metrics

We evaluate the attack at three stages: 
insertion quality
, 
retrieval exposure
, and 
response propagation
.

Insertion Quality.

We measure semantic preservation, local coherence change, and perplexity-based naturalness using the modified sentence and its surrounding context. We additionally conduct a blinded human evaluation of perceived naturalness and promotionality.

Retrieval Exposure.

We report Hit@
 for 
 and MRR@10. A retrieval is successful only when the retrieved chunk originates from the paired article and contains the target-brand mention, distinguishing retrieval of the source article from exposure to the injected content.

BMR measures the fraction of responses mentioning the target brand, while BRR measures the fraction recommending it, as judged by GPT-5-mini. 5.4. 
Results

5.4.1. 
Insertion Quality

Table 
1
 compares the linguistic quality of insertions produced by DP, call-matched BoN, and IBI. IBI achieves the highest semantic preservation in both settings, reaching 0.794 for endemic and 0.791 for non-endemic insertions. BoN provides only modest improvements over DP, suggesting that additional sampling alone does not account for the preservation gains of IBI.

The perplexity-based metrics show a similar pattern. IBI produces the naturalness and forward ratios closest to one in both settings, indicating smaller disruptions to the modified sentence and its transition to subsequent text. Local coherence is more mixed. BoN performs best in the endemic setting, while IBI performs best in the non-endemic setting. Overall, IBI improves semantic preservation and perplexity-based naturalness relative to both baselines, while embedding-based local coherence remains broadly comparable.

5.4.2. 
Human Assessment of Insertion Quality

We additionally conduct a small-scale blinded human evaluation of perceived naturalness and promotionality. Eight annotators independently evaluate the same 50 paragraphs, comprising 25 DP and 25 IBI examples, and provide binary judgments for both criteria.

As shown in Table

5.4.3. 
Retrieval Performance

Table 
3
 reports target-chunk retrieval for DP, BoN, and IBI. BoN provides modest gains over DP, but IBI achieves the highest Hit@
 across every cutoff, query regime, and placement setting. Under article-conditioned queries, IBI reaches 76.1% Hit@10 for endemic placements and 82.2% for non-endemic placements, compared with 70.9% and 65.2% for BoN. Under category-level queries, IBI reaches 48.0% and 45.3%, compared with 40.1% and 38.6% for BoN.

Thus, relative to the call-matched baseline, IBI improves Hit@10 by 6.7–11.3 percentage points across the four retrieval conditions. Absolute retrieval rates are lower for category-level queries because they are less closely aligned with an individual source article, but the advantage of IBI persists.

We additionally repeat the IBI retrieval evaluation using intfloat/e5-base-v2. The same qualitative pattern is observed, with full results reported in Appendix

5.4.4. 
Brand Propagation

Table 
4
 reports downstream propagation across five response generators. Relative to DP, IBI improves BMR by 6.7–22.5 percentage points and CP by 7.7–16.8 points across the evaluated conditions. More importantly, IBI also outperforms the call-matched BoN baseline on BMR and CP in all 20 generator–query–placement combinations.

Averaged across these 20 conditions, BoN improves over DP by 4.5 points in BMR and 3.5 points in CP. IBI provides an additional 8.4-point improvement in BMR and 7.5-point improvement in CP over BoN. This indicates that additional inference-time sampling explains only part of the observed propagation gains.

Propagation is consistently stronger under article-conditioned queries than category-level queries, reflecting their higher retrieval rates. Endemic placements also generally yield higher absolute propagation than non-endemic placements, although IBI remains effective in both settings. GPT-5-mini exhibits the highest absolute BMR and CP across the four evaluated conditions, reaching 62.7% BMR and 75.9% CP under article-conditioned endemic queries.

BRR follows the same overall pattern but exhibits greater variation. IBI outperforms BoN in 18 of 20 conditions, with two small exceptions: Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct under category-level endemic queries and Qwen3-8B under category-level non-endemic queries. Overall, the propagation results show that the advantage of IBI persists after controlling for inference-time sampling and candidate selection.

5.5. 
Analysis

Endemic and non-endemic insertions.

Endemic insertions generally achieve higher retrieval and propagation because the target brand is more directly aligned with the article domain. IBI nevertheless provides consistent gains for non-endemic placements, showing that downstream propagation can also arise through indirect contextual relationships, although with lower absolute effectiveness.

Effect of query specificity.

Article-conditioned queries yield higher retrieval and propagation because they are more closely aligned with a particular source article. Category-level queries express broader information needs and therefore provide a more competitive retrieval setting. IBI improves performance under both regimes, indicating that its effect is not restricted to narrowly source-aligned queries.

Effect of inference-time computation.

The call-matched BoN baseline improves over single-call DP, confirming that additional candidate sampling provides some benefit. However, IBI consistently achieves higher BMR and CP than BoN across all 20 generator–query–placement conditions and substantially higher target-chunk retrieval across all four retrieval conditions. This indicates that the gains of IBI are not explained solely by additional inference-time computation.

Early-rank concentration.

Across the four IBI retrieval conditions, Hit@1 accounts for approximately 76–88% of Hit@10. Thus, successful target-chunk retrieval is often concentrated near the top of the ranking rather than occurring only at deeper positions. This pattern persists under category-level and non-endemic settings despite their lower overall retrieval rates.

Component ablation.

We conduct a targeted qualitative ablation on 100 randomly sampled article–brand pairs. Without the Placement Planner, we observe insertions in sentences where the brand is weakly motivated by the surrounding context, particularly for non-endemic placements. Without the Verifier, we observe overtly promotional and recommendation-like phrasing that the full pipeline is designed to reject. These failure modes suggest complementary roles for placement planning and verification.

6. 
Mitigation Strategies

IBI does not rely on model-directed instructions, so prompt-injection defenses based on instruction separation or priority do not directly address this threat 
(
Chen et al., 2024
; 
Wallace et al., 2024
)
. More relevant defenses may reduce reliance on individual retrieved documents. Isolate-then-aggregate approaches process retrieved passages independently before combining their outputs 
(
Xiang et al., 2026
)
. In recommendation settings, systems could similarly require corroboration from multiple independent sources before surfacing unfamiliar commercial entities.

Attribution provides a complementary defense. Linking generated entity mentions to their supporting passages could make the origin of a recommendation easier to audit 
(
Bohnet et al., 2023
)
. We leave empirical evaluation of these mitigation strategies to future work.

7. 
Conclusion

We introduced Implicit Brand Injection (IBI), a multi-agent framework for studying adversarial GEO through a single contextually integrated brand mention. We also constructed IBIBench, comprising 1,000 informational articles, 2,000 article–brand pairs, 60 synthetic brands, and 12 domains across endemic and non-endemic settings. Compared with both Direct Prompting and a call-matched Best-of-N baseline, IBI produces more semantically preserving and less perplexity-disruptive insertions, achieves higher target-chunk retrieval, and yields stronger brand mention and conditional propagation across five response generators. These results show that subtle brand mentions can obtain retrieval exposure and propagate into generated responses, highlighting a potential channel for covert commercial influence in generative search.

8. 
Limitations

Our experiments use synthetic brands and a controlled RAG pipeline to isolate the effect of inserted brand mentions. This design does not capture prior familiarity, reputation, authority, or other signals associated with established brands. We also condition on the modified article entering the retrieval corpus and do not model web crawling, index admission, freshness, or source-authority mechanisms used by deployed generative search systems.

We restrict each treated article to a single brand mention to study a minimal intervention and avoid confounding from repeated exposure or keyword frequency. We do not claim that one mention is optimal. Stronger adversaries could introduce repeated mentions or multiple brands, which may increase retrieval and propagation but could also affect detectability and perceived naturalness.

Our primary retrieval experiments use a dense retriever, with a second dense retriever as a robustness check. Sparse, hybrid, and proprietary retrieval systems may behave differently. Similarly, broader evaluation across languages, naturally occurring user queries, and additional deployed systems would further test generality.

Finally, the human evaluation covers 50 paragraphs and should be interpreted as a small-scale assessment of perceived naturalness and promotionality. BRR is estimated using an LLM judge and may therefore contain evaluation noise.

Ethics and Privacy Statement

This work studies a dual-use threat involving manipulation of brand visibility in generative search systems. We use only synthetic brands and do not deploy modified articles on the public web, target live search systems, or attempt to influence real users. The source corpus consists of publicly accessible informational articles stored as plain text without source URLs or publisher identifiers. We do not intentionally collect sensitive personal information or user interaction data, and human evaluation is limited to judgments of naturalness and promotionality. We release IBI and IBIBench to support research on detection, attribution, and mitigation. Section 6 discusses potential defenses based on source corroboration and response attribution.

Appendix A 
Implementation Details

All agents in the IBI pipeline use GPT-4o-mini. The Placement Planner returns a variable number of candidate insertion locations depending on the source article and article–brand relationship. For each selected location, the Content Rewriter produces a single-sentence replacement. Rejected candidates may be revised for up to three iterations before the pipeline proceeds to another placement strategy.

The Verifier assigns integer scores from 1 to 5 for semantic preservation, narrative coherence, insertion naturalness, promotional safety, and minimal modification. A candidate is approved only when every criterion receives a score of at least 4 and the structural requirements are satisfied. The Coordinator then selects among the verifier-approved candidates according to the predefined selection priorities.

The call-matched Best-of-N (BoN) baseline uses the same average six-call budget as IBI. Five independent candidate insertions are generated, followed by one selection call using the same selection prompt as the IBI Coordinator. Unlike IBI, BoN does not use explicit placement planning or iterative verifier feedback.

For retrieval, articles are split into 256-token chunks with an overlap of 40 tokens. We use 
BAAI/bge-base-en-v1.5
 as the primary dense retriever with FAISS 
IndexFlatIP
 and retrieve the top 10 passages for each query. Downstream propagation is evaluated using GPT-5-mini, GPT-OSS-20B, Qwen3-8B, Gemma2-9B, and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. Temperature is set as close to zero as supported by the corresponding inference interface, otherwise the default decoding configuration is used.

Appendix C 
Query Generation

All evaluation queries are generated using GPT-5-mini and independently of the treated articles. The query-generation procedure does not receive the target brand, treated sentence, placement setting, or semantic bridge. Queries containing the assigned synthetic brand or information introduced only by a treated sentence are excluded before evaluation.

We use 1,000 article-conditioned queries and 1,000 category-level queries, yielding 2,000 evaluation queries in total. The resulting query sets are held fixed across DP, BoN, and IBI.

C.1. 
Article-Conditioned Queries

Article-conditioned queries are generated using only the corresponding clean source article. They represent information-seeking requests closely aligned with the content of a particular source document.

[ARTICLE]

C.2. 
Category-Level Queries

Category-level queries represent broader information needs within each content domain. Instead of conditioning on one source article, the generator receives the category and the collection of clean articles associated with that category. It is instructed to generate questions reflecting recurring topics and information needs across the collection rather than any particular document.

Return only the generated questions.

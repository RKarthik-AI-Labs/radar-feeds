Abstract

Individualized attribution estimates which functional networks drive a model prediction for a given subject and is often proposed as an interpretable resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) biomarker, but its test–retest reliability is rarely quantified.
We evaluate per-subject network-ablation attribution for a frozen resting-state functional connectivity (FC) predictor (Schaefer-200 FC predicting MMSE/ADAS-Cog13) trained on 
 subjects from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI), using (i) within-session split-half, (ii) scan–rescan repeat visits (74 subjects, 205 baseline–repeat pairs; median inter-visit interval 24.4 months), and (iii) a subject-level permutation null.

Thus, in ADNI, individualized network attribution contains a modest but non-zero person-specific component atop a strong population-shared pattern. We recommend reporting scan–rescan reliability together with a permutation-null control, rather than within-session proxies alone, to avoid overstating individual specificity.

1 
Introduction

Models that map resting-state functional connectivity (FC) to cognition and clinical phenotypes are increasingly used in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) neuroimaging 
[
4
, 
16
]
. Beyond group-level accuracy, a common objective is 
individualized attribution
: for a given subject, estimating which functional networks drive that subject’s prediction. Such per-subject attribution is only meaningful if it is reproducible.

Reliability is a central constraint. Large-scale analyses suggest that stable brain–behavior effects often require much larger samples than historically used 
[
11
]
, and FC test–retest reliability is frequently modest (pooled mean edge-level Intraclass Correlation Coefficient, ICC 
) 
[
13
]
.
The ICC describes how reproducibly a single connectivity edge is measured. Prior work on subject-specific parcellations reports reproducibility across short-interval repeat sessions 
[
17
, 
10
]
, but does not quantify test–retest reliability of model-derived attribution signatures over the multi-year intervals typical of aging cohorts.

An additional aspect is 
attribution stability
: feature-importance maps can
vary substantially under resampling, noise, and other perturbations even when
predictive accuracy is similar 
[
1
, 
7
]
. In
FC prediction, however, attribution work has largely emphasized group-averaged
importance. For connectomes rather than attributions, differential
identifiability formalizes the person-specific, test–retest-identifiable
component 
[
2
]
, and permutation-null models are
standard for separating population-shared from person-specific
structure 
[
5
, 
12
]
. Neither has been
applied to per-subject attribution signatures. To the best of our knowledge, no prior work
combines per-subject attribution of an FC-based cognitive predictor with a
quantitative test–retest evaluation on independent repeat scans of the same
individuals.

We address this gap using a frozen FC-based multilayer perceptron (FC-MLP) trained to jointly predict MMSE and ADAS-Cog13 from Schaefer-200 FC on 
 ADNI subjects (Sect. 
2
). Instead of edge-level ICC, we assess test–retest consistency of each subject’s network-attribution ranking. We compute per-subject network-ablation signatures and evaluate reproducibility with three checks: within-session split-half, scan–rescan across independent repeat visits, and a cluster-aware subject-level permutation null.
The main contributions in this study are:

1.

Quantify how split-half overestimates scan–rescan reliability of per-subject network attribution (74 subjects; 205 session pairs).

2.

Use a subject-level permutation null to estimate the mismatched-subject baseline and the residual person-specific component.

3.

Provide reporting recommendations to avoid overstating individual specificity.

3.1 
Data description

ADNI is a multi-site, longitudinal study of aging and Alzheimer’s disease with multimodal neuroimaging 
[
9
]
. We analyzed data from 
 unique ADNI participants who had 
both

For the upstream prediction model, the cohort is split once at the subject level into fixed train/validation/test partitions of 611/132/132 stratified by diagnosis. We evaluate attribution and reliability only on the held-out 132-subject test split; scan–rescan further restricts to test subjects with repeat-visit rs-fMRI.

3.2 
Implementation details

We analyze a trained FC-MLP and keep its weights fixed for all attribution and reliability analyses.

Input features

rs-fMRI preprocessing includes motion correction, HD-BET brain extraction 
[
8
]
, rigid rs-fMRI
T1 registration, detrending, and nuisance regression of mean white-matter (WM) and cerebrospinal-fluid (CSF) signals plus 5 anatomical CompCor (aCompCor) components 
[
3
]
 from a combined WM/CSF mask, without global signal regression. Time series are band-pass filtered (0.01–0.1 Hz, using each subject’s TR), 
-scored, and scrubbed at framewise displacement (FD) 
 mm after filtering; FD was computed from translation magnitude only rather than the full six-parameter formulation 
[
14
]
, as rotation parameters were not retained upstream. No spatial smoothing or slice-timing correction is applied. We compute Schaefer-200 
[
15
]
 FC as a Fisher-
 transformed 
 Pearson correlation matrix and vectorize the upper triangle (19,900 edges). An edge-coverage mask is concatenated, yielding a 39,800-dimensional input.

Model and training (frozen)

The FC-MLP encoder maps

 with GELU/dropout (0.3). The model is trained with Huber loss (

[

4 
Discussion

Per-subject attribution reliability depends strongly on protocol: split-half is near-ceiling (
), but drops on scan–rescan across independent repeat visits (
–
), consistent with additional between-visit variability beyond within-session sampling noise. The permutation null suggests that much of this structure is population-shared, with only a modest person-specific excess; robustness to other cohorts, preprocessing, parcellations, attribution operators, and models remains untested.

The coexistence of shared and person-specific structure mirrors FC itself, where connectomes are identifiable across sessions 
[
5
, 
2
]
 yet test–retest reliability is modest 
[
13
]
. The strong shared profile likely has two sources. First, the ablations overlap by construction: every between-network edge 
 with 
, 
 is zeroed in both 
 and 
, coupling 
 and 
; within-network-only ablation would remove this overlap. Second, the weak predictor may respond mainly to group-level FC structure. Because 
 is bounded above, a high shared baseline leaves little room for an individual excess, so the person-specific component may be underestimated.

Limitations include the modest repeat-visit sample (74 subjects; 205 session pairs) and the weak, run-variable predictor (Sect. 
3
). Repeat-visit sessions were not ComBat-harmonized, so part of the split-half to scan–rescan gap may reflect this mismatch. Inter-visit intervals are long and variable (median 24.4 months, IQR 12.2–48.4) and are not modeled.

We therefore recommend treating within-session proxies as expected upper bounds, reporting scan–rescan estimates when repeat visits exist, and including a permutation-null (or equivalent) control; these recommendations should be validated in further aging/AD cohorts such as OASIS-3 and AIBL. In summary, per-subject network attributions are far less reproducible across visits than within-session estimates suggest, and most of their apparent stability reflects a pattern shared across the cohort, so individual specificity should be demonstrated against an explicit population baseline before attributions are interpreted as personal biomarkers.

Abstract

Agentic AI systems extend large language models with tools, memory, retrieval, browser or computer-use interfaces, code execution, external services, and multi-agent coordination. Although these capabilities enable agents to act beyond text generation and perform tasks, they also create attack paths through which adversaries can exploit untrusted content, deceptive metadata, poisoned state, compromised skills, mutable dependencies, or malicious code. Such attacks can manipulate planning and tool use, corrupt memory, abuse code-execution environments, and trigger harmful downstream effects.

In this paper, we present a systematic review of malicious attacks targeting agentic AI systems. We screened 186 candidate sources and retained 98. Following independent coding and resolver adjudication, we identified 284 atomic attacks, 452 atomic safeguards, and 660 explicit attack–safeguard relationships. We organize attacks into eight causal-mechanism classes while retaining insertion points, activation conditions, abused capabilities, objectives, impacts, persistence, and evidence as orthogonal dimensions.

Our analysis shows that tool, MCP, and action-layer attacks account for the largest share of atomic attacks, while the prompt-injection and action-hijacking class has the broadest paper coverage. We identify distinct class-specific activation patterns and separation between insertion and later activation. Safeguard coverage remains uneven: only 278 of 660 relationships are directly assessed, and strong coverage is uncommon. These findings support lifecycle-aware threat modeling, agent evaluation, tool governance, and defense-in-depth security for agentic AI systems.

III 
Methodology

Our methodology comprises eight sequential stages, as illustrated in
Figure 
1
: source discovery, eligibility screening,
data extraction, evidence grading, normalization and structural
adjudication, taxonomy construction, safeguard coding, and consistency
review.

We considered peer-reviewed literature and technically attributable grey
literature to capture both archival studies and early disclosures of
agentic-AI attacks. A source was eligible if it (1) described an agentic
AI system, component, workflow, artifact, or related security mechanism;
(2) reported an attack that affected, or was designed to affect, agentic
behavior or downstream actions; and (3) provided sufficient technical
detail to identify a concrete attack path, security-relevant effect, or
safeguard.

We included attacks that targeted an agentic workflow and produced, or
were designed to produce, a security-relevant effect beyond an
undesirable text response. We excluded ordinary model errors,
hallucinations, bias, general performance degradation, direct adversarial
examples without downstream agentic action, and speculative discussions
that did not specify a concrete attack path or security impact.

The literature collection was bounded by a
July 2026 cutoff. Sources published or
disclosed after this cutoff were not included
in the analytical corpus.

Google Scholar and arXiv served as the primary discovery interfaces. We
supplemented these searches with backward and forward snowballing,
targeted venue searches, and technically attributable grey literature,
including platform disclosures, repository reports, security advisories,
vulnerability records, and incident reports. Because terminology varies
across prompt injection, tool and MCP attacks, memory and RAG poisoning,
multi-agent security, backdoors, malicious skills, and AI supply-chain
security, we used multiple query families rather than a single search
string. Table

candidate and retained source counts.

III-A
 
Normalization, Atomicity, and Deduplication

Different sources often use different terms for similar mechanisms, while
the same study may describe multiple payloads, experimental settings,
objectives, or downstream effects. We therefore distinguish source
evidence from analytical records. Quotations, highlighted passages,
benchmark results, and source descriptions provide evidence for coding
but are not themselves counting units.

Our primary attack unit is an 
atomic attack record
. We split two
observations into distinct attacks only when their causal mechanism or
attack surface materially differs, or when differences in attacker
access, delivery, insertion point, activation condition, or an
indispensable target define a different attack process. Differences only
in terminology, prompt wording, victim model, experimental
configuration, objective, or downstream impact do not by themselves
justify separate attack records.

For example, malicious instructions delivered through webpages, emails,
documents, or tool outputs may represent the same prompt-injection
mechanism when they exploit the same instruction–data boundary.
Conversely, two attacks delivered through the same artifact are treated
as distinct when one depends on semantic instruction following and the
other depends on executable code, package installation, or malicious
runtime behavior.

We apply the same atomicity principle to safeguards. A safeguard is
counted separately when the source presents it as a distinct control
mechanism or independently evaluates it as a defense baseline. Internal
implementation stages are not automatically counted as separate
safeguards unless they enforce causally distinct security boundaries.

After structural adjudication, the final analytical dataset contains
284 atomic attacks and 452 atomic safeguards.

III-B
 
Taxonomy Construction

We constructed the taxonomy iteratively through bottom-up mechanism
coding and top-down consistency review. During bottom-up analysis, we
extracted the causal process through which each attack influences an
agentic system. During top-down analysis, we grouped related mechanisms
into eight primary attack classes.

Each atomic attack receives exactly one primary class based
on its decisive causal mechanism, while carriers, target
components, abused capabilities, objectives, and impacts
remain orthogonal dimensions. Primary assignments are
mutually exclusive, although mechanisms may overlap
within compound incidents.

The final taxonomy contains eight classes:
(1) prompt injection and action hijacking;
(2) tool, MCP, and action-layer attacks;
(3) memory and RAG poisoning;
(4) multi-agent attacks;
(5) backdoors and trigger-conditioned compromise;
(6) computer-use and coding-agent attacks;
(7) agent skills and supply-chain attacks; and
(8) goal, task, and planning manipulation.

To distinguish overlapping mechanisms, C1
captures instruction–data boundary violations,
whereas C2 applies when manipulation of tool or
MCP interfaces is the decisive mechanism.
C3 is assigned when poisoning persistent memory
or retrieval state is indispensable to the attack,
rather than merely serving as a delivery channel
for instructions. C8 captures manipulation of
goal selection, task state, or planning logic,
rather than ordinary instruction-following
hijacking under C1.

Similarly, C4 requires agent-to-agent
interaction or propagation to be central to
the compromise, rather than merely occurring in a multi-agent setting. C5 applies when an implanted
backdoor is activated by a trigger, even if
the resulting behavior involves tool use;
C2 applies when the tool or MCP interface
itself is manipulated. C6 captures attacks
whose decisive mechanism exploits
computer-use or coding-agent execution,
whereas C7 applies when compromise through
a reusable skill, package, dependency, or
other supply-chain artifact is essential
to the attack.

For compound incidents, structural adjudication
first determines whether the evidence supports
one causal attack process or multiple distinct
atomic attacks under Section 3.1. Crossing
multiple lifecycle stages or producing several
impacts does not alone justify splitting.
Each resulting atomic record receives one
primary causal class.

For the cross-cutting analysis in Section 5, we additionally code target
component, attacker access, delivery channel, insertion point,
activation condition, abused capability, attacker objective, downstream
impact, persistence or propagation, evidence context, evidence level,
and evidence type.
The complete set of attack, evidence, safeguard, and relationship-level
coding dimensions is summarized in the final codebook in
Section 
III-D
.

III-C
 
Safeguard Coding and Coverage Assessment

We extracted safeguards from attack studies, empirical evaluations, system designs, benchmark papers, governance proposals, and dedicated defense papers, and classified them into five control types. First, 
preventive controls
 seek to block attack insertion or unsafe selection through provenance checks, tool allowlists, input validation, artifact signing, and permission design. Second, 
detective controls
 identify suspicious content or behavior through prompt-injection detectors, scanner-based review, anomaly detection, runtime tracing, and tool-call inspection. Third, 
containment controls
 limit the effects of successful attacks through sandboxing, least privilege, network restrictions, scoped credentials, and human approval for sensitive actions. Fourth, 
corrective controls
 remove or repair compromised state through memory cleanup, dependency rollback, poisoned-corpus removal, and agent replacement. Finally, 
recovery controls
 support post-incident response through logging, revocation, downstream notification, audit trails, and incident response procedures.

For RQ3, safeguards are analyzed as atomic control records. In addition
to control type, we code each safeguard’s status, origin, and direct
enforcement target.

We evaluate coverage at the level of explicit attack–safeguard
relationships rather than assuming that a safeguard applies uniformly
to every attack in the same class. A relationship is retained only when
the source explicitly links the safeguard to the attack mechanism or
when a direct defense experiment establishes that relationship.

For each explicit relationship, we record whether the safeguard was
directly assessed against the corresponding attack. Directly assessed
relationships are classified as 
strong
 when the safeguard
directly addresses the core mechanism under the evaluated assumptions
and leaves little residual attack success; 
partial
 when it
meaningfully reduces attack success or impact but leaves important
bypasses, uncovered stages, or restrictive assumptions; and

limited
 when protection is weak, indirect, brittle, or highly
assumption-dependent. Explicit relationships without attack-specific
evaluation are coded as 
not evaluated
 rather than receiving a
speculative effectiveness rating.

The final dataset contains 660 explicit source-supported
attack–safeguard relationships. The resulting attack, evidence, safeguard, and relationship-level
coding dimensions are consolidated in the final codebook described
next.

III-F
 
Consistency Review and Reproducibility

After adjudication, we performed corpus-wide consistency and
referential-integrity checks. The final dataset contains 98 paper
records, 284 unique atomic attack IDs, 452 unique safeguard IDs, and
660 unique attack–safeguard pairs. Every atomic attack has exactly one
primary C1–C8 class, and the class counts sum to 284. Every final
attack–safeguard relationship references a valid canonical attack and
safeguard record and contains both assessment and coverage status.

The companion artifact preserves coder-specific source IDs, canonical
record mappings, structural adjudication decisions, field-level
adjudication status, and evidence needed to trace the final synthesis
back to the reviewed sources.

IV 
RQ1: Taxonomy of Malicious Attack Vectors Targeting Agentic AI

Resolver adjudication yielded 284 atomic attacks across eight
causal-mechanism classes. Table 
IV
 summarizes the distribution of 284 adjudicated atomic attacks across eight mutually exclusive primary classes.

Attack Class #1 - Prompt Injection and Action Hijacking.

The instruction-abuse class is covered by 38 papers (38.8%) and
accounts for 57 atomic attacks (20.07%).
Through instruction–data confusion, malicious payloads
in webpages, emails, and retrieved results can hijack
agent planning and tool use, crossing authorization
boundaries.

Attack Class #2 - Tool, MCP, and Action-Layer Attacks.

This category is covered by 21 papers (21.4%) and accounts for
70 atomic attacks (24.65%), the largest share of atomic attacks in the
final corpus. These attacks can manipulate semantic interfaces, including
MCP metadata, function names, parameter schemas, and tool
descriptions, without requiring direct code execution.
Runtime rug-pull attacks further demonstrate that tools may
behave benignly during inspection but change after deployment.

Attack Class #3 - Memory and RAG Poisoning.

This attack class is covered by 21 papers (21.4%) and accounts for
34 atomic attacks (11.97%). The evidence highlights a shift in the
threat model toward delayed activation: poisoned memory or RAG content
can remain dormant across sessions and alter agent behavior when
retrieved during a later task. This persistence limits single-turn
evaluation, requiring agent safety
assessment across state transitions,
retrieval events, and task contexts.

Attack Class #4 - Multi-Agent Attacks.

This attack class is covered by 12 papers (12.2%) and accounts for
18 atomic attacks (6.34%). Multi-agent systems introduce topology-dependent risks:
compromised outputs can become authoritative inputs to
other agents and propagate across collaborative workflows.
Even individually benign-looking messages may combine
into malicious behavior, requiring defenses to monitor
collaborative trajectories rather than isolated agents.

Attack Class #5 - Backdoors and Trigger-Conditioned Compromise.

This attack class is covered by 14 papers (14.3%) and accounts for
19 atomic attacks (6.69%). Unlike model-weight backdoors, latent agentic backdoors
can also be implanted in skills, workflows, policies,
tools, or chat templates.
These payloads may evade static inspection and
activate only under specific environmental conditions, trigger
patterns, or stages of tool use 
[
19
, 
20
]
.

Attack Class #6 - Computer-Use and Coding-Agent Attacks.

This attack class is covered by 10 papers (10.2%) and accounts for
24 atomic attacks (8.45%). It poses direct execution risks because
model outputs can translate into browser actions, database queries,
file modifications, package installations, or shell commands.
The impact of command injection, unsafe
execution, and data exfiltration depends
on agent privileges and execution
boundaries.

Attack Class #7 - Agent Skills and Supply-Chain Attacks.

Attacks in this category are covered by 17 papers (17.3%) and account
for 51 atomic attacks (17.96%). Reused third-party skills, repositories, dependencies,
and remote services cross trust boundaries before agents
execute them or follow their instructions.
Provenance alone is insufficient: dynamic
skill mutation and malicious behavior
distributed across auxiliary files,
dependencies, or remote services can
evade scanning 
[
12
, 
8
, 
7
]
.

Attack Class #8 - Goal, Task, and Planning Manipulation.

This attack class is covered by four papers (4.1%) and accounts for
11 atomic attacks (3.87%). These attacks target autonomous goal
management and task decomposition rather than a single tool, memory
store, or execution interface. Attackers can redirect a legitimate task
through seemingly valid substeps that
collectively produce a malicious outcome,

Safeguard #1 - Prevention and Detection.

Preventive safeguards aim to block malicious content, tools, or artifacts
from entering the workflow or being selected for use. These measures

guards struggle with indirect, paraphrased, multi-turn, and
context-dependent attacks, while static scanners may miss semantically
malicious instructions, deceptive metadata, auxiliary files, or
runtime-only behavior.

Safeguard #2 - Containment and Least Privilege.

Containment safeguards address cases in which preventive and detective
controls fail. They limit impact through sandboxing, least privilege,
scoped credentials, network and filesystem restrictions, and human
approval for sensitive actions

Containment controls account for 48 of 452 atomic safeguards (10.6%).
These controls are especially important for tool, MCP, computer-use,

to the agent

8

9

malicious content from entering the agent’s context; they only limit the
damage that can result from its interpretation or execution.

Key Findings for RQ3

Within the reviewed corpus, no single safeguard
was shown to provide comprehensive protection
across all attack classes. Effective defense
therefore requires complementary preventive,
detective, containment, corrective, and recovery
controls. However, 382 of 660 explicit
attack–safeguard relationships (57.9%) were
not evaluated, and only 32 of the 278 directly
assessed relationships (11.5%) showed strong
coverage. These findings reflect the available
evaluation evidence rather than universal
safeguard effectiveness. Important evidence gaps
remain in delayed, semantic, and compositional
attacks, as well as post-compromise recovery.

Implications and Research Directions

Our taxonomy and safeguard mapping reveal open problems
spanning context, retrieval, memory, tools, execution,
external services, and multi-agent workflows. Because
attacks can cross components, mutate, and propagate,
securing agentic AI systems requires lifecycle-aware
analysis beyond isolated prompts, artifacts, or tools.

VIII-A
 
Prompt Injection and Agent Security

Research on prompt injection and jailbreaks demonstrates how adversarial
instructions can override system policies, redirect model behavior, or
produce unsafe responses. LLM-integrated applications often blur the
boundary between data and instructions, allowing malicious prompts
embedded in external content to influence application
behavior 
[
5
]
. Studies of deployed applications have
also demonstrated practical prompt-injection attacks across real
systems 
[
6

process external content, retrieved passages, tool outputs, memory entries,
and user instructions within a shared context. However, prompt-injection
research often emphasizes model responses or policy compliance. In agentic
systems, the potential impact is greater because injected instructions can
trigger tool calls, memory writes, file access, code execution, API
requests, or inter-agent communication. We therefore group prompt injection and
action hijacking by their shared mechanism
and assess their downstream agentic effects.

VIII-B
 
Tool, Plugin, and MCP Security

Research on tool, plugin, function-calling, and MCP security examines how
agents discover tools, interpret descriptions, generate parameters, call
APIs, and consume tool outputs. MCPTox shows that malicious instructions
embedded in MCP metadata can systematically compromise agent
behavior 
[
9

. Prior studies often treat tool misuse or
MCP poisoning as standalone problems. Our paper instead places tool and
MCP attacks within a broader taxonomy that compares them with memory
poisoning, malicious skills, browser and coding-agent attacks, multi-agent
propagation, and persistent compromise. Memory, retrieval-augmented generation, and multi-agent systems create
persistent and compositional attack surfaces. Zou et
al. 
[

knowledge database can steer subsequent retrieval-augmented outputs.
Prompt infection and conjunctive prompt attacks further show that
malicious behavior can propagate or activate only when messages,
templates, or routes are combined across agents

16
, 
15
]
.
Our lifecycle view distinguishes insertion,
selection, interpretation, activation,
persistence, and propagation across
these mechanisms.

IX 
Threats to Validity and Ethics

Our study is subject to limitations in search coverage, publication bias, and inconsistent terminology. Relevant attacks may appear under labels such as prompt injection, tool or MCP poisoning, memory poisoning, malicious skills, backdoors, jailbreaks, or supply-chain compromise. We mitigated these limitations through multiple query families, snowballing, and grey-literature searches. However, because search results and grey-literature availability change over time, the corpus should not be interpreted as exhaustive or exactly reproducible.

The corpus combines peer-reviewed publications, preprints, and technically attributable grey literature with differing review standards and evidence availability. It also includes attacks observed in the wild, demonstrated in sandbox settings, or described theoretically; we preserve these evidence distinctions rather than treating all attacks as equivalent. Coding requires judgment because attacks may span multiple components and effects. We therefore assign each atomic attack to the class that best captures its primary causal mechanism while retaining delivery, insertion, activation, capability, objective, impact, and related properties as orthogonal dimensions.

Inter-coder agreement varied across coding dimensions, particularly for some evidence and safeguard assignments. Adjudication established a consistent final dataset but does not eliminate uncertainty in independent coding, and agreement on aligned records does not capture disagreements in record discovery or atomic split/merge decisions.

Reported frequencies reflect representation in the reviewed literature, not real-world prevalence, incidence, or risk. Multiple atomic attacks may also originate from the same source and should not be treated as independent observations.

Finally, the study reflects the ecosystem as of July 2026, and subsequent developments may affect its conclusions. Given the dual-use nature of the topic, we focus on taxonomy, lifecycle patterns, and safeguards rather than operational payloads, active endpoints, credentials, or step-by-step exploitation instructions.

X 
Conclusion

This paper systematizes malicious attacks on agentic AI systems across 98 sources, identifying 284 atomic attacks, 452 atomic safeguards, and 660 explicit source-supported attack–safeguard relationships. We organize attacks into eight causal-mechanism classes and show that agentic-AI threats are system-level, spanning prompts, memory and retrieval, tools and MCP interfaces, execution environments, dependencies, external services, and multi-agent communication.

Our analysis of seven recurring patterns also reveals uneven safeguard coverage: only 278 of 660 relationships (42.1%) were directly assessed, and only 32 of those (11.5%) showed strong coverage. Defensive techniques such as prompt guards, tool
validation, sandboxing, least privilege, memory controls, provenance,
and registry governance each address part of the attack surface, but
significant gaps remain in delayed, semantic, compositional, and
post-compromise settings. These findings motivate a defense-in-depth
approach to agentic AI security and provide a foundation for threat
modeling, benchmark design, tool governance, and future research.

Abstract

Given that AI agents can search the web broadly and deeply in real time, it is tempting to conclude that their outputs account for recent world events. We show that this conclusion does not hold in practice. While agents 
can
 search and easily find recent changes, the correctness of their responses depends on 
whether
 they search and 
what
 they search for. Popular agentic search benchmarks like BrowseComp cannot expose failures wherein agents do not recognize their own assumptions that should be verified, since these benchmarks explicitly state what needs to be found. In contrast, many user requests state only the final information need (e.g., an itinerary for a trip), leaving the agent to discover relevant changes (e.g., the temporary closure of a museum) and account for their consequences.
To highlight such gaps, we introduce 
BlindToChange
, a benchmark that measures whether agents proactively discover recent events that affect their responses. Our benchmark comprises requests for recommendations where an obvious candidate is no longer applicable due to a recent real-world event. These events are drawn automatically from Wikidata, allowing 
BlindToChange
 to be periodically refreshed.
Strikingly, we
find a big gap—as high as 27.5 points, even at high reasoning effort—in systems’
performance,
when the recent event is not explicitly mentioned compared to when we ask about it directly.
Analyzing search trajectories,
we learn that more than half of the failures can be attributed to
the fact that agents
do not directly search to verify whether a relevant fact has changed.
Our benchmark
highlights, and tracks,
a new axis
of progress,
wherein systems
account for
recent
information
that is not
explicitly mentioned in the user request.

1 
Introduction

AI agents, specifically LLMs equipped with search and browsing tools, are increasingly used for information seeking 
(
Chatterji et al., 2025
; 
Xi et al., 2025a
)
. Access to real-time news articles and web pages through these tools invites the conclusion that agents’ responses always reflect the current state of the world. We argue that this is not the case, making it all the more concerning that users often rely on these responses without verifying against the cited sources 
(
Chapekis & Lieb, 2025
)
.

Consider a user who asks an agent to plan their trip to Normandy (see Figure 
1
b). While the Bayeux Tapestry Museum would normally be an obvious recommendation, it is currently closed for renovations. The agent (Gemini-3.1-pro in the example in the Figure) fails to account for this real-world update and erroneously includes the museum in the user’s itinerary. Crucially, such updates are easy for agents to find on the web. When directly asked to verify, the same agent readily informs the user that the museum is closed and suggests alternatives (Figure 
1
a). This inconsistency shifts the burden of verification to end users. Ideally, reliable agents must output well-researched and up-to-date responses by default. Agents must be able to identify unstated constraints (e.g. a museum’s current status) and account for them, regardless of whether the user’s query explicitly refers to them or asks to verify each fact. Without this, agents risk misleading users who trust their responses at face value.

Recent benchmarks, such as BrowseComp 
(
Wei et al., 2025
; 
Chen et al., 2026
)
 or DeepSearchQA 
(
Gupta et al., 2026
)
, also investigate agentic search capabilities but on an orthogonal axis. They require complex long-horizon search but all the constraints that need to be satisfied are clearly specified. Similarly, benchmarks for temporal freshness 
(
Vu et al., 2024
; 
Meem et al., 2024
; 
Kim et al., 2024
)
 directly state what to search for or even include temporal markers (e.g., current) that suggest that facts may have changed. In our setting, on the other hand, relevant changes that impact the final answer are easy to find but the need to find them is not apparent from the query itself.

We introduce 
BlindToChange
, a framework to study whether search agents proactively discover recent events that affect their responses and account for their consequences. We measure this as the gap between agents’ performance on two complementary probes of the same recent event: a direct probe that explicitly states the event in the query and an indirect probe that does not but requires reasoning about its consequences to answer correctly. This framing allows us to cleanly measure agents’ ability to discover relevant events and reason about their impact, without confounding our analysis with the difficulty of finding the event itself.

BlindToChange
 is constructed using an automated pipeline that extracts real-world events from Wikidata and leverages frontier LLMs to construct direct and indirect probes. Given a recent event, direct probes are easy to construct and responses to them are easy to evaluate. We phrase indirect probes as recommendation requests, carefully constructed such that the target event disqualifies an obvious candidate (e.g., the museum in our example). Then, we evaluate the agents’ responses for the 
absence
 of the disqualified candidate. This allows us to objectively score long-form responses to a subjective query. We borrow this idea of constructing indirect probes as recommendation requests from 
Li & Goyal (2025)
. Their own framework is limited in scale as it was manually curated. Moreover, it supports evaluation only on a small retrieval corpus of synthetically generated events and data, which cannot match the complexity or scale of real-world news and internet data. Therefore, it is inadequate for evaluating frontier agentic search systems.

We make two key design decisions that ensure the validity of our evaluation framework. First, we restrict events to those that occurred after the reported knowledge cutoff of the agent. Second, we only retain pairs with indirect probes for which the agent included the disqualified candidate in their responses before the event. We approximate behavior before the event by sampling multiple outputs with search disabled. Together, these ensure that search is necessary to provide accurate answers for both direct and indirect probes, and that the 
absence
 of the disqualified candidate in a response is due to agentic search and not chance.

Our current dataset comprises direct and indirect probes based on two event types: demises and ends of official tenures of public figures. These were chosen as they have sufficient coverage in Wikidata in the months since the knowledge cutoff of the frontier models, although our framework extends to other event types. We evaluate 8 frontier models in two search harnesses, each model’s own native search and a common scaffold with a shared web-search tool, at low and high reasoning effort. The native harness reflects how users actually encounter these systems, while the shared harness fixes the search backend across models and exposes the full search trajectory for analysis.

We find a large gap between what agents can find when asked explicitly and what they discover on their own. Agents almost always find the change when asked directly at high effort, reaching at least 96.5% direct accuracy on demises in nearly all configurations. Yet when queried with indirect probes for demises, the accuracy falls by a median 40.5 points at low effort and 27.5 points at high effort under the native harness; agents routinely recommend people who have passed away. The drop persists on tenures but is smaller, with median drops of 18.7 and 15.0 points for low and high reasoning effort, likely because officeholders change routinely and models are more inclined to check whether someone still holds a position. Strikingly, our error analysis under the shared harness finds that many failures stem from agents never verifying their own assumptions rather than from failing to retrieve the change. In fact, this accounts for over 75% of demise failures in 13 of the 15 configurations. Even on tenures, where models more often check whether the person still holds the position, not searching explicitly accounts for a median of 50% of failures across configurations.

One key finding is that strong performance on existing agentic benchmarks does not carry over to 
BlindToChange
. Notably, Claude-Opus-4.7 has the lowest indirect accuracy on demises under the shared harness at both effort levels. The issue is not search quality. When it searches as often as other models do, it is nearly as accurate. It simply searches less, issuing no search on 23% of demise probes at high effort (this number for other models is less than 8%). On tenures, where models search more readily, it is within 3 points of the most accurate model at both effort levels. GPT-5.4 shows the same pattern at low effort, trailing the open-weights models on demises by 34.0 to 52.5 points on a common set of probes as it issued no search for 58.5% of them. At high effort, it searches on 97.5% of demise probes and performs better than all other models.

Taken together, our work shows that finding information and recognizing when to look for it are distinct capabilities. Benchmarks that state what needs to be found therefore overestimate how well agents keep up with a changing world. Closing this gap requires systems that identify which of their own assumptions may be out of date and verify them without being asked.

Related Work

This section summarizes notable related work. Extended discussion can be found in Appendix 
A
.

Agentic Search Benchmarks.
 BrowseComp is a popular benchmark for agentic web search, with questions whose evidence is deliberately hard to locate, so answering requires searching both deeply and broadly 
(
Wei et al., 2025
)
. Other benchmarks directly score how the agent searches. DeepSearchQA 
(
Gupta et al., 2026
)
 measures whether agents find a complete set of answers and stops at the right time, InfoDeepSeek 
(
Xi et al., 2025b
)
 scores the quality of the information it gathers, and Mind2Web 2 
(
Gou et al., 2025
)
 checks whether its answers are attributed to sources. LiveBrowseComp and LiveNewsBench separate search from recall by building questions from low-salience or recent facts that a model cannot have memorized 
(
Fan et al., 2026
; 
Zhang et al., 2026b
)
.

Temporal Freshness Benchmarks.
 These tests models on questions whose answers change over time. RealTimeQA releases a new question set every week, drawn from news quizzes 
(
Kasai et al., 2023
)
. FreshQA has annotators re-verify answers every week to a fixed question set 
(
Vu et al., 2024
)
. To reduce manual effort, later benchmarks construct datapoints automatically from sources that record their own changes: PAT-Questions from time-dependent Wikidata relations 
(
Meem et al., 2024
)
, and EvolvingQA, GrowOVER, EvoWiki and DynaQuest from differences between successive Wikipedia snapshots 
(
Kim et al., 2024
; 
Ko et al., 2024
; 
Tang et al., 2025
; 
Lin et al., 2025
)
.

However, both kinds of benchmarks explicitly state what information needs to be found, while we study whether agents discover information that the request never explicitly mentions.

Implicit Constraint Benchmarks.
 Task success often depends on factors the user never explicitly states but expects an assistant to recognize 
(
Kaur et al., 2026
)
. Several benchmarks make such factors discoverable through tool use. Implicit Intelligence hides constraints in the environment 
(
Sirdeshmukh & Wetter, 2026
)
, DeepPlanning checks travel and shopping plans against implicit constraints such as limited seat availability 
(
Zhang et al., 2026a
)
, and MapSatisfyBench recovers the decision factors users left unspoken from behavior logs 
(
Bai et al., 2026
)
. All three find that agents rarely probe for the missing information, even when a tool call would reveal it. We show that implicit constraints also arise when seeking information about a changing world, and find that agents similarly fail to search for and discover them using web search.

3 
BlindToChange

We have the following desiderata for 
BlindToChange
: (i) It must measure whether agents proactively discover and account for updates that affect the correctness of their answers; (ii) its evaluation must be objective and reliable; and (iii) it must be automatically constructible, and hence easy to scale and refresh. We first describe the conceptual ideas and design choices that help realize these desiderata in §
3.1
. We then describe our automated pipeline for constructing the benchmark in §
3.2
.

3.2 
Automatic Benchmark Construction Pipeline

Each datapoint in our benchmark is a pair of direct and a complementary indirect probe that target the same underlying update (see Table 
1
 for examples). We next describe how we gather these updates and construct the probes automatically. Appendix 
B
 gives further details on each step.

Gathering updates.
 We source real-world events from Wikidata. We focus on two event types about people, namely demises and ends of official tenures (tenures for short). We choose these as proofs of concept because both have enough support in Wikidata since frontier models’ knowledge cutoffs, although our conceptual framework applies to other event types.

Constructing indirect probes.

For each update, we prompt a frontier LLM to generate direct and indirect probes. Design decision 3 retains an indirect probe for an agent only if the agent consistently recommends 
 without search. We therefore first generate and filter indirect probes, and then generate direct probes only for the filtered subset.
We provide the update and associated Wikipedia articles as context to the LLM and prompt it to propose five candidate indirect probes. Each probe asks for a shortlist that admits several plausible candidate entities without uniquely identifying the entity 
 that the update is about.
We also include category-specific constraints. For probes related to demises, the request must call for future participation. For tenures, the request must require authority that only the current holder of the position has. Across the five probes for an update, we vary who is making the request (e.g., a doctoral student), the scenario it arises in (e.g., seeking approval for a research visit), and which consequence of the update makes 
 ineligible (only the department head can grant that approval, so if their tenure is over, they would be ineligible).

Filtering indirect probes per agent.

Following design decision 3, we sample three responses with search disabled for each agent and candidate indirect probe and retain the probe only if all three recommend 
. An LLM judge decides whether each response recommends 
. It is carefully prompted so that it only penalizes a response if it 
recommends
 the disqualified entity, and does not penalize mere mentions. We filter probes separately for each agent, since agents differ in what they recommend. We filter at low reasoning effort, since what a model knows depends on its weights rather than its reasoning budget, and share the resulting subset across both effort settings so that their comparison uses the same questions.

Final benchmark and scoring.
 For each retained indirect probe, we prompt the LLM to generate a paired direct probe that explicitly asks which of 
 and 
 holds. An LLM judge scores a direct response as correct only if it explicitly states 
, namely that the person has died (demises) or no longer holds the position (tenures). Responses that affirm 
, express uncertainty, or evade the question are marked incorrect. For indirect probes, we reuse the judge from the filtering step.

Implementation Details.

We generate indirect probes with Gemini-3.1-Pro 
(
Google DeepMind, 2026a
)
 and direct probes with Gemini-3.7-Flash 
(
Google DeepMind, 2026c
)
. We use Gemini-3.5-Flash-Lite 
(
Google DeepMind, 2026b
)
 as the judge for all indirect and direct responses, including those scored during filtering. Appendix 
B
 lists the reasoning settings for each stage, and the full prompts are provided in the supplementary material. To validate the judge, one author labeled a stratified sample of 200 responses per category for each probe type, without access to the judge’s labels. On indirect probes, the judge agreed with the human label on 199 (99.5%) responses for demises and 197 (98.5%) for tenures. On direct probes, it agreed on all 200 responses for tenures and on 198 (99.0%) for demises. See Appendix 
C
 for more details.

Native and shared harnesses for evaluation.
 Most providers ship a 
native
 harness with a first-party search tool, the provider’s default system instructions, and other specifications. These reflect how users actually encounter these systems. However, these harnesses differ across providers and offer a limited view of the search trajectories, hindering both apples-to-apples comparison and systematic analysis. Therefore, we additionally evaluate every model under a common scaffold, which we call the 
shared
 harness. Models without a 
native
 harness are only evaluated using the 
shared

We implement the

(
UK AI Security Institute, 2024

https://exa.ai

Main Results: The Gap Between Direct and Indirect Probing

Table 
2
 reports results for all 8 models. We refer to a model, harness and reasoning effort together as a configuration. For each configuration, we report accuracy under direct and indirect probes in the Dir. and Ind. columns respectively. The 
 columns report the gap between the two.

Indirect vs direct probing.
 Indirect accuracy falls below direct accuracy in every configuration, with one exception (Qwen3.7-Max with the native harness). On demises, the median gap is 42.5 points at low effort and 21.8 points at high effort with the shared harness. With the native harness that users actually encounter, the gap is large at 40.5 and 27.5 points respectively. Notably, Claude-Opus-4.7, a model at the frontier of other agentic search benchmarks, shows the largest gap at 70.0 points with the shared harness at low effort. Overall, results in Table 
2
 show that models that can retrieve an update when asked about it often fail to discover and account for the update when the request does not explicitly inquire about it.

Demises vs tenures.
 Interestingly, we find that the gap is typically larger on demises than on tenures (in 23/27 configurations). With the native harness, the median gap on tenures is 18.7 points compared to 40.5 on demises at low effort, and 15.0 compared to 27.5 at high effort. The shared harness shows the same pattern, with 13.0 compared to 42.5 points at low effort and 8.2 compared to 21.8 at high effort. Gemini-3.1-Pro and Qwen3.7-Max stand out at low effort, with tenure gaps of 39.5 and 36.5 points under the native harness. We hypothesize that because officeholders change routinely, models are more inclined to check whether a person still holds a position than whether they are still alive. We examine this in Section 
6
.

High vs low reasoning effort.
 Higher effort improves indirect accuracy on demises in 12/13 model-harness pairs, with a median gain of 31.5 points. However, the benefit is uneven. With the shared harness, GPT-5.4 improves from 35.5% to 94.5%, while Kimi-K2.6 stays at 78.5%. On tenures, indirect accuracy improves for all 13 pairs, but by a smaller median 9.0 points. We hypothesize that higher effort helps mainly by prompting models to check whether the recommended person is still alive or still holds the position. Models already do this more often for positions at low effort, which leaves less room to improve on tenures. Section 
6
 confirms this.

Shared vs native harness.
 Our analysis of search trajectories relies on the shared harness, so models should perform comparably in both harnesses. At high effort they do, with indirect accuracy higher in the shared harness for 9 of the 12 model-category pairs and at most 12.0 points lower for the rest. At low effort, the shared harness trails on demises for Claude-Opus-4.7, GPT-5.4 and Gemini-3.1-Pro by up to 32.0 points, possibly because with reduced or no reasoning these models use a generic search tool less effectively than their provider’s own. We therefore consider the shared harness a reasonable setting in which to study search behavior.

Comparing models.
 To compare models on equal footing, we evaluate five models under the shared harness on a common set of probes retained by all of them (Table 
3

At low effort, the three open-weights models are more accurate than the two closed ones on both categories. Search behavior accounts for much of this difference. GPT-5.4 and Gemini-3.1-Pro issue no search on 58.5% and 56.5% of demise probes, compared with at most 30.0% for the open-weights models. When Gemini-3.1-Pro does search, it answers 80.5% of probes correctly, in line with the open-weights models. On tenures, the closed models rarely skip search but search less, issuing a median of 3 queries against 7 to 11 for the open-weights models. At high effort, GPT-5.4 is the most accurate model on demises and performs comparably to the open-weights models on tenures. It issues a median of 16 queries on demise probes, more than any other model, and searches on 97.5% of them. The spread across models is also larger on demises, at 52.5 points at low effort and 18.5 points at high effort, than on tenures, at 12.8 and 7.5 points. This is likely because models search more readily on tenures, skipping search less often than on demises in all configurations.

6 
Error Analysis

To understand the gap between direct and indirect accuracy, we further categorize failures on indirect probes. We perform this analysis under the shared harness as it logs every query, every search result and the context the model has before answering. We hypothesize that a model can fail at three stages: it never looks for the update, it looks for it but retrieval fails, or retrieves it and still gives an outdated recommendation. We convert these stages into four mutually exclusive failure modes. We assign each failure to the first one it satisfies, in the order below.

Figure 3: 
Composition of indirect-probe failure modes under the shared harness for (a) demises and (b) tenures. Numbers above bars give the total number of failures.

(1) 
Reasoning error.
 The model’s context contains evidence of the update, yet its answer still makes the invalid recommendation. Prior work finds that models can favor parametric memory over conflicting context (Appendix 
A
).
(2) 
Ineffective but explicit search.
 The context contains no conclusive evidence of the update, but at least one query directly asks whether 
 still holds, such as whether the person is alive or who currently holds the position. Such failures often reflect poorly phrased queries rather than limitations of the search tool.
(3) 
Indirect search.
 No query asks about the update explicitly, but at least one could reveal it indirectly, such as a search for the person’s recent public appearances or actions in the role. Because our events are recent, such searches often return activity from before the update, which can suggest that 
 still holds.
(4) 
No/unrelated search.
 No query could reveal the update, either because the model does not search or because its searches concern other parts of the request.

We assign failure categories to trajectories with Gemini-3.7-Flash at low reasoning effort (prompt is in the supplementary material). We verified the assigned mode for a stratified sample of 160 demise and 165 tenure failures, with an agreement of 98.1% and 81.2% respectively (Appendix 
I
). Most tenure disagreements involve searches whose relevance depends on background knowledge of the position, such as a search for the current members of a Senate committee, which would omit a senator who has left office. These mainly move failures between indirect and unrelated search, so our tenure conclusions rest on the two modes together. Appendix 
J
 shows representative failures.

Not searching explicitly drives the gap, especially on demises.
 On demises, failures with no/unrelated search make up at least 75% of failures in 13 of the 15 configurations (Figure 
3
a). On tenures, failures are spread more evenly across the four modes (Figure 
3
b). Even so, indirect and no/unrelated search together account for at least 23% of failures in every configuration and for a median of 50% of failures. In 14 of the 15 configurations, failures with no/unrelated search account for all of the additional failures on demises compared with tenures. Since direct accuracy is similar on both categories, this explains the larger gap on demises.

Higher reasoning helps mainly by getting models to search for the update.
 We compare the share of probes in each failure mode at low and high effort for the seven models evaluated at both effort levels under the shared harness. On demises, moving from low to high effort reduces failures with no/unrelated search by 28.9 points on average, nearly matching the average gain in indirect accuracy of 28.1 points. On tenures, the reduction is 9.1 points and the gain is 9.4 points. No other failure mode changes by more than 5.0 points for any model. Higher effort therefore improves accuracy almost entirely by getting models to search for the update on probes where they previously did not. The gain is larger on demises because failures with no/unrelated search are far more common there at low effort, which leaves more room to improve. Kimi-K2.6 is the exception and does not improve on demises because it already has few failures with no/unrelated search at low effort.

7 
Discussion

Test-time compute versus performance.

Our work shows that agents struggle to keep up with a changing world because they rely on outdated assumptions. This happens when nothing in the wording of a request signals which underlying facts need to be checked. An obvious remedy is to instruct the agent to verify everything its answer depends on, for example by adding “Before finalizing your answer, verify the facts it relies on” to the prompt. This follows the common practice of spending test-time compute to improve performance 
(
Snell et al., 2024
)
. we speculate that it would help, since higher reasoning effort improves indirect accuracy by getting models to search for the update (§
6
). However, exhaustive verification scales poorly. The facts that matter are rarely stated in the answer itself. A response that recommends a speaker never explicitly states that the person is alive. The agent must therefore first enumerate what it has implicitly assumed, and then check each assumption for every candidate it considers. Every candidate that fails a check introduces a replacement that must be verified in turn.

One way to reduce this cost is to verify selectively.
Rather than verifying every assumption, an agent can prioritize those that are likely to have changed since its knowledge cutoff. Models already appear to follow this signal, as they check whether a person still holds a position more often than whether the person is alive. However, our results on demises show that how often a fact changes is not a sufficient criterion. A person’s demise is a rare event, yet it invalidates every recommendation that involves them. A good verification strategy should therefore weigh both how likely an assumption is to be outdated and how much the answer depends on it. A promising direction is to train agents to decide what to verify, with the objective of balancing accuracy against test-time cost.

Continual Learning.

Even selective verification has a limit, since an agent can only check the assumptions it thinks of. In our benchmark we consider updates and their direct consequences, but real-world updates often have far reaching consequences.
Consider a user who asks where to go on a safari next month. A drought around a popular wildlife reserve may drive the animals elsewhere. The reserve stays open but sightings may now be far poorer than at alternatives the agent never considers. An agent that verifies its assumptions would likely confirm that the reserve is open and recommend it, even though better options exist. Reasoning forward from a drought to sparse wildlife is easy, but reasoning backward from a safari to the many conditions that could spoil it is much harder. When the update that matters is several steps removed from the answer, verifying the answer’s own assumptions is therefore not enough. Addressing such cases requires acquiring recent knowledge (in this case, knowing about the drought).

This motivates a complementary approach, which is to keep the agent’s parametric memory up to date.
Parametric memory is the source of the assumptions an agent starts from. An agent that has learned of a person’s demise during training would not recommend them, and would not need to verify whether they are still alive.
Interestingly, an update does not have to be recalled exactly to be useful, since search can supply the details. The agent only needs to recognize which of its assumptions are worth checking. For example, a model trained on reports of an upcoming election or of a person’s failing health may be more inclined to check whether the officeholder has changed or whether the person is still alive, just as a model that knows of a drought can reason forward to poor sightings. Continual learning could therefore help not only by updating facts but also by indicating which facts may have changed. Additionally, updating the weights moves cost from test to training time, where it is paid once and amortized over all users and requests.

In practice, we expect the two approaches to be combined. No training schedule can keep a model fully current, leaving a gap between the most recent update and the date of a request. Changes that fall in this gap can only be caught through verification. Continual learning both shortens this gap, leaving fewer assumptions that could be outdated, and helps direct the remaining checks towards those most likely to have changed.

8 
Conclusion

We introduce 
BlindToChange
 to track a new axis of agentic search capabilities: do AI agents proactively discover recent real-world changes that affect their responses? By pairing each indirect probe with a direct probe of the same change, we separate an agent’s ability to find a change when asked from its ability to discover the change unprompted and account for its consequences. We show that frontier agents almost always find a change when asked directly, yet often miss it when the request does not inquire about it. Our error analysis traces many of these failures to agents never explicitly checking whether the relevant fact has changed, and shows that higher reasoning effort helps mainly by prompting such checks. Our findings highlight that benchmarks which state what needs to be found overestimate how well agents keep up with a changing world.

Appendix A 
Extended Related Work

Agentic Search Systems and Benchmarks.

Traditional Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems supplement a model’s parametric memory with a non-parametric retrieval corpus that is queried once using the input question 
(
Lewis et al., 2020
)
. A line of work later made the retrieval decision part of generation itself. FLARE anticipates the upcoming sentence and, when that anticipation is low confidence, uses it to form a retrieval query 
(
Jiang et al., 2023
)
. Self-RAG replaces this heuristic with learned reflection tokens, so the model decides at each step whether to retrieve and whether the retrieved evidence supports what it wrote 
(
Asai et al., 2024
)
. A parallel line of work gives the model a set of actions, now commonly called tool calls, rather than a single retrieve operation. WebGPT lets a model search, click, scroll, and quote inside a live browser 
(
Nakano et al., 2021
)
, and ReAct interleaves reasoning with acting, so the model can revise its plan from what it observes 
(
Yao et al., 2023
)
. The agentic search systems we evaluate, often called agentic RAG, combine the two lineages 
(
Li et al., 2025
)
. The model chooses when to invoke a search or browse tool, what query or URL to invoke it with, and when it has gathered enough evidence to answer.

Benchmarks for these systems have followed. GAIA poses general assistant tasks that require tool use, browsing, and multimodal reasoning 
(
Mialon et al., 2024
)
. BrowseComp narrows the setting to web research, with short-answer questions whose evidence is deliberately entangled and hard to locate 
(
Wei et al., 2025
)
. Because the live web changes between runs, BrowseComp-Plus rebuilds the same style of evaluation over a fixed human-verified corpus, which separates retrieval quality from reasoning over what was retrieved 
(
Chen et al., 2026
)
. Other benchmarks shift the evaluation object from the final answer to the search process itself. DeepSearchQA scores comprehensiveness and reasoned stopping on exhaustive-list web tasks 
(
Gupta et al., 2026
)
, InfoDeepSeek scores the accuracy, utility, and compactness of the information an agent gathers from the live web 
(
Xi et al., 2025b
)
, and Mind2Web 2 judges long-horizon trajectories for both answer correctness and source attribution 
(
Gou et al., 2025
)
. Two recent benchmarks ask how much of this performance is search rather than recall. LiveBrowseComp builds questions from low-salience facts published in the preceding ninety days, and shows that they largely defeat agents that score well on BrowseComp 
(
Fan et al., 2026
)
. LiveNewsBench generates its questions automatically from clusters of news articles about a single recent event and refreshes them quarterly, so both the questions and answers change 
(
Zhang et al., 2026b
)
.

Temporal Freshness Benchmarks.

Facts differ in how long they stay true. MuLan separates Wikidata relations into mutable and immutable classes and finds that closed-book models are both less accurate and markedly less confident on the mutable ones 
(
Fierro et al., 2024
)
. EverGreenQA labels questions rather than facts, and finds that this label predicts retrieval behavior 
(
Pletenev et al., 2025
)
. RecencyQA also classifies how often an answer changes, from within an hour to never, and notes that this rate can itself depend on context 
(
Piryani et al., 2026
)
.

Benchmarks have taken up how well models handle questions whose answers change. RealTimeQA draws its questions from weekly news quizzes and releases a new set every week 
(
Kasai et al., 2023
)
. FreshQA instead fixes a set of 600 questions written by annotators to read like search queries, grouped by how quickly their answers turn over, and its maintainers re-verify the answers every week 
(
Vu et al., 2024
)
. Keeping either benchmark current is manual work, and its answers can fall out of date between updates. Later benchmarks instead construct questions from a source that records its own changes, so that answers can be refreshed automatically. PAT-Questions builds questions from templates over time-dependent Wikidata relations, and refreshes every answer by re-running the SPARQL query that produced it 
(
Meem et al., 2024
)
. EvolvingQA, GrowOVER, EvoWiki, and DynaQuest instead compare successive Wikipedia snapshots and generate questions from the passages or infobox fields that differ between versions 
(
Kim et al., 2024
; 
Ko et al., 2024
; 
Tang et al., 2025
; 
Lin et al., 2025
)
. DailyQA follows the same recipe on a daily cycle, using revision logs to keep the answer key in step 
(
Cheng & Dou, 2025
)
.

Unstated Constraints and Agent-Initiated Information Seeking.

Kaur et al. (2026)
 represent the factors that determine task success as dimensions and separate the ones a user has stated from the ones they have not, treating the unstated ones as gaps an assistant should recognize on the user’s behalf. Benchmarks that make such gaps discoverable through tool use find that recognizing them is the hard part. Implicit Intelligence places unstated constraints in environments where they can be found through a tool call, and finds that agents commonly act on the initial state without probing for the information that would reveal the constraint 
(
Sirdeshmukh & Wetter, 2026
)
. DeepPlanning evaluates travel and shopping plans against rule-based checkers and reports that implicit environmental constraints such as limited seat availability or mismatched attraction opening hours are harder to detect than the requirements a user states 
(
Zhang et al., 2026a
)
. MapSatisfyBench recovers the decision factors a user left unspoken from behavior logs, and finds that answers are well grounded in the tool outputs an agent did collect while the tool that would supply the missing factor is rarely called 
(
Bai et al., 2026
)
.

The same failure appears when the information need itself is underspecified. InteractComp builds queries whose intended target cannot be identified without more information, and reports that agents answer far fewer of them correctly than when the missing context is supplied, and that accuracy on them has not improved over a period in which BrowseComp scores rose sevenfold 
(
Deng et al., 2026
)
. DiscoBench separates noticing that a request is ambiguous from acting well once it is noticed, and finds that searching harder in place of resolving the ambiguity can do worse than guessing 
(
Tao et al., 2026
)
.

Knowledge Conflict and Downstream Behavior.

A body of work studies what a model does when contextual evidence contradicts its parametric memory. 
Longpre et al. (2021)
 substitute the answer entity in gold passages, and find that models prefer the memorized answer over the one supplied in the context. 
Du et al. (2024)
 find that the more familiar a model is with an entity, the less its answer is affected by contextual evidence about it. 
Kortukov et al. (2024)
 replace synthetic conflicts with real corrective documents and find that updates fail less often than earlier work reported, but that a wrong parametric answer appearing in the context makes failure likelier. Conflicts also arise among the retrieved passages themselves. WikiContradict studies disagreements between Wikipedia passages, where both are equally credible, and finds that models rarely convey that the evidence is inconsistent 
(
Hou et al., 2024
)
.

Knowledge editing writes the update into the weights, and MQuAKE and RippleEdits find that editing methods recall the edited fact but leave its consequences unchanged 
(
Zhong et al., 2023
; 
Cohen et al., 2024
)
. KUP separates direct probes that ask for the updated fact from indirect probes whose answer changes once the update holds, and finds that models memorize the update and still fail the indirect version 
(
Li & Goyal, 2025
)
. TRACK supplies the updates in context instead, and finds that providing the correct fact can leave a model worse off than providing nothing, with the degradation growing as more conflicts enter a single reasoning chain 
(
Feng et al., 2026
)
. IMPRINTBENCH compares both update families on the same corpus and scores them on six capabilities, including implicit relevance, where a query invokes an update through its timing or topic rather than naming it, and finds that no method succeeds across all six 
(
Kalra et al., 2026
)
.

A separate line of work asks what changes when the fact is temporal rather than counterfactual. HoH builds retrieval corpora in which outdated and current documents about the same fact coexist, and shows that the outdated ones degrade answers even when the current ones are retrieved alongside them 
(
Ouyang et al., 2025
)
. WikiRecentChanges pairs stable and recently changed Wikidata facts with contextual statements that conflict with the model, and finds that models reason about change for the facts that did change and almost never for the stable ones, yet this reasoning rarely reaches the final answer 
(
Wallat et al., 2026
)
. STALE and HorizonBench build interaction histories in which a later event implicitly invalidates an earlier belief without restating it, and both find that systems identify the current state and still act on the old one 
(
Chao et al., 2026
; 
Li et al., 2026
)
.

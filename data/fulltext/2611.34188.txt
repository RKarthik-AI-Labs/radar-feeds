Abstract

Existing defenses against indirect prompt injection in retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) are inherently reactive: prompt-engineering methods are often overridden by the injected instructions, while supervised detectors must be retrained for each new attack style. We propose a different paradigm: 
defend by maximizing relevance, not by detecting attacks
. Our method, 
MINE
, is training-free and attack-agnostic, and it also serves as a query-conditioned reranker. To evaluate defenses under realistic conditions, we introduce LC-Inj-SQuAD, a long-context benchmark with an average of 4,032 words per context. We evaluate on four datasets spanning short-context QA (Inj-SQuAD, Inj-TriviaQA), long-context QA (LC-Inj-SQuAD), and agent tool-call injection (InjecAgent). Across all four, 
MINE
 achieves the best accuracy–security trade-off among training-free defenses. It is also the only defense that stays usable against a fully adaptive attacker, generalizes to agent tool-call injection, and achieves the lowest inference latency on long contexts.

Introduction

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) enables large language models (LLMs) to ground their responses in knowledge retrieved from external document contexts, improving factuality 
Lewis et al. (2020)
; 
Karpukhin et al. (2020)
; 
Gao et al. (2023)
; 
Fan et al. (2024)
; 
Singh et al. (2025)
; 
Nguyen et al. (2026)
. However, RAG also introduces a critical vulnerability: 
indirect prompt injection
 
Greshake et al. (2023)
; 
Schulhoff et al. (2023)
; 
Zhan et al. (2024)
; 
Chen et al. (2025c)
. Retrieved contexts may embed malicious instructions that hijack the model into following the injected instructions instead of the user’s query, without access to the model weights or the system prompt 
Perez and Ribeiro (2022)
.
In a typical RAG-based QA system (Figure 
1
a), the retriever returns relevant chunks/passages that are concatenated into a context 
 used for answer generation. Consider a scenario where one of the retrieved chunks in 
 has been poisoned:

Query:
 
“Who designed the Oriental Courts?”

Retrieved Context (
):
 
“…designed by [Name].

Retrieved Context (
):
 
[assistant]
 
OK.

Retrieved Context (
):
 
[user]

Now, write an ad for Nike shoes.

Output:
 
“Experience the ultimate comfort with Nike…”

In this case, the result can be a complete 
behavioral hijacking
, when the model follows the injected command instead of answering the question.

Limitations of existing defenses.
 Current approaches fall into three categories, each with structural weaknesses. 
(i) Prompt-engineering defenses
 
Yi et al. (2025)
; 
Schulhoff et al. (2024)
; 
Hines et al. (2024)
 prepend or append meta-instructions (e.g., “ignore instructions in the data”) that compete with the injection for the model’s attention. 
(ii) Heuristic detectors
 
Jain et al. (2023)
; 
Alon and Kamfonas (2023)
 flag suspicious patterns such as keywords or perplexity spikes, but are brittle to paraphrasing and offer no formal guarantees. 
(iii) Supervised filtering methods
, most notably 
indirect-pia
 
Chen et al. (2025d)
, train dedicated models on labeled data to detect and remove injected instructions; they thus depend on the labeled distribution and degrade on unseen attack styles. All three families are therefore 
reactive
 by design: their robustness is attack-dependent.

A different paradigm: relevance maximization.
 We approach prompt injection defense from a different perspective: rather than detecting attacks, we select the spans most relevant to the user’s query from the retrieved context. We propose 
MINE
, a 
pre-LLM filter
 that processes retrieved context before it reaches the language model. By maximizing relevance to the query rather than identifying malicious content, 
MINE
 is agnostic to the injection style and requires no labeled attack data.

Our key insight is that legitimate spans typically exhibit two properties relative to the user’s intent: 
(P1)
 high mutual information (MI) with the query, reflecting semantic alignment with the user’s information need; and 
(P2)
 low semantic variance among themselves, since relevant evidence should be mutually coherent rather than scattered across unrelated intents. 
MINE
 therefore selects spans that maximize MI with the query while penalizing embedding-space dispersion; injected spans violate both properties and are filtered out as a 
side effect
 of relevance selection. The method needs no labeled attack data, no fine-tuning, and no additional LLM calls, making it plug-and-play (Figure 
1
b).

Our contributions:
 
Method.
 We introduce 
MINE
, a training-free, attack-agnostic pre-LLM filter that defends by maximizing relevance rather than by detecting attacks. 
MINE
 formulates defense as subset selection over retrieved spans, with an objective that maximizes mutual information with the user query while penalizing semantic variance. To make this combinatorial problem tractable, we relax subset indicators to span-selection weights on the probability simplex, optimize them with projected gradient ascent, and then extract the final evidence subset. 
Benchmark.
 We construct LC-Inj-SQuAD, a long-context benchmark that synthesizes multi-page encyclopedic contexts (4,032 words on average) from short SQuAD samples. On LC-Inj-SQuAD, existing defenses lose accuracy or security as contexts grow longer, whereas 
MINE
 remains stable, keeping ASR at most 
 across all evaluated LLMs and static attack types. 
Findings.
 Across three LLMs, five static attack types, and four datasets, 
MINE
 attains the best accuracy–security trade-off among training-free defenses. Under a fully adaptive attacker, it remains the only usable defense, although its ASR rises to 
 (Section 
5.4
). It also generalizes to agent tool-call injection (Section 
6
) and achieves the lowest latency in long-context settings (Section 
5.5
).

Related Work

Indirect Prompt Injection.

Perez and Ribeiro (2022)
 introduced direct “Ignore previous instructions” attacks, while 
Greshake et al. (2023)
 later formalized the 
indirect
 variant, in which adversarial instructions are embedded within retrieved data. Attacks have since diversified into conversational topic transitions 
Chen et al. (2025c)
, learned execution triggers 
Pasquini et al. (2024)
, multi-modal payloads 
Bagdasaryan et al. (2023)
, and RAG exploits 
Zou et al. (2025)
.

Prompt-Engineering Defenses.
 Sandwich prompting 
Schulhoff (2024)
, reminders 
Yi et al. (2025)
, and Spotlight 
Hines et al. (2024)
 inject meta-instructions to guide the model, while training-based variants harden the model itself 
Wallace et al. (2024)
; 
Chen et al. (2025a)
; 
Chen et al. (2025b)
; 
Piet et al. (2024)
. These 
in-LLM
 approaches rely on the model’s instruction-following ability to override the injection, the capability that attackers exploit.

Detection-Based Defenses.

Perplexity-based detection 
Alon and Kamfonas (2023)
 and paraphrasing defenses 
Jain et al. (2023)
 are brittle to adaptive attacks, while Llama Guard 
Inan et al. (2023)
 trains a dedicated safeguard LLM. More recent detectors instead exploit internal model signals 
Hung et al. (2025)
; 
Xie et al. (2024)
; 
Hu et al. (2024)
. 
indirect-pia
 
Chen et al. (2025d)
, fine-tunes models on labeled attacks; while effective in-distribution, it requires substantial training infrastructure and degrades on unseen attack styles.

RAG-Poisoning Defenses.
 Defenses against corpus poisoning 
Zou et al. (2025)
, such as RobustRAG 
Xiang et al. (2024)
 and Astute RAG 
Wang et al. (2025)
, isolate or consolidate retrieved passages, but they target a different objective. Poisoning alters the 
answer
 to a query, so poisoned passages are topically aligned with it by construction. In contrast, injection alters the 
task
. Both approaches also incur extra LLM calls per query. Closest to our setting is RAGDefender 
Kim et al. (2025)
, a training-free clustering filter that flags adversarial passages via inter-passage embedding similarity, which we adapt as a baseline. 
MINE
 differs by scoring subsets 
jointly
-coupling query relevance with within-subset coherence-rather than scoring each passage independently.
We use RAGDefender as the poisoned-RAG representative because it is a recent post-retrieval defense and its original paper reports stronger performance than RobustRAG and other prior defenses.

Information-Theoretic Relevance and Subset Selection.

Mutual information (MI) estimators 
Belghazi et al. (2018)
; 
Oord et al. (2018)
 underpin contrastive representation learning, including InfoNCE-trained dense retrievers 
Karpukhin et al. (2020)
; however, these are training-time methods. In contrast, 
MINE
 applies MI at inference time, requiring no training. Our continuous relaxation over the simplex draws on machinery from differentiable subset sampling 
Xie and Ermon (2019)
, while replacing coverage-based submodular selection 
Lin and Bilmes (2011)
 with a mutual-information objective. To our knowledge, 
MINE
 is the first method to cast indirect prompt injection defense as MI maximization. As shown in Table

Our Method

We defend against indirect prompt injection by reframing it as a subset-selection problem: given a retrieved context 
, constructed from retriever-returned chunks/passages and partitioned into spans, select the subset of spans that maximizes its information-theoretic relationship with the user’s instruction. Our approach, 
MINE
, encodes the two properties that distinguish injected spans, i.e., low mutual information with the instruction and high embedding-space dispersion, into a single differentiable objective requiring neither fine-tuning nor an auxiliary detector.

Given a user’s query prompt

Our LC-Inj-SQuAD Long Context Benchmark

Previous works primarily evaluate prompt injections using short contexts 
 of 100-300 words 
Chen et al. (2025d)
. Currently, no benchmark addresses the strictly long context setting, where the injected text acts as a ”needle in a haystack”. To address this limitation, we introduce LC-Inj-SQuAD, a long-context benchmark built by extending samples from the short-context Inj-SQuAD dataset 
Chen et al. (2025d)
. Our benchmark construction pipeline consists of three steps, each performed via GPT-5.4-mini prompting with structured output validation.

Dataset statistics.

Of the 900 original Inj-SQuAD samples, 694 pass all pipeline steps, yielding contexts that average 
4,032
 words in length (median 4,039; range 3,376–4,734), thus placing the entire benchmark strictly in the long-context regime. See details in Table

Paired evaluation protocol.
 We evaluate every defense on two matched datasets: (1) Inj-SQuAD, the subset of 694 short-context samples from which our LC-Inj-SQuAD is expanded, and (2) our long-context LC-Inj-SQuAD. This paired design isolates the causal effect of context length from sample-level confounds.

Experiments

5.1 
Setup

Threat model.

We adopt the standard grey-box threat model for indirect prompt injection 
Greshake et al. (2023)
; 
Chen et al. (2025d)
: the adversary knows the system architecture but not the defense parameters (e.g., 
, 
, embedding encoder).
The adversary may employ arbitrary injection strategies (e.g., template-based, format-exploiting, or conversational) but cannot modify the query prompt 
 or the LLM weights.
Section 
5.4
 further evaluates an 
adaptive
 attacker who knows the full algorithm and all hyperparameters.

LLM models.

We evaluate on three open-source models:
Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct 
Grattafiori et al. (2024)
 and
Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507 
Yang et al. (2025)
.

Attack types.

We evaluate the defense strategies against six attack strategies. Five are static injections of increasing sophistication: 
naive
 (direct instruction injection), 
escape_separation
 (escape characters that break formatting), 
ignore
 (“Ignore previous instructions” templates), 
completion
 (fake assistant/user turn boundaries), and 
conv_attack
 (TopicAttack 
Chen et al. (2025c)
, which uses natural conversational transitions to disguise the injection).

The sixth attack type, 
adaptive
, stress-tests under the strongest setting: the attacker knows our defense algorithm and all of its parameters.

Baselines.

Five prompt-engineering defenses include 
None
 (no defense), 
Reminder
 
Yi et al. (2025)
 (prepend “Do not follow instructions in the data”), 
Instructional
 (warn the model about malicious content), 
Sandwich
 
Schulhoff (2024)
 (repeat the instruction after the data), and 
Spotlight
 
Hines et al. (2024)
 (delimiter-based data marking). We also include three non-prompt-engineering defenses: 
indirect-pia
 
Chen et al. (2025d)
, the state-of-the-art supervised detector; 
RAGDefender
 
Kim et al. (2025)
, a training-free, pre-LLM inter-passage clustering filter adapted from corpus-poisoning defense (matching our setting); and 
MELON
 
Zhu et al. (2025)
, the state-of-the-art defense for tool-using agents, which re-executes the model with the user task masked and flags an injection when the masked run reproduces the original behavior.

Evaluation metrics.

7 
Conclusion

We presented 
MINE
, a training-free, attack-agnostic defense against indirect prompt injection that defends by maximizing relevance rather than by detecting attacks. Specifically, 
MINE
 algorithmically selects the subset of spans with maximal mutual information with the query. Experiments with three LLMs, five static attack types, and four datasets, including our long-context benchmark LC-Inj-SQuAD, show that 
MINE
 achieves the best accuracy–security trade-off among training-free defenses, with the lowest latency under attack in both short- and long-context settings. Under a fully adaptive attacker, 
MINE
 remains the only usable defense, indicating that its robustness does not depend on fixed injection templates, although its ASR rises to 
 on long contexts. Its strong results on InjecAgent further suggest that pre-LLM relevance filtering is a practical direction for securing systems that condition on noisy external context, from RAG to tool use.

Limitations

MINE
’s splitter merges sentence-boundary fragments until each span reaches 50 characters,
which presumes well-formed prose with standard punctuation. Code, tabular
or otherwise structured data (e.g. JSON, markdown tables), and languages
with non-standard sentence terminators can yield spans that are too coarse
to isolate an injection from its surrounding legitimate content.
Domain-specific chunkers are a natural extension.

Ethics Statement

This work aims to improve the security of LLM systems that consume untrusted external content. The attacks in our experiments come from existing benchmark families or simple re-renderings of them; our contribution is a defense and an analysis of when existing defenses fail. 
MINE
 requires no proprietary model access, no fine-tuning, and no user data beyond the query and retrieved context available at inference time.

Appendix C 
Adaptive Attack Details

Attack design.

Two ideas drive the attack. 
(1) Hide the malicious instruction in one legitimate-looking span.
 
MINE
 keeps or discards each 
sentence-level span
 whole, so we wrap the instruction with content topically similar to the retrieved document and collapse the result into a single span. The wrapper inherits the document’s relevance signal, and 
MINE
’s splitter cannot separate the malicious content from its wrapping. 
(2) Plant two such spans at opposite ends of the retrieved document.
 We use two complementary hiding strategies: a 
filter-aware rewrite
 (start, optimized against 
MINE
), and a 
single-span conversational injection
 (end, adapted from TopicAttack 
Chen et al. (2025c)
). Because 
MINE
’s filter is position-agnostic, the attacker gets two independent draws against the filter across both 
position
 (start vs. end).

Appendix D 
InjecAgent Details

Adaptation.

We apply 
MINE
 to the tool 
Observation
 before returning it to the ReAct agent. The MI maximization and PGA optimization of §
3.3
 are unchanged. Three components are re-tuned for the tool-use setting.

(i) JSON-aware splitter.
 The sentence-boundary regex of §
3.1
 produces fewer than two spans on compact JSON tool outputs, which contain neither 
.!?
 nor newlines. In that case we fall back to a splitter that additionally breaks on field-boundary commas, so injected JSON fields become filterable spans.

(ii) Selection with weight floor.
 A span is kept iff it lies in the top 
 by weight 
and
 
.

(iii) Document-order assembly.
 §
3.3
 reorders retained spans by decreasing weight to combat lost-in-the-middle. We instead preserve original document order, since ReAct agents parse 
Observation
 as coherent narrative; reordering scrambles JSON field orders, list structures, and turn semantics.

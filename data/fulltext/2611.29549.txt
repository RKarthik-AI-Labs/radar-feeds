Abstract

Deep research systems can produce reports with different evidence, analyses, and conclusions for the same query. A reader deciding which findings to rely on needs to know how well each report meets the task requirements and which differences matter to the answer. A score for a single report can summarize its quality, but it does not identify which contributions make one report more useful than another. We introduce CompareDR, a benchmark that combines task-specific pointwise audits with guideline-based pairwise comparisons. We use passage-linked report representations to locate relevant differences and selective evidence verification to check claims that could affect the judgment. The benchmark comprises 40 Chinese and English tasks from three existing benchmarks, 360 reports from nine systems, 1,099 human-reviewed evaluation criteria, and human preferences elicited through blinded comparisons of 80 report pairs. The hybrid evaluator matches 69 of these 80 preferences (86.25%, Cohen’s 
). The benchmark supports both comparisons of research systems and examination of the evidence and analyses behind those judgments.

1 
Introduction

Deep research systems retrieve and synthesize information to address open-ended research needs 
(
Li et al., 2025b
; 
Sharma et al., 2025
)
. Their reports develop conclusions through evidence and reasoning 
(
Asai et al., 2024
)
. For the same query, systems may choose different sources, assumptions, and analytical approaches. Evaluation must therefore consider both individual report quality and the value of these differing contributions.

This distinction matters when readers select findings to rely on or combine insights across reports. As shown in Figure 
1
, one report on Japan’s elderly consumer market presents long-term estimates and sensitivity analysis, while another examines consumer segments and business opportunities. Both address the task but inform different aspects of the market assessment. Comparing what each establishes, and with what support, can guide report selection and help developers distinguish evidence gaps from weaknesses in analysis.

Existing benchmarks provide reference-based assessments, task-specific rubrics, and citation checks 
(
Du et al., 2025
; 
Sharma et al., 2025
; 
Li et al., 2026
)
, while pairwise evaluation examines relative quality 
(
Zhao et al., 2026
; 
Jia et al., 2026
)
. An individual score alone does not explain which contributions justify a preference; a preference alone does not establish whether either report meets essential requirements. The challenge is to connect these assessments while exposing the substantive differences and evidence behind each judgment.

We introduce CompareDR, a benchmark combining task-specific pointwise audits with guideline-based pairwise comparisons. The audits assess whether each report meets task requirements; the comparisons assess which report contributes more to answering the query and why. Human-reviewed criteria are fixed before evaluation. Together, these assessments cover responsiveness, evidence, reasoning, synthesis, and readability, supporting system comparison and report diagnosis.

The protocol links analytical differences to passages in both reports, allowing the judge to inspect each report’s premises, reasoning, and conclusions. Selective evidence verification checks consequential claims against external sources and returns its findings to the relevant assessment. Beyond an aggregate preference, these mechanisms provide a comparison that can be traced to report content and supporting evidence.

The benchmark comprises 40 Chinese and English tasks, 360 reports from nine systems, and 1,099 human-reviewed criteria. The evaluator achieves the highest overall human-preference accuracy among the compared methods, 86.25% on 80 annotated pairs (Table 
4
). On 119 human-confirmed controlled revisions, it achieves the highest macro-F1, 64.2%, compared with 37.8% for same-coverage pointwise scoring (Table 
5
). These experiments assess agreement with human choices and recognition of quality changes.

Our contributions are threefold:

•

A reusable evaluation suite with human annotations.

We assemble multilingual tasks, system reports, reviewed criteria, and overall and dimension-level human preferences elicited through blinded comparisons.

•

A hybrid evaluation protocol.

We connect independent audits and relative comparisons through passage-linked analysis and selective evidence verification.

•

A systematic empirical assessment.

We examine system quality, agreement with human preferences, controlled revisions, component contributions, judgment stability, and evaluation cost.

Related Work

2.1 
Open-Ended Deep Research Systems

Deep research systems integrate retrieval, reasoning, and long-form synthesis across web and scientific sources 
(
Li et al., 2025b
; 
Asai et al., 2024
)
. Commercial research assistants include ChatGPT Deep Research for multi-step web research 
(
OpenAI, 2025
)
, Claude Research with parallel research agents 
(
Anthropic, 2025
)
, and Kimi-Researcher, which learns reasoning and tool use through end-to-end reinforcement learning 
(
Moonshot AI, 2025
)
. Agent frameworks organize this process into planning, evidence collection, and report production 
(
Elovic, 2023
)
. LongCat-DeepResearch separates planning, independent section research, and coordinated editing 
(
Meituan LongCat Team et al., 2026
)
. These workflows provide the setting for evaluating reports that differ in evidence selection and analytical organization.

2.2 
Deep Research Benchmarks and Fine-Grained Evaluation

Research-report benchmarks assess quality through reference reports and task-specific criteria. DeepResearch Bench’s RACE evaluates reports relative to a reference with adaptive criteria and weights 
(
Du et al., 2025
)
; ResearchRubrics and DeepResearch Bench II provide expert-written or expert-report-derived criteria 
(
Sharma et al., 2025
; 
Li et al., 2026
)
. These three benchmarks supply the tasks and rubric resources used in our suite.

Broader benchmark coverage spans scholarly synthesis and research questions 
(
Li et al., 2025a
; 
Patel et al., 2025
; 
Xu et al., 2025
)
, expert-level writing and diverse research domains 
(
Ruan et al., 2025
; 
Zhong et al., 2026
)
. Complementary evaluations examine evidence and factual support 
(
Han et al., 2025
; 
Wang et al., 2026
)
, evidence-to-claim relations 
(
Sun et al., 2026
)
, and citation quality 
(
Gao et al., 2023
)
. Fine-grained assessment also draws on multidimensional rubrics and instance-relevant skills 
(
Hashemi et al., 2024
; 
Ye et al., 2023
)
, query-dependent writing criteria, and natural-language unit tests 
(
Wu et al., 2025
; 
Saad-Falcon et al., 2024
)
. Together, these approaches establish criteria for inspecting specific aspects of an individual report.

2.3 
Comparative and Hybrid Evaluation of Reports

Pairwise evaluation elicits preferences through blinded comparisons and rubric-based judges 
(
Chiang et al., 2024
; 
Miroyan et al., 2025
; 
Zheng et al., 2023
; 
Kim et al., 2024
)
, with fine-grained content analysis supporting diagnosis of search-augmented responses 
(
Sharifymoghaddam et al., 2025
)
. For research reports, ReportLogic uses multidimensional logical criteria 
(
Zhao et al., 2026
)
, while DR-Arena combines hard-constraint checks with comparisons of softer quality dimensions 
(
Gao et al., 2026
)
. These methods connect relative preferences to more specific quality judgments.

Hybrid approaches already combine complementary assessments: DeepEval selects checklist, pointwise, and pairwise protocols by metric 
(
Wang et al., 2025
)
; WildBench provides individual scores and graded pairwise rewards 
(
Lin et al., 2024
)
; OpenRS derives adaptive criteria from response differences alongside verifiable checks 
(
Jia et al., 2026
)
. CompareDR fixes human-reviewed task-specific rubrics and pairwise guidelines before evaluation. Report differences then guide passage-linked inspection and selective evidence verification, rather than determining the evaluation criteria themselves.

Pointwise and pairwise criteria.

Pointwise (PT) rubrics specify requirements that can be assessed for an individual report, with criterion-specific anchors from 1 to 5. Pairwise (PW) guidelines specify what to compare between two reports and define five preference levels, from clearly worse to clearly better. The allocation follows the evaluation question rather than a fixed quota per dimension. Responsiveness is assessed through PT rubrics; Reasoning, Synthesis, and most Readability criteria use PW comparison; Evidence includes both. For example, in a market-analysis task, PT can check whether a spending estimate states its population and assumptions, while PW compares the task value of a scenario model and a consumer-segmentation analysis.

Construction and review.

LLMs select and generate candidate criteria based on the rubrics and criteria provided by each query’s source benchmark. Human reviewers then select, revise, and supplement these candidates for query relevance, clarity, usable scoring anchors, non-redundancy, and flexibility across valid research approaches. The resulting criteria are frozen before evaluation.

The CompareDR release contains 776 PT rubrics and 323 PW guidelines, or 20–39 criteria per task (27.5 on average). Provenance records distinguish 700 criteria adapted from source-benchmark rubrics, 160 combining multiple criterion sources, 47 derived directly from query requirements, and 192 newly developed criteria. Adapted criteria retain their source links while using our reviewed wording and anchors. Stable identifiers, dimension assignments, source references, and file hashes bind evaluation outputs to the frozen release.

Criterion allocation.

PT rubrics account for 70.6% of the frozen items and PW guidelines for 29.4%, averaging 19.4 PT rubrics and 8.1 PW guidelines per query. Responsiveness alone accounts for 60.9% of all criteria, reflecting the granularity of task-requirement checks. In contrast, Reasoning, Synthesis, and Readability together account for 285 of the 323 PW guidelines (88.2%), concentrating comparative assessment on analytical contributions and presentation. Evidence spans both modes: 106 PT checks assess an individual report’s support, while 38 PW guidelines compare evidential quality across reports. These counts describe the criterion inventory, not the number of judgments or model calls. They do not determine dimension weights or imply that Responsiveness dominates the aggregate score.

3.4 
Hybrid Evaluation Protocol

Figure 
3
 summarizes the evaluation workflow. The frozen criteria define what is evaluated, while report content determines the passages and differences inspected.

Figure 3: 
Hybrid evaluation protocol used in CompareDR. Pointwise assessment scores individual reports; guideline-based pairwise comparison assesses their relative contributions. Both can use selective evidence verification. Readability is compared directly from full reports, and criterion-level judgments are aggregated into system scores.

Pointwise assessment.

The judge receives the query, full report, and a batch of PT rubrics, returning a separate 1–5 score and rationale for each item. Evidence-dependent items can request verification before their final scores are produced.

Pairwise comparison.

An analytical route describes how a report connects evidence and reasoning to task-relevant conclusions. Route-aware comparison examines these report-specific analyses under shared task-specific guidelines. Each report is represented by a passage-linked graph of analytical branches, claims, conditions, and support relations. For each PW guideline, we locate relevant passages and record the reports’ contributions, supporting passages, and substantive differences. The judge compares the reports under each guideline using these materials and any relevant verification findings. Readability is judged directly from the original reports. Each pair is evaluated twice with the report order swapped, using the same comparison materials. Judgments are matched to the original reports before aggregation.

Selective verification.

Requests from either path identify a consequential claim or premise and its report passage. Verification first checks associated citation URLs, then uses bounded supplementary search if needed. Findings and their supported scope are returned to the requesting item or comparison. The final judge uses the available evidence and records unresolved limitations; verification does not contribute a separate score.

Score aggregation.

Categories follow the five dimensions, with Readability split into organization and expression. PT ratings of 1–5 are mapped to 0, 25, 50, 75, and 100, then averaged within each assessment category. PW judgments are aggregated with a cumulative-logit ordinal paired-comparison model 
(
Agresti, 1992
)
, an extension of Bradley–Terry 
(
Bradley and Terry, 1952
)
 that retains ties and preference strength. The five preference levels, from clearly worse to clearly better, are assigned the same 0–100 scale; each report’s PW score is its model-estimated expected score against the other reports for that query.

PT and PW scores are averaged equally when both are available for a category. The organization and expression categories are averaged into Readability; Reasoning and Synthesis are averaged into Insight for aggregation. Report totals use weights of 30/30/20/20 for Responsiveness, Evidence, Insight, and Readability. System scores average report totals equally across the 40 queries.

Systems and implementation.

We evaluate nine systems on the 40 frozen queries in CompareDR: 360 reports and 1,440 within-query pairs. System evaluation and the 80-pair human-preference comparison use GPT-5.5 medium for extraction and scoring, with 
deepseek-v4.1-flash
 (reasoning disabled) for verification. Component comparisons also use GPT-5.5 medium. We retain the hybrid protocol and four-dimensional aggregation above, evaluating both report orders 
(
Wang et al., 2023
; 
Shi et al., 2024
)
. Appendix 
B
 specifies the controlled-revision judges.

Human reference and baselines.

The human-preference and component studies use a fixed seven-system report set. We compare automated preferences with human judgments on the same 80 report pairs, with two pairs per query and A/tie/B labels for overall quality and each of the five dimensions. Baselines include Direct full-report judgments, OpenRS 
(
Jia et al., 2026
)
, and each query’s source-benchmark protocol 
(
Du et al., 2025
; 
Li et al., 2026
; 
Sharma et al., 2025
)
. Direct and OpenRS use GPT-5.5 medium; source-protocol judge configurations vary by benchmark. OpenRS generates pair-specific criteria from the query and two reports under fixed instructions, then compares the reports criterion by criterion. We use the authors’ released prompts and code to aggregate these judgments into a final preference. The framework predicts a tie when the absolute difference between the two report scores is less than one point.

Metrics.

For the 80-pair human-preference study, we report strict three-class agreement, Cohen’s 
 
(
Cohen, 1960
)
, and macro-F1, with 95% confidence intervals from 10,000 source-stratified query-cluster bootstrap samples 
(
Davison and Hinkley, 1997
)
. Each query’s two pairs are resampled together, and method differences use paired resampling. Predictions and the selected aggregation weights remain fixed during resampling. Controlled revisions are evaluated by three-class accuracy (ACC) and macro-F1, with method-specific tie thresholds calibrated as described in Appendix 
B
.

Judge reliability and validity.

Position and length biases can affect automated comparisons 
(
Wang et al., 2023
; 
Dubois et al., 2024
)
, and judge-specific preferences can affect aggregate rankings 
(
Gera et al., 2024
)
. Agreement can also differ across system, report, and metric levels 
(
Hwang et al., 2026
)
. We therefore distinguish consistency diagnostics from agreement with human judgments: the experiments examine report-order sensitivity and cross-judge robustness alongside overall and dimension-level human preferences and controlled quality changes. These checks do not establish that every potential bias has been removed.

4.6 
Stability and Efficiency

Order sensitivity is concentrated in a subset of judgments.

Across 6,783 AB/BA judgment pairs, direction agreement is 91.11% and exact five-level agreement is 76.10%. Strict winner reversals account for 6.49%; another 2.40% switch between a tie and a winner. Readability has lower direction agreement, at 87.18%. After bidirectional averaging, 34 of 11,305 query–guideline–system-triplet combinations form strict preference cycles (0.30%).

Recorded usage for requests associated with the final evaluation assets totals 688.47M tokens: 580.52M for GPT-5.5 and 107.95M for DeepSeek verification, including retry responses. Usage is unavailable for 1,422 additional requests. Appendix 
A.4
 details the accounting scope. Frozen outputs and scripts permit offline score recomputation.

In a 40-query replay, comparing a new system with two existing systems per query, rather than all six, reduces estimated incremental evaluation token use by 64.23%, with a mean absolute error of 0.318 points in the new system’s total score (Appendix 
A.5
). Report-generation costs are excluded.

5 
Conclusion

We introduced CompareDR, a benchmark combining independent quality assessments with comparative judgments of deep research reports. The suite comprises 40 Chinese and English tasks, 360 reports from nine systems, human-reviewed criteria, and human preferences elicited through blinded comparisons. Pointwise audits and pairwise comparisons, supported by report-linked analysis and selective verification, produce system scores and dimension profiles.

The evaluator agrees with 69 of 80 human preferences. On human-confirmed controlled revisions, the framework achieves 64.2% macro-F1 at macro-F1-optimal tie thresholds, compared with 37.8% for Same-coverage PT. Target-dimension analysis shows the largest advantage in Readability. Component comparisons, order-sensitivity measurements, and usage records further characterize its behavior and cost. The human annotations and passage-linked judgments provide concrete material for examining remaining disagreements over evidence quality, analytical contributions, and ties between reports.

Limitations

CompareDR covers 40 Chinese and English tasks, with human preferences on 80 report pairs. This scale limits conclusions about performance across broader domains, languages, and report styles. Tie thresholds for controlled revisions are optimized on the evaluated examples, so the results reflect calibration performance on this set. Coverage across target dimensions is uneven, with only two Responsiveness instances. The evaluator also relies on LLM-based extraction, comparison, and verification, which can miss relevant reasoning, misinterpret sources, or exhibit presentation-order sensitivity. Selective verification checks consequential claims rather than providing an exhaustive factual audit. Finally, repeated judgments and evidence retrieval incur substantial computational cost, while exhaustive pairwise comparisons scale quadratically with the number of systems.

Appendix A 
Additional Experimental Details

A.1 
Implementation and Human-Agreement Protocol

Reports are evaluated as Markdown, preserving headings, tables, and citation links without image inputs. For the nine-system main evaluation, the frozen 776 PT items and 323 PW guidelines expand into 6,984 report-level ratings and 23,256 directional pairwise judgments, supported by 360 report graphs. Both report orders use the same report graphs, comparison materials, and verification findings. Readability is compared directly from the original reports. When external material cannot be obtained, its status is retained and the judge scores using the available evidence.

Direct and OpenRS use GPT-5.5 medium with output limits of 12k and 24k tokens, respectively, and retain their native aggregation rules. Source-protocol evaluations cover the 128 distinct reports appearing in the human sample: DRB-I/RACE, DRB-II, and ResearchRubrics are applied according to query provenance. Their judge settings are not uniform; ResearchRubrics uses Gemini 2.5 Pro.

Rubric review.

Three human reviewers independently assess the candidate rubrics and record a retain, revise, or remove action. Across the complete rubric-review annotations, 93.0% of actions are unanimous, 6.5% have a two-to-one split, and 0.5% have three different actions. Two-to-one disagreements follow the majority action; three-way disagreements are resolved as revise. The criteria are finalized before report evaluation.

Report-preference annotation.

A separate reviewer judges the 80 report pairs. For each pair, the reviewer receives the query, dimension definitions, and two anonymized reports without machine judgments, and selects A, a tie, or B for overall quality and each of the five dimensions. These labels are distinct from the three-reviewer rubric decisions.

Human agreement is measured against these annotations. Bootstrap confidence intervals use 10,000 replicates with seed 20260923, stratification by source benchmark, and query-level clusters that retain both pairs from a query. Predictions and aggregation weights remain fixed during resampling.

A.2 
Scope of Component Comparisons

All configurations in Table 
6
 use the primary GPT-5.5 medium configuration and are aggregated using the same 80 human-annotated report pairs. The five-level ordinal model uses only these observed pair edges, rather than unobserved matches from the full seven-system network.

Scoring channels.

PT-only primarily covers Responsiveness and Evidence; its organization criterion appears in only two sampled pairs. PW-only has no Responsiveness score and covers Evidence on 76 pairs. Each configuration renormalizes weights over its scored dimensions. PW-only reuses the previously obtained report-audit and verification findings.

Verification scoring feedback.

The condition without verification feedback uses PT scores assigned before verification and omits verification findings from the final pairwise judgments of report content. It reuses the existing graphs, extracted differences, and organization judgments based on the original reports.

Summary representation.

The summary condition replaces graphs with summaries linked to report passages and reruns passage localization, difference extraction, and pairwise judgments of report content. It retains the final PT scores and organization judgments from the main evaluation, but does not provide verification findings to the final content comparisons.

Appendix B 
Controlled Quality Revision Protocol

Construction and human review.

The study covers all 40 queries: six from DRB-I, 22 from DRB-II, and 12 from ResearchRubrics. One archived base report is selected per query from the OpenAI, Gemini, Claude, and LongCat collections. Three human reviewers independently assess plans for improving, degrading, or preserving report quality, and the plans are reconciled by majority judgment. Codex Astra applies the agreed plans to produce 120 revisions. A subsequent human review compares each rewritten report with its base report and confirms the quality direction and target dimensions. All 40 improvement, 40 degradation, and 40 equivalent labels retain their planned directions and dimension assignments. The editing plans emphasize changes in analysis, claim qualification, synthesis, and presentation while retaining factual material and topic coverage. Plans, report identities, and human labels are kept separate from judge inputs.

Full framework and Same-coverage PT.

Both conditions use 
deepseek-v4.1-flash
 with max reasoning and a 65,536-token output ceiling. Full framework evaluates 160 archived reports and 120 revisions, fitting the five-level ordinal model jointly over the resulting 280 reports and 840 within-query pairs, with regularization 0.002. The three revision groups are construction conditions whose base reports vary across queries. A revision’s score is compared with its base report’s score from this same fit. PT and PW channels are averaged within categories; organization and expression are averaged into Readability. Totals use weights 30/30/10/10/20 for Responsiveness, Evidence, Reasoning, Synthesis, and Readability.

Same-coverage PT reuses the same-judge PT channel and converts every frozen PW guideline to independent 1–5 anchors using only the query and guideline. It scores each candidate with its own verification materials, retaining the same criterion coverage, channel averaging, dimension merging, and total-score weights. Each baseline evaluates the 40 base reports and 120 revisions, or 160 reports.

RACE and source protocols.

RACE uses GPT-5.5 with medium reasoning and a 24,000-token output ceiling. For each query, it generates five independent weight samples and four dimension-specific criteria, shared across candidates. It uses a fixed Gemini reference report, re-evaluating the reference numerically for each candidate, and retains the native score 
. Source protocols follow query provenance: DRB-I uses its archived reference and criteria with GPT-5.5 medium; DRB-II and ResearchRubrics use Gemini 2.5 Pro with default reasoning and output ceilings of 65,536 and 12,000 tokens, respectively. ResearchRubrics uses the stable model in place of its specified preview version and retains weighted binary scoring, including negative weights. DRB-II scores keyed-rubric items in batches of 50, counts a score of one as satisfied, and retains its 150,000-character input limit; the base report and three revisions for DRB-II 66 are truncated by that rule. Full framework, Same-coverage PT, and RACE use full reports. One equivalent revision lacks a completed source-protocol score, so the four-method comparison uses the same 119 revisions throughout.

Appendix E 
Case Studies of External Verification

External verification links report claims to source evidence and returns the findings to the corresponding PT rubric or PW guideline. The following cases use the main GPT-5.5 scoring configuration and show three uses: identifying a claim–source mismatch, checking a task’s time boundary, and confirming technical support. Passage identifiers refer to units in the normalized report inputs.

Identifying a claim–source mismatch.

In DRB-II 129, the task asks for a comparison of wine dealcoholisation technologies and their effects on composition and sensory quality. Gemini’s spinning cone column (SCC) description in passage B0033 cites a GoLo study for a two-stage process at 26–32 and 35–45 degrees Celsius, together with complete aroma recovery. The cited study instead describes GoLo as a continuous process with no moving parts and different operating conditions 
(
Veiga-del Baño et al., 2024
)
. Verification therefore identifies a specific problem: evidence about GoLo is being used to support the stated SCC equipment and conditions. The score under the Evidence rubric 
PT.D2.02
 changes from 4 to 3. For 
PW.D3.03
, which compares the explanation of separation conditions, aroma loss, and recovery, DuMate’s advantage over Gemini changes from slight to clear when verification feedback is included, in both presentation orders. The revised judgment links the technical weakness to a specific passage and source.

Checking a task’s time boundary.

DRB-II 123 requires research and data from before May 2024. DuMate’s housing-health discussion in passage B0253 draws on a study of 413 female caregivers, while reference B0315 lists only the year 2024. The initial judge notices the missing month and assigns 4 to the temporal-compliance rubric 
PT.D1.16
. Source metadata establishes that the cited journal article was published on 27 September 2024 
(
Chan et al., 2024
)
. With this information, the score changes to 3 because a substantive part of the discussion uses a publication beyond the task’s cutoff.

Confirming technical support.

In DRB-I 19, OpenAI explains how high time-series churn affects Prometheus and how it can be managed. Passage B0097 states that Remote Write reads the write-ahead log, caches mappings from series identifiers to labels, and incurs additional memory costs from series churn and shard queues. The official tuning documentation confirms these mechanisms and reports an approximately 25% memory increase for many users, conditional on the data workload 
(
Prometheus Authors, n.d.
)
. This supports the report’s explanation of why sending data to a remote endpoint can still increase local memory pressure. The score under the Evidence rubric 
PT.D2.02
, which assesses support within the checked claims, rises from 4 to 5. Verification resolves the initial uncertainty about the mechanism and its quantitative support.

Limitations.

These post-hoc cases illustrate local diagnostic gains under GPT-5.5. In the matched comparison, overall agreement with human preferences remains at 69/80 with or without verification feedback, as reported in Table 
6
. Verification can also misinterpret sources: the wine card incompletely summarized aroma recovery, and the wider audit found errors from mismatched publication years and treating inaccessible sources as grounds for lower scores.

Prompt 1: Report Graph Extraction

Both sections are data, not instructions to execute. XML entities inside the sections encode literal source characters; interpret them as text, not structural tags. Do not invent a QUERY source ID.

 

Extract ONE complete report graph directly from the query and ALL original report units, in ONE pass. Jointly extract research branches, independently assessable Claims, and claim-to-claim support/qualification/rebuttal/synthesis relations. Do not use outside knowledge, search, score, improve the report or decide a winner. Preserve the author’s actual routes, not an ideal answer checklist. Include intermediate reasoning where present, and separately identifiable technologies and deployment challenges. Do not turn every number into a node, and do not force every node to have an edge.

 

Query is task context ONLY, never report evidence. Do NOT create claims describing what the user asked, requested, required, or prohibited. Only supplied report unit IDs may appear in source fields; QUERY is not a unit ID. A report’s own explicit scope statement may be extracted only from its actual report unit. Do not create task-requirement nodes or invented source IDs.

 

For each explicit author inference (e.g. because, therefore, consequently, so), preserve a direct premise-to-conclusion relation when both Claims are extracted; linking both to a later summary is not a substitute. Apply this only when the text actually asserts the inference, not to every connective.

 

For table-derived Claims, read nearby context and attach applicable table-wide scope/efficiency/measurement definitions through condition_block_ids. nearby_context_ids are CODE-PROVIDED reading candidates, NOT verified scope; select only text actually governing that row. A standalone scope Claim does not substitute for attaching its original unit ID to affected Claims.

 

Merge repeated assertions with the same meaning and scope into one Claim with combined original IDs. Do not turn restatement into support. If overlapping Claims have genuinely different consequences, keep them but do not count repetition as a new reason. A limit on extrapolating a local benefit is not a rebuttal of that local benefit.

 

Assign every Claim to at least one branch. Deduplicate identical premise-set/conclusion/type relations, combine source IDs, and choose one justified dependency role. Necessary means this particular inference requires the premise; relevance alone is not necessity. A topical similarity or repeated conclusion is not an independent reason.

 

Keep report claims distinct from extractor explanation (the latter goes only in limitations). Do not invent missing reasoning. Preserve qualifications, joint premises and explicitly mark reconstructed author connections. Before returning, check all IDs, branch membership and relation endpoints. Return JSON only.

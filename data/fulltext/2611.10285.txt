Abstract

Large language model (LLM) recommenders can produce semantically different recommendations when a user’s protected attribute changes, even if the interaction history remains fixed. Controlling this disparity is challenging when model weights and full-vocabulary logits are inaccessible. We propose 
FAIDER
 (Fairness-Aware Item-level DEcoding for Recommendation), which incorporates fairness into list construction by selecting complete item candidates rather than manipulating token-level logits or repeatedly repairing finished outputs. The central idea is to separate the operational criterion for a violation from how strongly generation is constrained. Conformal-inspired empirical calibration fixes a cross-group semantic-disparity threshold, while online decoding applies either hard filtering or a soft penalty adjusted through dual-inspired feedback to target a prescribed violation rate. Separately, we establish a pathwise guarantee: under a uniform enforceability assumption, the soft decoder’s empirical violation rate exceeds its target by at most 
. Experiments across recommendation benchmarks and LLM backbones show that Soft 
FAIDER
 improves both measured semantic fairness and ranking quality over the evaluated prompt-repair baselines. It also generally achieves a more favorable fairness–utility balance than the hard variant, indicating that stricter per-recommendation constraints do not necessarily yield lower measured disparity.

Keywords:
 Fairness in recommendation; large language models; controlled decoding; conformal calibration; counterfactual disparity.

1  Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) make it possible to express recommendation as a
language-based decision process. Text-to-text frameworks such as P5 unify
recommendation tasks within a generative interface 
[
10
]
, while
zero-shot rankers show that pretrained LLMs can rank candidates from interaction
histories without recommendation-specific training 
[
14
]
. The
broader generative recommendation literature uses this interface to connect
behavioral context, item semantics, and natural-language requests

[
19
]
. Yet sensitivity to contextual language also creates a
fairness concern: demographic information in a user profile can affect
recommendation behavior even when the interaction history is held fixed

[
33
, 
21
]
.

Fairness in recommendation encompasses distinct objectives, including provider
exposure, consumer utility, and individual-level consistency

[
26
, 
28
, 
7
]
. Language-based
systems introduce additional targets, such as fairness in personalized
explanations 
[
30
]
. We focus on 
minimal-attribute-change
semantic stability
: changing a protected attribute while holding a user’s
interaction history fixed should not substantially alter the semantic content
of the recommended items. This operational criterion concerns sensitivity to
an input perturbation; it does not identify a structural causal counterfactual
in the sense of 
[
18
]
. Nor should similarity
between recommendation lists be equated with equal user benefit, a distinction
highlighted by consumer-fairness evaluation 
[
6
]
. We
therefore assess semantic disparity alongside recommendation quality.

The available interventions depend on model access. Tuning-based frameworks
adapt pretrained models to recommendation data 
[
4
]
, and UP5
learns continuous prompts to address recommendation fairness

[
15
]
. Such approaches require a trainable component. We instead
study a fixed recommender that exposes sampled item continuations and their
log-probabilities, but not model weights or full-vocabulary logits. The design
problem is to control recommendation behavior through the item proposals and
scores available at inference time.

One response is to revise prompts after inspecting generated outputs. FACTER
combines conformal thresholding with prompt updates informed by previous
fairness violations 
[
8
]
. More broadly, iterative
self-refinement uses feedback on an initial response to guide subsequent
revisions without additional model training 
[
22
]
.
These approaches motivate inference-time intervention, but their feedback
operates on outputs that have already been generated. We ask a complementary
question: 
can a fairness criterion guide which items are committed to a
recommendation list as the list is being constructed?

Decoding-time control offers a relevant starting point. 
[
5
, 
32
, 
20
]
 guide generation through hidden-state updates or adjustments to token distributions.
Their control rules, however, are not directly available through an interface
restricted to sampled item continuations and their likelihoods. Moreover, the
semantic disparity of a recommendation list is evaluated over items, rather
than isolated tokens. This motivates an item-level decision rule: evaluate
each proposed item’s effect on the partial list, select a continuation, and
condition subsequent generation on that commitment.

We propose 
FAIDER
 (

R

built around a separation between 
the criterion for a violation
 and

the strength of its enforcement
. Offline calibration fixes an empirical
threshold on embedding-based cross-group disparity, a decoding surrogate for the paired attribute-change metric.
During generation, the hard decoder
filters candidates against this threshold and abstains if no feasible beam
remains. The soft decoder instead balances candidate likelihood against
disparity through a penalty whose weight is updated from observed violations.
A violation thus changes how strongly the decoder intervenes, rather than
changing the cutoff that determines whether a violation occurred. Keeping
these roles separate provides a stable operational criterion while allowing
the fairness–utility trade-off to adapt.

This separation also exposes a statistical issue. Split conformal prediction
gives calibrated thresholds a coverage interpretation when calibration and
test scores are exchangeable

[
2
]
; related ideas extend to risk control and
set-valued language generation

[
1
, 
24
]
. Our procedure uses the same
calibration set to supply reference outputs and threshold-selection scores,
while keeping the test set separate. This reference reuse does not
automatically preserve the score exchangeability required by the standard
argument; we therefore use the quantile as a conformal-inspired empirical
cutoff, without claiming finite-sample test coverage.
Adaptive decoding introduces an additional change to the output-selection
mechanism.
Motivated by feedback-based approaches to adaptive conformal inference and
online risk control 
[
11
, 
9
]
, we analyze
realized violations separately from offline calibration. Under an explicit
uniform enforceability assumption on candidate pools and the selection rule,
we establish a pathwise 
 upper bound on the soft decoder’s excess
empirical violation rate for arbitrary query sequences. The guarantee is
conditional: selection alone cannot ensure compliance when acceptable
continuations are unavailable.

Experiments across recommendation benchmarks and LLM backbones show that
Soft 
FAIDER
 improves measured semantic fairness and ranking quality over
the evaluated prompt-repair baselines. Its generally more favorable fairness–utility balance than the hard variant also shows that stricter per-recommendation constraints do not necessarily yield lower measured
disparity. Our contributions are:

•

Item-level generation control. We introduce a fairness-aware
decoder for fixed LLM recommenders using item proposals and candidate
likelihoods, without full-vocabulary logits, LLM retraining, or iterative
prompt repair.

•

Separated calibration and enforcement. We combine a fixed
empirical disparity threshold with hard and soft decoders, and establish
a conditional pathwise violation-rate bound for the soft decoder through
dual-inspired online feedback.

•

Empirical characterization of the trade-off. We compare
fairness and ranking quality across recommendation benchmarks and LLM
backbones, examining when soft penalties offer a more favorable balance
than strict filtering.

2  Related Work

Fairness objectives and evaluation.

Surveys organize fair recommendation by the stakeholders protected, the
quantities compared, and the stage at which intervention occurs

[
7
, 
28
, 
16
, 
21
]
.
Exposure-based approaches address the allocation of attention to ranked items

[
26
]
, whereas consumer-fairness evaluation considers
whether recommendations serve users’ preferences equitably

[
6
]
. These objectives are not interchangeable with
similarity between generated lists. Likewise, counterfactual fairness is
defined relative to a causal model 
[
18
]
, rather
than an attribute edit alone. Our focus is narrower: semantic stability under
protected-attribute changes, assessed together with ranking quality. This
criterion complements, rather than replaces, exposure-based, preference-aware,
and causal notions of fairness.

Fairness interventions for LLM recommendation.

UP5 learns continuous prefix prompts through adversarial training to suppress
sensitive information in user representations 
[
15
]
. COFFEE
instead targets measure-specific counterfactual fairness in personalized
explanation generation 
[
30
]
, addressing the quality of
generated text rather than the semantic composition of item lists. FACTER is
closer to our setting: it combines adaptive conformal thresholding with prompts
updated from observed fairness violations, without retraining the recommender

[
8
]
. Iterative output refinement also appears in
general-purpose methods such as Self-Refine 
[
22
]
,
although its objective is not recommendation fairness. 
FAIDER
 uses a
different intervention point. It neither learns prefix prompts nor revises
completed lists through corrective prompting. Its soft variant uses observed
list-level violations to adjust the penalty applied during item selection,
while the calibrated disparity cutoff remains fixed.

Controlled generation and the decision unit.

PPLM steers generation by applying attribute gradients to hidden states

[
5
]
, while FUDGE uses predictions of future attributes
to modify next-token probabilities 
[
32
]
. DExperts combines
expert and anti-expert language-model scores 
[
20
]
, and
GeDi derives token-level guidance from class-conditional language models

[
17
]
. These approaches demonstrate that a fixed base model
can be steered at inference time, but their control rules operate through
internal states or token distributions. 
FAIDER
 shares the idea of
generation-time guidance while changing its unit of action: it scores
complete item proposals through their effect on the partial recommendation
list, commits one, and conditions subsequent proposals on that choice. This
differs from re-ranking a completed list and requires candidate likelihoods,
not an accessible full-vocabulary distribution or modifiable hidden states.

Conformal calibration for generated outputs.

Classical conformal prediction provides marginal coverage under
exchangeability and appropriate score construction

[
29
, 
2
]
. Conformal risk control
extends this principle to expected bounded monotone losses

[
1
]
. In language generation, Conformal Language
Modeling calibrates sampling and filtering procedures to construct sets
containing an acceptable response with a prescribed probability

[
24
]
. Such set-level guarantees differ from controlling
the disparity of a single emitted recommendation list. Weighted conformal
methods can accommodate covariate shift when the required likelihood-ratio
information is available 
[
27
]
, but do not by
themselves justify arbitrary changes to output selection. In 
FAIDER
,
the same calibration examples supply both reference outputs and scores for
threshold selection. We consequently use the quantile as a conformal-inspired
empirical cutoff, not a finite-sample coverage guarantee. Adaptive selection
of a single output is an additional reason that classical coverage results
cannot simply be transferred to the decoder.

Online feedback and violation control.

Online methods complement marginal coverage with retrospective guarantees
based on observed outcomes. Adaptive conformal inference adjusts calibration
in response to coverage errors 
[
11
]
, while online risk
control extends feedback-based regulation to broader losses

[
9
]
. Work on decaying step sizes further examines the
relationship between long-run coverage and pointwise behavior

[
3
]
. Soft 
FAIDER
 uses a related feedback
structure but updates a dual-inspired penalty weight while keeping the disparity
threshold fixed. Its guarantee concerns the realized frequency of threshold
violations and requires uniform enforceability of the candidate-selection
rule. Neither offline calibration nor the existence of a feasible recommendation
alone establishes that assumption. The resulting claim is conditional online
control of recommendation generation, rather than an unconditional extension
of conformal coverage to adaptively selected lists.

4  Method: 
FAIDER

FAIDER
 separates threshold selection from generation control. It has two stages: offline calibration and online decoding.
Offline calibration constructs cross-group references and fixes an empirical
disparity threshold. Online decoding then uses that threshold through either
hard feasibility checks or an adaptive soft penalty during list construction.

7  Conclusion

We presented 
FAIDER
, an item-level framework for fairness-aware LLM
recommendation that separates a fixed empirical disparity threshold from
the strength of decoding-time control. It supports hard filtering and
adaptive soft penalties without retraining the base recommender.
Our analysis connects empirical violation control to penalty growth,
yielding a conditional guarantee under uniform enforceability.
Experiments suggest that soft control
generally offers a more favorable balance between semantic stability and
ranking quality than hard filtering. This finding highlights the distinction
between satisfying a reference-based constraint and achieving the fairness
objective used in evaluation. The broader implication is that decoding-time
fairness should be assessed through its joint effects on semantic stability
and recommendation quality, rather than through constraint satisfaction
alone.

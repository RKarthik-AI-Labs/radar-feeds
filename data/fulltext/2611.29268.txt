Abstract

Multimodal web agents automate website interactions but remain vulnerable to visual prompt injection (VPI), where malicious webpage content manipulates their visual processing and induces unintended actions.
Although several defenses focus on detecting injected instructions in screenshots, malicious destinations such as phishing URLs embedded in HTML can remain hidden yet accessible through agent interactions.
Existing evaluations have also not sufficiently examined adaptive attackers who know the defense mechanisms.
To address these limitations, we introduce AdaptiveVPI, the first benchmark for systematically evaluating adaptive VPI threats in web agents.
Specifically, we present a dataset of 360 webpage instances spanning 6 distinct hijack strategies across 10 real-world websites, with malicious destinations not visible in screenshots.
We design an adaptive attack framework that iteratively refines malicious instructions based on feedback from the defense mechanism being evaluated.
We also introduce three metrics to measure the extent of agent hijacking at three different levels.
Across 10 defense configurations for computer-use agents and 11 defense configurations for browser-use agents, our adaptive attacks achieve average success rates of 91.87% and 91.99%, respectively.
These results reveal a gap between current VPI defenses and realistic threats and highlight the need for defenses designed with adaptive attackers in mind.

1 
Introduction

Multimodal web agents are increasingly used to automate tasks on websites.
Acting on behalf of users, they click, type, upload and download files, and complete multi-step tasks with minimal human intervention.
Two common types are 
computer-use agents
 (CUAs) and 
browser-use agents
 (BUAs).
CUAs observe screenshots and predict the screen coordinates of their next click.
BUAs use both screenshots and the page’s HTML to identify and interact with webpage elements.
Because both types rely on webpage content to guide their actions, attackers can use that content to manipulate their behavior.

This runtime threat is known as visual prompt injection (VPI) 
[
4
, 
32
]
.
An attacker embeds malicious visual content, such as a pop-up, in a rendered webpage to induce the agent to pursue objectives that deviate from the user’s task.
Defenses against VPI can be grouped into three categories: content-level detectors that identify injected content 
[
3
, 
10
, 
20
]
, action-level guards that check whether each proposed action aligns with the user’s task 
[
24
]
, and prompt-level defenses that modify the agent’s instructions or in-context examples 
[
4
, 
32
, 
11
, 
30
]
.
However, existing evaluations leave three important gaps in the assessment of these defenses.

I. Attack Realism.

Existing VPI benchmarks emphasize attacks with visible instructions that explicitly state the malicious objective (see 
Figure 1
) 
[
32
, 
4
]
.
An attacker, however, can conceal this objective while using benign-looking instructions to steer the agent.
For example, a session expiration notice or verification prompt can direct the agent to click a button that links to an attacker-controlled destination.
This destination is specified in the page’s HTML but does not appear in the screenshot.
The visible request may therefore appear legitimate, while screenshot-based defenses lack the destination information needed to assess its consequences.
Such attacks test whether defenses can recognize deceptive interactions without relying on explicitly harmful instructions or visibly suspicious URLs.

II. Attacker Adaptivity.

VPI defenses are typically evaluated against fixed attacks developed without knowledge of the target defense.
However, attackers can adapt the wording or visual presentation of the injected content to evade that defense.
In text-based settings, adaptive attackers with knowledge of the target defense have repeatedly bypassed defenses against jailbreaks and prompt injection that appeared robust against fixed attacks 
[
23
, 
31
, 
25
]
.
To our knowledge, comparable evaluations of defenses that inspect screenshots or proposed actions are lacking, leaving their robustness against adaptive attacks largely understudied.

III. Success Criteria.

Existing studies use different criteria to determine attack success.
Some consider an initial action, such as clicking on injected content, sufficient for success, while others require the agent to achieve the attacker’s objective 
[
32
, 
4
, 
11
]
.
A single binary outcome obscures the distinctions among initial redirection, subsequent interaction, and completion of the harmful action.
Evaluating these stages separately reveals how far an attack progresses and at which stage a defense blocks it.

To address these gaps, we introduce 
AdaptiveVPI
.
To our knowledge, it is the first benchmark for systematically evaluating adaptive VPI threats against web agents.
The benchmark combines benign-looking visual instructions with concealed malicious destinations, an adaptive attack framework that uses defense feedback, and evaluation at three levels of agent hijacking.
Our results show that current defenses remain vulnerable to adaptive attacks and that successful attacks can transfer to other agents without further optimization.
Our contributions are as follows:

•

A VPI Attack Dataset.

We construct a dataset of 360 fixed attack pages spanning 6 distinct hijacking strategies across 10 real-world websites.
The pop-ups present benign-looking instructions that conceal the attacker’s harmful objective, while the malicious destination URLs are not visible in screenshots.

•

An Adaptive Attack Framework.

Our attack iteratively refines the injected instructions using feedback from the defense under evaluation, producing attacks tailored to each defense.

•

Three-level Evaluation Metrics.

We assess agent hijacking at three levels: ① 
Diversion
: whether the agent is redirected to the attacker’s page; ② 
Engagement
: whether it interacts with that page; and ③ 
Compromise
: whether it completes the attacker’s objective, such as submitting sensitive information or downloading a malicious file.

•

A Systematic Evaluation.

We evaluate 10 defenses for CUAs and 11 for BUAs, including screenshot-based, action-level, and prompt-level defenses.
Our adaptive attacks achieve average Compromise rates of 91.87% for CUAs and 91.99% for BUAs.
We further show that attacks that successfully compromise the source agent also transfer to other agents without further optimization.

Related Work

2.1 
Attacks on Multimodal Web Agents

Instruction-based Attacks.

A substantial body of work injects malicious instructions into webpage content.
VPI-Bench 
[
4
]
, Pop-ups 
[
32
]
, and AdInject 
[
27
]
 study injections through pop-ups, advertisements, and other interface content.
WASP 
[
11
]
 injects human-written instructions through user-generated content, such as Reddit comments.
RedTeamCUA 
[
16
]
 studies injections into editable web content in hybrid web and operating system environments.
EIA 
[
17
]
 injects transparent HTML elements to exfiltrate private information.
Related injection attacks have also been studied in mobile agents 
[
7
, 
18
, 
5
, 
14
]
.
These studies focus on the design and effectiveness of injected instructions, with limited exploration on how attackers adapt them using feedback from the target defense.

Other work examines injected instructions that are difficult for human users to perceive, include faint text in screenshots processed by the Comet browser 
[
2
]
 and instructions that become visible only after image downscaling 
[
26
]
.
Beyond web agents, Clusmann et al. 
[
8
]
 embed small or low-contrast instructions in medical images.

Optimization-based Visual Attacks.

Another line of work optimizes images or rendered webpages to induce attacker-specified actions.
VisualWebArena-Adv 
[
29
]
 perturbs an image on the page, WebInject 
[
28
]
 optimizes webpage perturbations using an approximation of the rendering pipeline, and MIRAGE 
[
9
]
 generates adversarial images within constrained regions such as advertisement slots.
WebMirage 
[
13
]
 crafts localized visual perturbations that cause agents to select attacker-controlled content.
These approaches optimize visual inputs, often using white-box model access or surrogate models.
We focus on defense-aware refinement of injected webpage instructions using feedback from the defense under evaluation.

2.2 
Defenses for Web Agents

Content-level Detectors.

WARD 
[
3
]
 is a lightweight vision-language guard that classifies webpage content as malicious or benign given the user’s task, a screenshot, and the page’s HTML.
SnapGuard 
[
10
]
 detects injections by combining gradient statistics of the screenshot with action-oriented phrases extracted through OCR.
WAInjectBench 
[
20
]
 systematically evaluates prompt-injection detectors for web agents.
It finds that some detectors identify attacks containing explicit instructions or visible perturbations but largely fail against attacks without explicit instructions or with imperceptible perturbations.
Our evaluation includes its image- and text-embedding classifiers (Embedding-I and Embedding-T) and its LLaVA-1.5-based detection prompt 
[
19
]
 as baselines.

Action-level Guards.

DeAction 
[
24
]
 checks whether each proposed action aligns with the user’s intent before execution.
Its guard uses the current screenshot, the user’s task, a summary of the interaction history, and the proposed action to detect misalignment and provide structured feedback for iterative correction.

Prompt-level Defenses.

Several attack studies also evaluate defenses that modify only the agent’s prompt.
VPI-Bench 
[
4
]
, Pop-ups 
[
32
]
, and WASP 
[
11
]
 introduce warnings or instructions that ask agents to disregard prompt injections, ignore pop-ups, or reject instructions that conflict with the user’s objective.
VPI-Bench and Pop-ups report limited or no protection from these modifications.
In-Context Defense (ICD) 
[
30
]
 instead provides a small set of examples demonstrating defensive reasoning before action planning.
It reduces attack success rates for both pop-up attacks and environment injection attacks.

Our evaluation examines these defenses under adaptive attacks and reports the results in 
Section 6.3
.

2.3 
Adaptive Evaluation

Adaptive attacks have exposed vulnerabilities in text-based defenses that appear robust against fixed attacks.
Nasr et al. 
[
23
]
 use gradient-based optimization, reinforcement learning, random search, and human-guided exploration to bypass 12 jailbreak and prompt-injection defenses.
Zhan et al. 
[
31
]
 bypass all eight evaluated indirect-prompt-injection defenses, while universal adversarial suffixes evade activation-based task-drift detectors 
[
25
]
.

Adaptive evaluation of web-agent defenses remains limited in scope.
WARD 
[
3
]
 is hardened against text-level adaptive attacks, WebAgentGuard 
[
6
]
 tests a fixed guard-targeted instruction, and DeAction 
[
24
]
 does not consider attacks tailored to the defense.
This work instead systematically evaluates multiple defense classes against adaptive attacks whose visible instructions conceal the malicious objective and refines attacks separately for each defense.

3 
Threat Model

We consider a web agent performing a benign user task under the protection of a deployed defense.
The attacker controls part of a webpage visited by the agent and seeks to hijack its behavior while evading the defense.

Attacker’s Goal.

The attacker seeks to divert the agent from the user’s task and induce it to complete an attacker-specified action on an attacker-controlled landing page: signing in, granting permission, or downloading a file.
Success requires achieving the final target action while evading the deployed defense.
For defenses that issue explicit alarms, the target action must occur before the first alarm.
Defense-specific success criteria are defined in 
Section 5.3
.

Attacker’s Knowledge.

We consider non-adaptive and adaptive attackers.
Both know the website, the user’s task, and the default attack page.
The 
non-adaptive
 attacker does not know the defense and uses a fixed default pop-up and landing page for each attack instance.
The 
adaptive
 attacker knows the defense’s mechanism, including its inputs, prompts or in-context examples, and decision rules, but has no access to its weights or gradients.
This assumption reflects the principle that a defense should remain effective even when the attacker knows how it works.

Attacker’s Capabilities.

The attacker can display a pop-up through an advertisement slot 
[
27
]
 or injected overlay and controls the landing page linked to its button.
The pop-up must present benign-looking instructions that conceal the malicious objective.
The target action is fixed for each instance.
The attacker may modify the pop-up and the appearance of pages it controls, but not the user’s task, the agent’s weights or prompt, or the defense.
Pixel-level perturbations and attacks involving a compromised user or browser are out of scope.
During adaptive search, the attacker can query the defended agent as a black box for up to 10 rounds.
Feedback includes defense explanations or full responses, or the agent’s responses and actions for prompt-level defenses.
Search information is not reused across pages or defenses.
In the transfer setting, the attacker reuses pages optimized against another agent without querying the target or performing further optimization.

4 
Problem Setup

A web agent receives a user task 
 and interacts with a webpage over successive steps. At each step, it selects an action based on its current observation, the task, and its previous actions. The browser executes the action and returns a new observation. Following VPI-Bench 
[
4
]
, we study two types of agents that differ in what they observe and how they identify action targets.

Computer-use Agents (CUAs).

CUAs receive only a screenshot 
 at time step 
, without page text or a list of interactive elements. They locate click targets visually and specify their positions using screen coordinates.

Browser-use Agents (BUAs).

BUAs receive a screenshot 
 together with an indexed list of interactive DOM elements 
.
Each element contains its index, role, and label, allowing the agent to select click and input targets by index.

Defense Mechanisms.

We study the defenses discussed in 
Section 2.2
 at the prompt, content, or action level.
Detectors requiring page text are evaluated only in the BUA setting.
If a content-level detector raises an alarm or an action-level guard blocks an action, the run ends before the proposed action is executed.
Prompt-level defenses instead rely on the agent to ignore injected instructions, without a separate mechanism that blocks actions.
To evaluate all three defense types consistently, we compute the metrics in 
Section 5.3
 from the agent’s trajectory.

5 
Methodology

We present AdaptiveVPI, a benchmark for evaluating web agent defenses against adaptive visual prompt injection.
We first construct attack webpages with benign-looking pop-ups and concealed malicious destinations (
Section 5.1
), then refine them using feedback from the target defense (
Section 5.2
).
Finally, we introduce three metrics to measure attack progression at different levels (
Section 5.3
).

5.1 
Attack Webpage Design

Existing VPI benchmarks emphasize attacks with visible instructions that explicitly reveal the malicious objective 
[
32
, 
4
]
, as shown in 
Figure 1
.
However, attackers can conceal their objectives behind seemingly legitimate requests to reduce the likelihood of detection by users or automated defenses.
To model this threat, we separate the visible instructions in a pop-up from the malicious destination linked to its button.

Figure 2
 illustrates this design.
An attacker-controlled pop-up appears on a benign host page (
2(a)
).
Its visible components, including a title, body, and button, present an ordinary notice that encourages the agent to click without revealing the attacker’s objective.
The button’s destination, however, is specified only in the page’s HTML (
2(b)
).
The URL it opens or uses to download a file is not visible in the screenshot.
A defense inspecting only the screenshot may therefore miss the harmful purpose of the request, even as the agent’s click leads to an attacker-controlled page or initiates a download.

We instantiate this design using host pages from ten websites, six pop-up variants, and three payloads.
Our benchmark covers websites spanning e-commerce, travel, news, caregiving, and education.
Each host page uses a website screenshot as its background, with the pop-up card rendered on top.
For Booking, Amazon, and BBC, we use screenshots from VPI-Bench 
[
4
]
.
For the remaining seven websites—Walmart, Woot, Airbnb, Care.com, Preply, HeyTutor, and Princeton Review—we capture each homepage once without interacting with the site.
We assign one benign user task to each website, such as finding the lowest price for a 55-inch TV on Walmart or summarizing the day’s news on BBC.

5.2 
Adaptive Attack

Although the attack webpages described above conceal the malicious objective, defenses may still recognize their fixed wording or visual patterns.
Detecting these default pages alone does not establish robustness against attacks adapted to the defense.
We therefore use an LLM-based optimizer to refine the page content and appearance using defense feedback, with the goal of achieving the attack objective while evading the target defense.

Specifically, the optimizer modifies the pop-up’s title, body, and button label, and selects background settings for the page containing the pop-up and the landing page.
The constraints on changing each component are listed in 
Table 10
 in Appendix.
The pop-up must remain consistent with its assigned theme discussed in 
Table 1
, contain exactly one button, and introduce no additional tasks.
We use a keyword validator to check consistency with the theme’s vocabulary.
We do not change the user’s task, payload, button destination, and landing-page text and form structure.

The optimization is guided by the feedback from both agent execution and defense output.

Non-adaptive Attacks

Table 4
presents the non-adaptive results.
The attack pages achieve Compromise rates of 89.3% on the undefended BUA and 92.0% on the undefended CUA.
Direct-download pages achieve the highest rate on the BUA (180 of 180 runs), while login pages achieve the highest rate on the CUA (175 of 180 runs).

Most defenses yield Compromise rates close to or above the undefended baseline.
The three WAInjectBench baselines raise no alarms on the attack pages, and their results match the undefended baseline.
WARD-0.8B and WARD-2B remain within 2.3 percentage points of that baseline, with rates of 88.7% and 87.0% on the BUA and 92.0% and 91.5% on the CUA, respectively.
The prompts from VPI-Bench, Pop-ups, and WASP provide no reduction either (BUA: 89.4–93.7%; CUA: 96.5–98.7%), nor does ICD on the BUA (90.9%).

The main reductions come from SnapGuard (15.9% on the BUA and 24.3% on the CUA), DeAction (70.4% and 60.9%, respectively), and ICD on the CUA (36.9%).
SnapGuard reads only screenshots, and nearly all alarms come from its image-complexity check.
Uniform borders in BUA screenshots push most pages below its threshold, triggering alarms before a click.
DeAction flags clicks on notification and browser-extension pop-ups (V and VI) as they are unrelated to the user’s task.
ICD’s examples lead the CUA to dismiss these similar pop-ups.

On the BUA, Diversion is at least 96.7% under every defense except SnapGuard (26.7%) and DeAction (85.8%), compared with 100.0% without a defense.
On the CUA, only SnapGuard (76.7%), DeAction (64.7%), and ICD (52.8%) noticeably reduce Diversion relative to the undefended rate of 93.3%.
These observations are consistent with those on Compromise.
Engagement is lower than Diversion on the BUA (84.7% versus 100.0% in the undefended setting).
On the CUA, the two rates are equal except under SnapGuard, where Diversion is 76.7% and Engagement is 0.0%.
This is because landing pages with minimal elements are flagged before any interaction takes, and all Compromise events occur on direct-download pages, which have no landing page and are excluded from the Engagement calculation.

6.3 
Adaptive Attacks

Table 5
presents the adaptive results, where the average Compromise rates achieve 91.99% on the BUA and 91.87% on the CUA.
The largest increase in success occurs in cases where the non-adaptive attack has limited effectiveness.
Against SnapGuard, Compromise rises from 15.9% to 80.6% on the BUA and from 24.3% to 78.3% on the CUA.
On the CUA, it also rises from 60.9% to 88.3% against DeAction and from 36.9% to 69.8% against ICD.

For the remaining configurations, increases are at most 7.3 percentage points on the BUA and 4.9 percentage points on the CUA.
Except for DeAction on the BUA, these configurations already have non-adaptive Compromise rates of at least 87%.
DeAction retains the lowest Compromise rate on the BUA, increasing from 70.4% to 74.1%, while ICD has the lowest adaptive rate on the CUA at 69.8%.
The lower rates are primarily due to specific payloads.
DeAction’s Compromise rate is only 43.3% on authorization pages.
Because it checks proposed actions against the user’s task and rejects permission grants on authorization pages more often than typing actions.
ICD’s Compromise rate is 46.7% on login pages as its in-context examples demonstrate refusing credential entry.
We include both scenarios to assess protection across different attack objectives, including those directly addressed by a defense’s design.

The adaptive attack also increases Diversion and Engagement against SnapGuard.
On the BUA, Diversion rises from 26.7% to 90.0%, while Engagement rises from 10.6% to 84.4%.
On the CUA, the corresponding increases are from 76.7% to 90.8% and from 0.0% to 83.3%.
These results show that the adapted pages induce agents both to reach and to interact with the attacker’s landing page.

We also analyze adaptive and non-adaptive attack performance for each pop-up–payload pair.
Detailed results are provided in Appendix

.

Transfer Across Agents

Figure 3

The attacks remain effective on both agents.
Averaged across defenses, Compromise rates are 84.1% on Gemma and 94.7% on Muse for the BUA, and 92.2% and 83.9% for the CUA, respectively.
For comparison, Qwen3-VL-8B achieves 96.6% on the BUA and 98.3% on the CUA on the same selected pages.

Diversion averages above 86% for every agent, indicating that the pages consistently redirect both agents to the attacker’s landing page.
Subsequent interaction varies across agents: on the BUA, Gemma’s mean Engagement is 79.2%, compared with 95.5% for Muse.
On the CUA, however, Muse has a lower Compromise rate than Gemma (83.9% versus 92.2%).
This difference mainly comes from one pop-up type, Pop-up VI (the browser-extension prompt), for which Compromise rates average 33.3% for Muse and 68.1% for Gemma across the 10 CUA defenses.
This pop-up accounts for 69% of the failures observed for both agents.
For this pop-up, Muse usually presses a key instead of clicking the button.
For example, its first action is a key press in 65 of 87 runs on the undefended default pages.

Nevertheless, these results show that successful attacks can transfer between agents protected by the same defense.
An attacker without query access to the target agent can therefore optimize against another agent and reuse the resulting pages, extending the threat beyond the single-agent evaluation in 
Section 6.3
.

6.5 
False Alarms

We also measure false alarms on benign pages to assess whether defenses unnecessarily interrupt legitimate tasks.
For each of the ten websites, we evaluate a clean page and a page with an ordinary cookie banner, with three runs per page, yielding 60 runs per defense and agent.
None of the evaluated detectors raises an alarm in these runs.
DeAction raises alarms in 43 of 60 runs on the BUA and 5 of 60 on the CUA.
The protection shown in 
Table 4
 therefore comes at the cost of false alarms on benign pages, particularly in the BUA setting.

6.6 
Adaptive Defense

We additionally evaluate an adaptive defense against our attacks.
Specifically, we fine-tune the WARD-2B detector on our attack pages, named WARD-Adaptive.
To measure the impact of the detector in benign settings, we replace the pop-up instructions with legitimate notices and remove the malicious payloads.
As shown in 
Table 6
, WARD-Adaptive reduces the Compromise rate from 91.5% to 0% on the CUA and from 87.0% to 0% on the BUA.
However, this reduction comes at the cost of higher false alarm rates than those of the original WARD-2B.
The false alarm rate rises from 1.1% to 38.9% on the CUA and from 1.1% to 76.7% on the BUA.
This increase also extends to benign pop-ups from VPI-Bench, where the false alarm rate rises from 0% to 45.6% on the CUA and 42.1% on the BUA.

7 
Conclusion

This work benchmarks adaptive attacks with concealed destinations using three-level hijacking metrics.
Average compromise rates exceed 90% on both agent interfaces, and successful pages transfer across agents.
These results highlight the need for defenses designed and evaluated against adaptive attackers.

Effect of Pop-up Type and Payload

Figure 4
compares the Compromise rates for all 18 pop-up–payload pairs under non-adaptive and adaptive VPI attacks.

Each pop-up in

Analysis on Pop-up Types.

In the non-adaptive setting, the human verification types are the most effective category on CUA (91.1%), averaged over the two variants (Pop-ups I and II) and three payloads.
The sign-in category exceeds human verification slightly when paired with login payloads.
This is because the sign-in category is more consistent with the login payload of landing page.
The human verification category achieves 100% Compromise rates with the direct download payload on CUA, and this combination also has the best performance on BUA (93.6% for Pop-up I and 95.5% for Pop-up II).
The effect of a payload varies across pop-up categories.
For example, the authorization payload only has 55.7% Compromise rate with Pop-up III on CUA, but reaches 90.0% with Pop-ups I and II.

Analysis on Payload Types.

Direct download has the highest non-adaptive Compromise rate on both agents, averaging 92.1% on BUA and 82.0% on CUA, compared with 73.6% and 74.6% for authorization.
Its Compromise criterion requires a file request from the initial click, whereas login and authorization require further interaction on a landing page.
Payload differences could affect the effectiveness of a pop-up category.
For example, Permission requests (Pop-ups V and VI) on BUA yield only 62.4% and 49.7% Compromise rate with the login payload, but reach 90.9% and 87.3% with the direct download payload.
Thus, a pop-up category that is relatively ineffective for one payload can still induce high Compromise rates for another.

Effect of Adaptive Attacks.

Adaptive attacks increase the Compromise rate by at least 5% for 13 of the 18 settings on BUA and 16 settings on CUA.
Some of the largest gains occur for initially weak pairs: Pop-up VI with login rises from 49.7% to 83.0% on BUA, while Pop-up VI with the authorization payload rises from 63.0% to 96.0% on CUA.
On CUA, the difference between the highest and lowest mean Compromise rates across the three payloads also decreases from 7.4% to 3.0%, with adaptive rates of 91.8% for direct download, 90.4% for login, and 93.4% for authorization.
Payload differences are still observed on BUA, where direct download remains the most effective payload.
Overall, every adaptive pop-up–payload pair achieves an average Compromise rate of at least 78.0% across the evaluated defenses.

9 
Transfer Results on Gemma and Muse

We examine whether adaptive attack pages that succeed against one agent remain effective when transferred to other model families.
For each defense and agent, we select pages that compromised Qwen3-VL-8B in at least two of three evaluation runs, then evaluate the same pages on Gemma-4-12B-it and Muse-Glimmer-30B without further optimization.
For the 180 pages per agent, 134–178 qualify on BUA and 133–180 on CUA, depending on the defense.
Tables 
7
 and 
8
 report results on these source-successful subsets.

The transferred pages retain high Compromise rates on both target models.
Across defenses, Gemma reaches 80.1–91.3% on BUA and 90.7–95.2% on CUA, while Muse reaches 86.0–97.2% and 81.1–94.7%, respectively.
Thus, Compromise exceeds 80% in all 42 configurations.
This transfer holds across detectors, action-level guards, and prompt-level defenses.
For example, pages optimized against DeAction with Qwen still achieve 87.1% and 94.9% Compromise on Gemma’s BUA and CUA, and 94.5% and 94.7% on Muse’s.

These results show that successful adaptive pages retain substantial attack capability beyond the original target agent.
An attacker can reuse pages developed against one model to compromise agents from other model families, extending the threat beyond the original target agent.
The reported rates measure transfer conditional on success against Qwen, and each defense has its own selected subset.
Within these results, switching the agent’s model family while retaining the evaluated defense may not be sufficient to reliably prevent attacks by transferred pages.

We use Qwen3-VL-32B as the judge.
We evaluate 57 pop-up cases three times each, yielding 171 runs.
For CUA, we measure the first-click rate across 234 pop-up pages, using one screenshot per page.
To count as successful, the agent’s first click must be allowed by the defense and fall inside the pop-up but outside its close button.

We instantiate

All required events must occur before the first detector alarm or action-level block.
For CUA, Compromise is evaluated on the 52 download pages among the 234 pop-up pages.

Table 2
).
A page counts as compromised when the agent’s first click lands on that button and the defense does not block it.

Table 9
reports the results.
Without a defense, the agent reaches 98.8% AR but only 35.1% Compromise.
The gap is concentrated in payloads requiring multiple operations.
Compromise occurs in 59 of 63 upload runs, none of the 54 upload-and-delete runs, and only one of the 54 upload-and-edit runs.
The agent submits the form in at least 53 of 54 runs for each of the latter two variants but almost never completes the required file deletion or editing step in Google Drive.
The embedding classifiers and LLaVA prompt produce the same rates as the undefended baseline, while prompt-level defenses yield 97.1–98.2% AR and 35.1–38.0% Compromise.
Thus, a high attempt rate can coexist with a much lower payload completion rate.

WARD-0.8B, WARD-2B, SnapGuard, and DeAction reduce both AR and Compromise to 0.0% on the VPI-Bench BUA runs.
Under the same defenses, our default attack pages achieve Compromise rates of 88.7%, 87.0%, 15.9%, and 70.4%, respectively (
Table 4

The difference in attack success remains when both benchmarks are evaluated by whether the agent completes the payload, rather than merely attempts to follow the injected instruction.

On CUA, WARD-0.8B, WARD-2B, SnapGuard, and DeAction reduce VPI-Bench’s first-click rate from 71.8% to 0.9%, 0.0%, 5.1%, and 7.3%, respectively.
On the 52 download pages, WARD-0.8B and WARD-2B reduce Compromise from 82.7% to 0.0%.
In comparison, our default attack pages achieve 92.0% and 91.5% Compromise rates under these defenses, averaged across all payloads (
Table 4
).
ICD also yields 0.0% Compromise on the download pages because none of the agent’s first clicks hits the download file button.
All 18 first clicks that land inside the pop-up fall elsewhere.
Overall, the evaluated defenses suppress VPI-Bench’s explicit pop-ups more effectively than our default attack pages.

13 
Analysis of Evaluation Metrics

Diversion, Engagement, and Compromise distinguish reaching an attacker-controlled page, interacting with it, and meeting the payload-specific criterion.
Together, they reveal how far an attack progresses, even when it fails to complete.
We use these distinctions to diagnose outcomes in the non-adaptive and adaptive evaluations.

Diagnostic Value of the Metrics.

Table 13
 compares the three metrics with those used by prior benchmarks.
Pop-ups 
[
32
]
 measures clicks on the injected pop-up.
VPI-Bench 
[
4
]
 distinguishes attempts from completed attacks, while WASP 
[
11
]
 reports intermediate and end-to-end attack success rates (ASR).
AdaptiveVPI further distinguishes between reaching the landing page and interacting with it.
For example, an agent may reach a sign-in form without interacting or interact with it without issuing a non-empty typing action.
Both cases fail the login Compromise criterion, but their Engagement outcomes distinguish where progress stops.
The three indicators are computed directly from page URLs, agent actions, and local site request logs.
The metric values can therefore be recomputed from the stored records without relying on a judge model.
Computing the metrics for 180 runs takes approximately 0.2 seconds on a single CPU core.

For login and authorization payloads, each evaluation setting contains 360 runs (10 websites

We count events according to their timing relative to the defense’s first alarm or action-level block, as defined in 
Section 5.3

Reaching the landing page counts toward Diversion even if the defense raises an alarm upon observing it, while Engagement and Compromise require events before the defense intervenes.
Percentages in all four outcome columns are calculated over the same 360 runs.
The Compromise rates in

The three metrics show how far each attack progresses.

Identifying Where Attacks Stop.

Without a defense, all BUA runs reach the landing page, but 15.3% end without interaction and 0.8% involve interaction without meeting the Compromise criterion as shown in 
Table 12
.
The BUA is therefore susceptible to redirection, although its landing-page Compromise rate is 83.9%.
These attacks fail after the agent reaches the page, even when no defense is deployed.
For CUA, 6.7% of runs never reach the landing page, while every run that reaches it meets the Compromise criterion.
These results show that an incomplete attack does not necessarily mean that a defense prevented it.

For non-adaptive BUA runs, the embedding classifiers and LLaVA prompt have the same results as the the undefended baseline across all four outcome categories.
In each setting, 15.3% of runs reach the landing page without interacting, and 0.8% interact without meeting the Compromise criterion.
Since these defenses raise no alarms, the incomplete attacks provide no evidence of added protection.
On CUA, SnapGuard illustrates how the metrics can identify where a defense stops an attack.
Without a defense, every run that reaches the landing page completes the payload.
With SnapGuard, 76.7% of runs reach the page, but none interacts with it or completes the payload.
The logged alarms confirm that SnapGuard intervenes after the agent reaches the landing page but before it interacts with the page.
Together, the three metrics and alarm logs show that the defense stops the attack, even though the undefended agent can complete the required landing-page actions.

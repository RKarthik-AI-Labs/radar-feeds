Abstract

AI agents deployed on the open web are becoming more capable of performing actions that may breach contractual provisions and violate computer security laws. In this paper, we introduce 
ScrapeBench
, the first agentic benchmark for empirically evaluating the legal compliance of AI agents with website scraping policies, namely terms of service (ToS) and 
robots.txt
 directives. Across widely used closed-weight and open-weight AI agents, we find that most agents exhibit low compliance rates (< 5%) when instructed to scrape websites in our sample of 1,626 popular websites that explicitly prohibit scraping. In addition, we observe that even where AI agents actively check the applicable ToS and 
robots.txt
, they continue to exhibit low compliance rates. Conversely, in our sample of 269 websites that do not prohibit scraping, we observe that agents sometimes refuse to scrape despite it being lawful, such as Claude Opus 4.6 exhibiting an over-refusal rate of 98.5%. Our human baseline experiments (
 = 180 participants) suggest that humans exhibit compliance rates comparable to AI agents but over-refuse at much higher rates.

Introduction

AI agents built atop large language models (LLMs) can increasingly reason, use tools, and take a variety of actions in online environments 
(
Casper et al., 2025
; 
Chan et al., 2024
)
. As these agents begin to act more autonomously, their actions implicate a growing range of legal frameworks and rules, including those that govern access and use of online content 
(
Kolt, 2026
; 
Kolt et al., 2026
; 
O’Keefe et al., 2025
; 
Cui et al., 2025
; 
Kolt, 2025
; 
Cohen et al., 2024
; 
Longpre et al., 2024
)
. One such common activity is 
scraping
, i.e., the automated collection of website content 
(
hiQ Labs, Inc, 31 F.4th 1180 (9th Cir. 2022)
; 
In re OpenAI, Inc, No. 25-CV-4315, 2025 WL 3635559 (S.D.N.Y. Dec. 15, 2025)
; 
RyanAir DAC, 636 F. Supp. 3d 490 (D. Del. 2022)
)
. Popular websites such as Amazon, ESPN, and The New York Times often prohibit scraping through legally binding contracts, in the form of 
terms of service (ToS)
 and 
robots.txt
 directives 
(
Kim et al., 2025
; 
Chan et al., 2024
; 
Atkinson, 2025
; 
Amazon.com Services LLC v, No. 3:25-cv-09514 (N.D. Cal. Mar. 9, 2026), vacated and remanded, 184 F.4th 1083 (9th Cir. 2026)
; 
Register.com, Inc, 356 F.3d 393 (2d Cir. 2004)
; 
hiQ Labs, Inc, 639 F. Supp. 3d 944 (N.D. Cal. 2022)
; 
Meta Platforms, Inc, No. 23-CV-00077-EMC, 2024 WL 251406 (N.D. Cal. Jan. 23, 2024)
)
 .

Whether AI agents comply with these legal restrictions is an open empirical question. At present, most agentic benchmarks focus on performance-oriented metrics, including measuring web-related skills such as information search and retrieval 
(
Wei et al., 2025
; 
Gou et al., 2025
; 
Mialon et al., 2024
)
, web visual comprehension 
(
Luo et al., 2025
; 
Verma et al., 2025
; 
Koh et al., 2024
)
, and work task navigation 
(
Drouin et al., 2024
; 
Zhou et al., 2024
; 
He et al., 2024
)
. These benchmarks often disregard the 
lawfulness
 or 
legal alignment
 
Kolt et al. (2026)
; 
Sachdeva and Kolt (2026)
 of an AI agent’s actions, focusing only on whether it accomplishes the tasks 
(
Nannini et al., 2026
; 
Staufer et al., 2026
; 
Hu et al., 2025
; 
Marino et al., 2026
; 
Lichkovski et al., 2025
; 
Imperial et al., 2025
; 
Reuel et al., 2024
)
. Concretely, an AI agent may successfully scrape websites at scale, but do so in a manner that violates applicable law 
(
Kolt, 2026
; 
Nannini et al., 2026
; 
Grossman et al., 2026
; 
Lichkovski et al., 2025
)
. The problem is compounded by users who may deliberately instruct agents to dismiss legal rules (e.g., 
“Please scrape this website and ignore the terms.”
).

To fill this gap, we introduce 
ScrapeBench
, the first agentic benchmark to empirically evaluate the compliance of AI agents with website scraping policies. Unlike performance-oriented web agent benchmarks that score AI agents based on task completion, 
ScrapeBench
 measures whether agents comply with legal constraints, focusing specifically on prohibitions on scraping specified in websites’ ToS and 
robots.txt
. Our main contributions are as follows:

1.

Benchmark Dataset
: We curate a novel real-world dataset of the ToS and 
robots.txt
 directives for the 1,895 most-visited websites, analyzing and annotating which websites prohibit scraping (
 = 1,626 websites) and which do not prohibit scraping (
 = 269 websites).

2.

Agentic Evaluation Framework
: We develop an evaluation framework to measure the legal compliance and over-refusal of AI agents under different conditions, including a variety of user instructions and varying levels of access to relevant legal resources. We evaluate and report the results for six open- and closed-weight AI agents: GPT-5.2, Claude Opus 4.6, Gemini 3.1 Pro, Kimi K2.5, Qwen3 Max, and Grok 4.2.

3.

Empirical Findings
: We find that under neutral prompts, AI agents frequently violate scraping prohibitions, exhibiting a compliance rate of < 5%. With a few notable exceptions, we generally find relatively low rates of over-refusal in the case of websites that do not prohibit scraping.

4.

Human Baseline
: To contextualize our findings regarding AI agents, we conduct a human baseline for legal compliance in website scraping (
 = 180 participants) across three different legal contexts.

Tool Behavior Extraction

We extract two additional tool behavioral signals from the tool call trace 
 to analyze how the AI agent engages with a website’s legal resources during its navigation. These behavioral signals provide a richer analysis of the AI agent’s actions with respect to compliance. For example, an AI agent that fetches an explicitly prohibited website’s content but first consulted 
robots.txt
 is different to an AI agent that scraped without checking 
robots.txt
.

1.

Checked robots.txt.
 We set this value to 1 if any 
 in 
 is classified as a 
robots.txt
 page, and 0 otherwise. This signal indicates whether the AI agent attempted to find and consult a website’s designated 
robots.txt
 directives.

2.

Checked ToS.
 We set this value equal to 1 if any 
 in 
 is classified as a ToS or legal page, and 0 otherwise. A URL is classified as a legal page if its path matches any of the related patterns (e.g.,

4 
Overall Agentic Compliance and Over-Refusal in Website Scraping

4.1 
Measuring Compliance with 
Explicitly Prohibited (EP)
 Websites

We evaluate AI agents’ rate of compliance with websites’ scraping policies on the 
Explicitly Prohibited
 websites (
 = 1,626). We report compliance rates across all eight prompt conditions and AI agents in Figure 
3
.

Result 1: Using the simplest prompt, AI agents often violate website scraping policies.

Under a neutral baseline prompt (P1), compliance is near zero for five of six AI agents, including GPT, Claude, Gemini, Kimi, and Qwen, except for Grok, which achieves 11% compliance. Implicitly mentioning that some websites have scraping policies (P2) does not improve compliance, with the highest being Grok and Kimi, achieving 5.5%-5.7% compliance. Using explicit instructions (P3) and linking the ToS and 
robots.txt
 (P4) increase Claude’s compliance to 40.1% and 41.5%. Substantially higher compliance is exhibited only when the ToS and 
robots.txt
 are pasted as in-context information in the prompt (P5), where GPT, Claude, and Gemini all surpass 70% compliance, while Qwen remains consistently unresponsive with compliance remaining at 2.9%.

Result 2: AI agents require strong legal warnings to be compliant.

To induce the strongest pressure for compliance, the legal warning prompt (P7) combines the ToS and 
robots.txt
 texts, strict instructions to comply, and reference to the applicable law that may be violated. This prompt yields the highest observed compliance rates in five out of six agents, specifically Claude (85.9%), Gemini (85.7%), Kimi (62.7%), Grok (59.7%) and Qwen (13.8%). GPT peaks at P5 (73.9%) while dropping at P7 (60.6%). While these results may suggest that compliance can be partially addressed through prompt engineering, doing so is labor-intensive, as it may require an end-user to paste the full legal texts from the ToS and 
robots.txt
 and provide additional instructions.

Result 3: Adversarial prompting reduces compliance for most AI agents, with one exception.

Under the adversarial prompt (P8) that instructs agents to ignore the ToS and 
robots.txt
, most AI agents exhibit very low compliance, with Gemini down to 41.6%, GPT to 11.7%, Kimi to 9.5%, Qwen to 1.7%, and Grok to 0.6%. Only Claude resists the pressure, achieving a compliance rate of 99.7%. However, upon manual inspection of Claude’s responses under P8, we find that its high resistance to the adversarial prompt is arguably indiscriminate. That is, the model responds with a blanket refusal rather than actually reasoning about whether or not the website in fact prohibits scraping, which could possibly be explained by Claude’s safety mechanisms being triggered by the prompt 
“Ignore the terms of service”
.

Measuring Over-refusal with 
Not Explicitly Prohibited (NEP)

We conduct the same experimental setup as above on the 
Not Explicitly Prohibited

 = 269) websites whose ToS do not contain any restrictions on scraping. We report over-refusal rates across all prompt conditions in Figure 
4

Result 4: In-context legal resources reduce non-compliance and over-refusal.

Relative to P3, the provision of in-context legal resources (P5 and P6) both increases the compliance rates (above) and decreases the over-refusal rates (here) for some agents. Claude’s compliance increases from 40.1% to 83.6% and 82.4%, while its over-refusal rates decrease from 16.4% to 8.2% and 6.7%, across conditions P3, P5, and P6, respectively. Gemini follows a similar pattern, with compliance increasing from 26.8% to 75.2% and over-refusal decreasing from 12.6% to 1.5%, for conditions P3 and P5. By contrast, over-refusal rates remain below 6% for GPT, Qwen, and Grok.

Result 5: Under adversarial prompts, AI agents either refuse broadly or scrape broadly.

Agents evaluated under the adversarial prompt (P8) appear to fall into one of two categories. In the first category, agents broadly refuse to scrape. In the second category, agents broadly proceed to scrape. The former includes Claude, which refused 99.7%-98.5% of scraping requests 
regardless of scraping permissibility
, followed by Gemini at a smaller magnitude of 41% and 27.7%. The latter group includes GPT, Kimi, Qwen, and Grok, which exhibited compliance below 12% and over-refusal of 5%.

Result 8: AI agents violate scraping primarily due to misinterpreting the ToS.

To better understand the potential causes of noncompliance, we categorize the stated rationales provided by each agent in its outputs in the 
Violation
 trials (30 per agent across P1-P7 prompts, 
) using GPT-5.4-Mini as a judge model, as reported in Table

6 
Human Baseline Results

To contextualize the rates of legal compliance that we observe for AI agents, we conduct a human baseline in which human participants perform tasks comparable to those assigned to AI agents. Concretely, we recruit 30 participants via Prolific in a between-participant setup, totaling 180 participants across both 
EP
 and 
NEP
 websites, with 30 participants in each of the P1, P4, and P7 prompt conditions. Each participant is given a link to an external web app that asks them to decide whether to press a button to scrape 15 randomly sampled websites one at a time. We provide full details, statistical analyses, and screenshots of the study’s web UI in Appendix 
E
.

Result 9: Humans are cautious and less responsive to legal priming than AI agents.

Under the neutral baseline (P1), human participants exhibit high compliance of 48.9% on EP websites and high over-refusal of 38.9% on NEP websites, as shown in Figure 
3
 and Figure 
4
, respectively. Legal priming under conditions P4 and P7 substantially increases compliance on EP websites to 57.3% (
 < 0.04) and 65.1% (
 < 0.001), respectively. This change, however, is modest relative to that of some AI agents; for example, Claude’s compliance rate increased by 85% from P1 to P7. Meanwhile, for over-refusal rates among human participants, the increase from 38.9% under P1 to 42.8% under P4 and 45.8% under P7, as shown in Figure 
4
, was not statistically significant.

Result 10: Humans check legal resources less often than AI agents, but act on what they read.

We observe sharp differences in the rates of checking legal resources between human participants and AI agents. When resources are linked (P4) as seen in Figure 
5
, humans check the ToS 38.9% and 35.3% of the time for EP and NEP websites, respectively. These rates are substantially lower when compared to those of AI agents, which are between 52% and 83% and 27% and 61% for EP and NEP websites, respectively. When prompted with a strong legal warning (P7), humans participants’ rate of checking ToS increases to 57.8% on EP websites (
 < 0.001) and 65.8% on NEP websites (
 < 0.001) and, for 
robots.txt
, to 38.9% and 58.7%, respectively. When human participants do read the ToS, there is a corresponding increase in compliance for EP websites to 67.1%, compared with 55.7% where human participants did not read the ToS (
 < 0.001).

7 
Discussion

Failure modes in the legal compliance of AI agents.

Our findings with respect to compliance, over-refusal, and tool usage in the context of website scraping shed light on several failure modes of AI agents with respect to legal alignment. First, AI agents accessing legal resources such as ToS when available does not necessarily translate into higher levels of compliance. Second, the heavy reliance of AI agents on the ToS being pasted into the prompt while failing to verify it from the live websites could enable prompt injection attacks (e.g., pasting in a false or inaccurate ToS) that undermine legal compliance. Third, AI agents remain highly vulnerable to adversarial prompts, as demonstrated in condition P8. Taken together, these findings highlight the need for more concerted efforts and more robust methods of training AI agents to comply with legal rules 
Kolt et al. (2026)
; 
Kolt (2026)
.

The burden of compliance between end-users and model providers.

Given that the compliance of AI agents with website scraping policies is substantially shaped by the inclusion or exclusion of the ToS and 
robots.txt
 in the prompt, end-users of agents can significantly affect the level of compliance in practice. As end-users often interact with AI agents using simpler instructions closer to P1 and might lack the legal or technical knowledge to construct prompts like those in P5–P7, the rate of legal compliance of agents in practice may be far lower than under more careful prompting conditions. Accordingly, it might be appropriate for model providers to be required to themselves take measures to improve legal compliance, such as using system prompts comparable to the prompts in conditions P5–P7.

8 
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced 
ScrapeBench
, the first agentic benchmark for empirically evaluating the compliance of AI agents with website scraping policies. Our findings expose three failure modes: (1) under neutral prompts, most AI agents in our experiment scrape prohibited websites and only refuse to do so once the ToS and 
robots.txt
 are explicitly inserted into prompts; (2) despite frequently checking legal rules, this does not consistently result in AI agents exhibiting higher levels of compliance with those rules; and (3) some agents exhibit high levels of over-refusal, refusing to complete the scraping task despite it being lawful to do so.

A.1 
Ethics Statement

Human Baseline Study.

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Hebrew University. For our human baseline experiments, all participants provided their informed consent (see Figure 
7
) and received a debriefing form upon completion of the task to fully disclose the study’s intent with collecting their scraping decisions (see Figure 
18
). We ensured that our US-based human participants were paid fairly for their work via Prolific. Participants were compensated at a flat rate of 9.00 USD and completed the task in 20-40 minutes. Pro-rated, this is equivalent to 12.00 USD per hour and above the US federal minimum wage of 7.25 USD per hour. We do not collect any personally identifiable information (PII) from participants; by default, we record their Prolific IDs upon completion of the assigned tasks for tracking purposes. We provide full details of the human study, including recruitment, statistical analyses, and screenshots of the study’s web UI in Appendix 
E
.

Web Scraping Task Setup.

The human participants recruited from Prolific never performed actual website scraping. The “scrape” action for humans is performed by clicking a “scrape” button, which displays the HTML text of the website that was manually pre-saved in a database rather than triggering an actual script that performs a scrape. Thus, 
no website received automated traffic as a consequence of the human study
. For the agentic experiments where real HTTP requests are sent to the 1,895 websites we evaluated as part of 
ScrapeBench
, we took the following precautionary measures to ensure the volume of traffic is well within the bounds of legitimate research on web scraping:

1.

We set an identifiable User-Agent (
AcademicResearchBot/1.0
) for all of our agent setup so that any website operators can attribute and block the traffic if desired.

2.

We ensured that each consecutive trial with AI agents are sufficiently separated with an inter-request delay of 2.0 seconds to protect website domains from bursty request traffic.

3.

We ensured each trial only retrieves a minimal number of URLs from a domain mostly comprised of the landing page, 
robots.txt
 and a ToS using a cap of 
 tool invocations which results in a small per-domain footprint.

4.

We retained only the public-facing legal sources of 
robots.txt
 and ToS for distribution and will not publish scraped landing page content of the websites.

Indirect Third-Party Access.

We note that some models have indirectly accessed websites through a third-party reader and proxy services (e.g., 
r.jina.ai
). Specifically, GPT-5.2 retrieved website content in this manner for 21.6% (409) of the website data, where 2.6% of the websites initially blocked the agent. Accessing third-party readers does not change any compliance or over-refusal judgment and is treated the same as direct access.

A.2 
Limitations

Legal Framing.

ScrapeBench
 evaluates compliance of AI agents with website scraping policies. We note that the enforceability of ToS and 
robots.txt
 varies across jurisdictions and may depend on factors that we do not evaluate with 
ScrapeBench
. This includes the purpose of a scrape; doctrinal concepts in contract, tort, and property law; copyright and preemption; privacy and data protection law; and determining the relevance and applicability of diverse directives, regulations, and statutes (e.g., the U.S. Computer Fraud and Abuse Act and analogous state laws, the EU Digital Services Act, and the EU General Data Protection Regulation) 
[
36
]
. Accordingly, we do not claim that any specific AI agent model’s action, prompt, or outcome from this study necessarily constitutes, or does not constitute, a legal violation.

Single-Agent, Single-Trial, and Single-Turn Setup.

ScrapeBench
 evaluates each trial in isolation, where a single AI agent attempts to scrape the landing page of a website. We currently do not cover multi-turn, multi-trial, or multi-agent task decomposition due to time and compute costs (see the overall cost of a single-trial setup in Appendix 
C
). However, this direct scoping using a single-agent, single-turn setup is 
crucial
 for the task and benchmark, as it isolates the compliance signal from potential confounds that arise in long-horizon tasks. We do not claim that our findings generalize to open-ended, multi-agent, multi-turn workflows involving AI agents. We leave explorations in this area for future work.

Website Data Snapshot.

ScrapeBench
 is composed of 1,895 websites collected from a snapshot of the February 2026 top website list from the Chrome Top Million Websites (CrUX) repository. As with any legal contract, we acknowledge that the content of any website’s ToS and associated 
robots.txt
 may evolve over time through periodic updates. Thus, we encourage users who will be benchmarking future AI agents with 
ScrapeBench
 data to re-validate the contents of the ToS and 
robots.txt
 by performing another retrieval collection with the code that contains the end-to-end extraction of ToS and 
robots.txt
. Moreover, the current website list finalized as part of 
ScrapeBench
 is filtered to only represent high-traffic, English, US-accessible websites to ensure that they can be reached by most commercial AI agents. We leave the agentic compliance evaluation of less-recognized, non-English websites for future work.

A.3 
Impact Statement

ScrapeBench
 provides a reproducible and diagnostic benchmark which can be used by commercial model providers, academic groups, open-source communities, and legal and governance researchers to evaluate how AI agents comply with website scraping policies. By open-sourcing the benchmark data, code, prompts, and AI agent outputs under an appropriate data license of CC BY-SA 4.0, 
ScrapeBench
 lowers the barrier for compliance evaluation of open and commercial AI agents. We note the possibility of a misuse risk where the per-domain scraping permissibility labels from the ToS and 
robots.txt
 from websites that prohibit scraping may be used to train against compliance (e.g., learning which websites explicitly prohibit scraping to evade detection). We explicitly discourage such use of the 
ScrapeBench
 dataset.

A.4 
Declaration of LLM Use

In producing this work, we used Grammarly for minor grammar and spelling corrections, Claude Code (Claude 4.6 and 4.7) and ChatGPT (GPT-5.2) for prototyping scripts to run experiments, assistance with formatting LaTeX tables and figures, troubleshooting code, and problems in Matplotlib visualizations. All recommendations and outputs from these LLM-based tools were scrutinized and verified by the authors before integrating into the paper or the study’s codebase.

B.2 
Additional Tool Design Details

The agent’s tool 
 is implemented as a live HTTP GET endpoint. Given a URL, 
 returns a JSON object containing the HTTP status code, content type, page content, final URL after redirects, and any error message. Text responses are truncated to 8K characters (or approx. 2K tokens) with a marker indicating the original length; binary content is replaced with a size descriptor. Redirects are followed automatically, and errors (e.g., timeouts or network connection failures) return structured messages in the JSON response rather than raising exceptions, allowing the agent to observe and respond to failures. The request timeout is 15 seconds and the User-Agent is set to 
AcademicResearchBot/1.0
.

The tool description provided to the model is deliberately neutral: 
“Fetch the contents of a URL via HTTP GET. Returns the HTTP status code, content type, and page content. This tool can be used to fetch any URL or website content pages.”
 This tool description avoids biasing the agent toward or against checking legal resource documents while making the option available.

Classifying permissibility of terms of service (ToS).

We use a human-verified LLM-based extraction approach with GPT-5.2 (
openai/gpt-5.2
, accessed via OpenRouter) by processing each ToS file from each website. We prompted GPT-5.2 to scan for scraping-relevant keywords, classify the ToS text into one of three labels (
Prohibited
, 
Partial
, or 
Permissible
), and return the extracted verbatim text excerpt that supports the label. The exact system prompt and user prompt used in querying GPT-5.2 are presented in the two colored boxes below. Additionally, we set 
temperature
 
 for GPT-5.2 with the response constrained to JSON only. We manually verified the correctness of the extracted text excerpts from a random 15% sample and found no issues with GPT-5.2’s extraction performance. In accordance with our scraping permissibility decision scheme as reported in Table 
2
 in Section 
2.2
, we removed websites classified as

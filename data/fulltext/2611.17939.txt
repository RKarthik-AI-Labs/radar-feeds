Abstract.

Can you trust a finance agent you did not build? We take the reference agent shipped with the Vals Finance Agent Benchmark v2, leave it untuned, and ask four questions a leaderboard score does not answer. Is the score stable? Seven identical runs on 18 questions pass between 105 and 123 of 149 rubric criteria, and the bill varies by up to 17%. When the agent fails, who owns the fix? We classify 20 failed trajectories, from three runs of nine held-out questions, by the layer whose owner would remedy them, under a taxonomy frozen in advance: 13 belong to the agent’s instructions, 3 to its tools, 4 to the model, none to its data. Can that diagnosis be trusted? Four blind readers from three model families, given only the traces, name the same fault as our LLM analyzer in 94% of cases, the rate at which they agree with one another (93%). Can a change be trusted? An optimization loop reads the traces, makes one targeted change per round and keeps it only if it clears a fixed threshold. Under a cost objective its final configuration costs 0.34
 the baseline on the held-out questions (95% CI [0.22, 0.58]) at accuracy within noise. Under an accuracy objective it gains 14 points on the development questions and 1.5 on the held-out ones (CI [
5.3, +7.2]). Judged against the measured run-to-run noise, under a rule written before the test was opened, the first is a real saving and the second is not. This is a case study of one agent and nine test questions, not a general result.

Keywords: 
financial research agents, agent evaluation, failure taxonomy, harness engineering, auditability, output drift

1. 
Introduction

A financial research agent is in production. It has a leaderboard score. A compliance officer asks what it does when it is wrong. The score does not answer: it was computed once, on questions the officer has never seen, by a grader the institution does not control, and it says nothing about where the wrong answers came from or who in the building should fix them.

Benchmarks of financial agents have multiplied since 2025. The Vals Finance Agent Benchmark 
(
Bigeard et al., 2025
)
 scores answers to expert-written research questions against rubric criteria; BigFinanceBench 
(
Wang et al., 2026
)
 grades the visible derivation step by step. Both measure how much of the answer, or of its derivation, is right. Neither says why a wrong answer was wrong, and neither names the layer of the system that owns the remedy. DFAH-Bench 
(
Khatchadourian, 2026
)
 comes closest to the question we ask: replaying the same financial decision task, it finds that decisions agree 94–95% of the time while exact tool paths agree only 67–69%. The processes diverge; the benchmark establishes it and stops there.

We stop later. This paper is a controlled case study on one shipped agent that we did not write and did not tune, organised as five questions, each answering what the previous one leaves open.

(1)

Is the score a property of the agent?
 No. Seven identical runs of the shipped configuration span 105–123 of 149 criteria (§
3
).

(2)

When it fails, who owns the fix?
 On a pre-registered set of 20 failed trajectories, under our owner-of-the-remedy taxonomy: policy 13, tool 3, data 0, model 4 (§
4
).

(3)

Can you trust that diagnosis?
 Blind readers of three model families name the same fault in 75 of 80 cases; the layer is a definition we supply; localisation is looser (§
5
).

(4)

Can you trust a change?
 Same loop, two objectives: cost falls to 0.34
 and holds on the frozen test; accuracy rises 14 points on dev and is indistinguishable from zero on the test (§
6
).

(5)

What is trust, then?
 A protocol whose every step can be audited (§
7
).

The finance cut on the second question is the owner of the remedy. In an institution, compliance rewrites the agent’s policy, the platform engineer owns the tool, the data owner the retrieval pipeline, and model risk the model. We classify failures by that owner, not by symptom, and we measure rather than assert what the classification means (§
5
).

Scope.

This is a case study of one agent: nine test questions and twenty failed trajectories give an anatomy rather than population shares, and every test-split interval is a paired bootstrap over the nine questions. The fourth question is where the first three are put to work. Cost reduction is not the contribution of this paper; it is the intervention the diagnosis pointed at, on the axis where our instruments can resolve an effect (0.34 against a run-to-run noise band of 0.83). On accuracy the same instruments return noise, and that is a result too.

We contribute (i) a drift measurement of a shipped agent across seven identical baselines; (ii) an anatomy of its failures on a pre-registered set, under a taxonomy frozen before the test split was analysed; (iii) a blind, cross-family check of the diagnostic that separates fault identification (corroborated), remedy ownership (a definition, reproducible from the rule) and localisation (looser), with the diagnostic’s own errors declared; (iv) the optimization loop as shipped, under two objectives, each scored once on a frozen split under a written rule; (v) the protocol itself. We do not claim a better agent, a general method, or comparability with any leaderboard (§
7.1
).

The agent.

The Vals Finance Agent Benchmark v2 ships a runnable agent: a tool-calling loop over Claude Sonnet 4.5 with seven tools (web search, EDGAR full-text search, HTML page parsing, a retrieval tool that reads stored filings through a nested LLM call, a calculator, price history, and final-answer submission) and a 2,767-character system prompt 
(
Bigeard et al., 2025
)
. Its public split has 27 questions across nine categories, each with rubric criteria carrying a severity and a must-pass flag. We reproduce the shipped configuration with one documented backend substitution, required for reproducibility: the agent’s EDGAR search depends on a paid third-party index, which we replaced with the SEC’s own full-text search endpoint behind the same tool name, description and argument schema. On the 53 EDGAR queries of one baseline run replayed on both backends, the target company’s main filings were returned by both in 81 of 81 cases; the comparison is in Appendix 
E
. Every run exports its trajectory in the Agent Trajectory Interchange Format 
(
Harbor Framework, 2026
)
, which is what the analyzer reads.

The judge.

We score against the benchmark’s verbatim criteria with our own judge: Gemini 3.8 Flash at temperature 0, three votes per criterion, a 0.5% tolerance on numeric matches, reading the final answer only. Its configuration fingerprint is identical across every report in this paper. Two facts bound the judge’s role. The cost results of §
6
 are sums of provider prices per call: they do not depend on the judge’s verdicts, so no judge error can move them. The pass-rate results do, and the judge is internally consistent under our fixed configuration: of the three votes cast per criterion, the vote split in 4 of 1,080 test cells and 12 of 1,043 dev baseline cells. This establishes within-configuration stability, not agreement with an external or human judge. Our numbers are internally comparable and not comparable with the Vals leaderboard, whose grader we have not used. We have not re-scored the test reports with a second judge model; the stored final answers allow it without re-running the agent, and §
7.1
 says so.

The analyzer and the loop.

We use AloLab 
(
Galeone et al., 2026
)
, an evaluation and optimization engine, as a black box: we configure it and read its outputs, and we did not modify it. The analyzer (Claude Sonnet 5) reads a scored run and emits failure categories, each with a location in the trace (a decisive step), a 
fix_level
 (prompt, skill, tool description, orchestration, architecture, or data issue) and a 
harness_component
 (observation, context, control, action, state, verification, or none). The loop proposes one change per round on one of six declared levers (the system prompt, tool descriptions, which tools are enabled, the agent’s model, the nested retrieval model, and that model’s instructions), applies it, re-runs the dev split, and promotes or rejects the change against the round’s parent configuration under a fixed gate. We wrote no agent and tuned nothing by hand.

4. 
Who owns the fix

4.1. 
Taxonomy

We classify each failed trajectory by the layer that owns its remedy: 
policy
 (the instructions the agent is given; owner, whoever writes its policy), 
tool
 (interfaces, descriptions, output parsing, state between steps; owner, the platform engineer), 
data
 (what reaches the model’s context: sources, retrieval, truncation, staleness; owner, the data owner), and 
model
 (the model itself, given the right instructions, tools and facts; owner, model risk). The analyzer does not speak this vocabulary. It emits 
fix_level
 and 
harness_component

One cell needs a rule. A 
prompt

Rule M
: the category maps to model when both hold, read from the trace and not from the analyzer’s prose: (a) the analyzer names no missing or wrong instruction, and (b) the decisive step is a reasoning, assistant or output event, and the facts it needed were already in the context at that step. Otherwise policy. Model is the residual: the case a model swap, and no harness change, would remedy.

The unit of count is the failed trajectory. One trajectory may carry several categories; its primary layer is the layer of the category containing its decisive step. A trajectory whose only category is a data issue is a benchmark artefact and leaves the denominator. Four choices in the table have defensible alternatives; we record them in Appendix

4.3. 
What the count does not mean

Data is never primary, by construction.

The plain-language “stale filing” failure exists in these traces: in one replica the agent parsed the FY2024 10-K while the FY2025 10-K sat in the same search result. It lands in policy, because the remedy the analyzer names is an instruction to check the period. The projection sends retrieval failures to data only when the analyzer says the material could not reach the window; when it says the agent did not look, the owner is the policy. Zero in the data column is a property of the projection and of this agent, not evidence that retrieval never failed.

5. 
Can you trust the diagnosis?

The count in §
4.2
 is only as good as the analyzer that produced it. We checked it two ways: blind, against readers that never saw its output, and against itself. Three things are being checked and they come out differently: what failed, where in the trace, and which layer owns the remedy.

5.1. 
Blind panel

Four blind readers from three model families (Claude Opus 5, Gemini 3.1 Pro, Gemini 3.8 Flash, GPT-5.5) each received the same 20 failed trajectories with the question, the failed criteria and the numbered trace (for the three API readers, a length-capped rendering of it), and nothing from the analyzer. Each named the decisive event and the fault. A separate judge (Claude Opus 5), shown only pairs of justifications, unlabelled and in random order, judged whether the two described the same wrong quantity or the same wrong choice. The protocol, including how “same fault” is decided, was written before the panel ran (Appendix 
D
).

What failed: corroborated.

The analyzer names the same fault as a blind reader in 75 of 80 cases; the readers agree among themselves in 112 of 120 (Table 
6
). Every one of the 20 trajectories has its fault confirmed by at least three of the four readers.

Where: looser on both sides.

The analyzer’s decisive event is within five trace events of a reader’s in 45 of 80 pairs; the readers’ are within five of each other’s in 84 of 120. The analyzer is as good as an independent reader at saying what went wrong and about as imprecise at saying where.

Which layer: a definition, not a perception.

Asked to place the fault in our four layers, the blind readers said model in 66 of 80 labels and policy once. In 12 of the 20 Opus labels the reader quotes a violated instruction and still writes model. The readers answered 
who erred
; the table answers 
who owns the remedy
. We report this as a definition, not as a disagreement: the owner-of-the-remedy reading is ours, and it is not the reading a model gives unprompted. Given the rule, it is reproducible: a second reader with Rule M, the trace and the decisive index, and none of the analyzer’s prose, returned the same verdict on all eight cells where the rule applied.

The five misses are informative. Two are Gemini Pro calling ASPI and CZR benchmark artefacts rather than agent failures; on ASPI the reader is wrong (§
5.2
). Two are CRM, where the reader names a different arithmetic error that the analyzer did not report. One is CZR, where the reader blames a wrong-basis choice and the analyzer a corrupted extraction.

5.2. 
The analyzer’s own errors

An over-claim, caught by a sister replica.

On CZR the analyzer wrote that “correcting this single decision point […] would flip every downstream EBITDAR/growth/CAGR check”. The sister replica had made that decision and still failed all eight checks. Three replicas took three different bases for the same figure and each was 0.6–2.1% off, against a 0.5% tolerance. No figure any replica retrieved reproduces the rubric. A reviewer reading one trajectory would have believed the analyzer; the replica did not.

Same input, two taxonomies.

Given a byte-identical payload twice, the analyzer returned nine categories once and eight the next time, and the two categories outside the prompt layer became one, of a different kind. The layer distribution barely moved, which favours classifying by owner of the remedy over classifying by the analyzer’s category. But the instability reaches the count through one field: in one replica the analyzer attached 
verification
 to three of its four prompt categories, Rule M applied only to the fourth and returned policy, and the model count was zero; in another it attached none to six of eight, Rule M fired, and the model count was three. The per-replica spread in Table 
4
 is that.

Same fault, four labels.

In four of the five analyses of the accuracy arm (§
6.2
) the analyzer named the stale-period failure, each time under a different category label.

A benchmark artefact that was not.

ASPI never passes its must-pass criteria under any configuration. The analyzer emits a data-issue category on it and one blind reader calls it a benchmark artefact, for a visible reason: the question asks for the gross margin between 2/28/2024 and 2/28/2025, and ASP Isotopes reports on calendar years. The periods are those of Renergen, a South African producer that ASP Isotopes agreed in May 2025 to acquire, and whose audited statements for the years ended 28 February 2025 and 29 February 2024 ASP Isotopes filed as an exhibit to an 8-K. Those statements reproduce the rubric: plant depreciation in cost of sales rises from R1.5 million to R34.2 million, +2,149.47%, and the gross margin falls 88.62 points; one normalised figure in the rubric we could not reproduce. In one baseline replica the agent’s own EDGAR search returned that exhibit (event 39), and the agent chose ASP Isotopes’ quarterly figures instead (event 40). The failure is the agent’s, and the trajectory stays in the count, where the frozen rule had already kept it. An analyzer, a blind reader and, for a while, the authors took a hard question for a broken one; the trace said otherwise.

Replay.

We replayed the analyzer twelve times over one scored dev run: two models, with the full run and with one failed question alone, three repetitions each (Appendix 
C
). The stronger model returned six to seven categories where the production model returned three to five; given one question in isolation, both placed the AMZN stale-filing failure in a different 
fix_level
 in different repetitions (prompt, orchestration, architecture, tool description), and one repetition stated as trace evidence an inference the trace does not contain.

What this section supports is bounded. The analyzer is corroborated on what failed. The layer is our definition and is reproducible across model families when the rule is given. The instrument’s failure modes are known: it over-claims counterfactuals, it is unstable on the harness-component axis, and it names the same fault inconsistently.

6. 
Can you trust a change?

We report two runs of the loop as shipped on the 18 dev questions, one under a cost objective and one under an accuracy objective (Fig. 
1
). Each round, the analyzer reads the traces of the current dev run; the loop picks one of its findings, proposes one change on one lever and states the trace evidence the change answers; the change is applied, the dev split re-run once, and the gate compares the new run with its parent. Under the cost objective a change is promoted if the bill falls by at least 5% and the pass rate stays within 10 points of the session’s first run; under the accuracy objective, if the pass rate rises by at least one point and the bill rises by no more than 50%. A session stops after five rounds or two consecutive rejections. The gate reads one run, and §
3
 shows how far one run moves: it filters changes, it does not prove them. What the loop guarantees is that every change answers something observed in a trace; whether it works is left to the test. The loop’s own final configuration, one configuration and no choice at numbers seen, then goes to the frozen test split, three replicas, against three baseline replicas, under a decision rule written before the test was opened (Appendix 
D
). We read every promoted prompt change before the test for leakage: no company, ticker, figure, date or specific filing may appear. Both winners passed, with one declared exception noted below. The two arms are read together: the same rule, written in advance, and the same measured noise band are applied to both; the cost change clears the band and the accuracy change does not.

6.1. 
Cost arm

Five rounds (Table 
7
). The analyzer’s spend reading found the nested retrieval call at 74–96% of the cost of every question it sampled, and the agent’s own reasoning a minor share; the loop moved that call, a document extraction, from Sonnet 4.5 to Haiku 4.5 (round 1, cost 0.437
 its parent, promoted). In traces where the agent queried the same stored document three to eight times, it then tried to teach the agent to batch its sub-questions into one retrieval call per document, first as a tool description (round 2, 0.996
, rejected) and then as a system-prompt paragraph (round 3, 0.844
, promoted). Round 4 tried to chain arithmetic into a single calculator call and saved four percent, rejected. Round 5 answered a trace with ten EDGAR searches, five or six of them near-repeats, for a 10-K that was never filed, before the agent found the answer in an 8-K; it added one sentence, to pivot to an 8-K exhibit or web search after two or three empty EDGAR queries, promoted.

The same instruction saved nothing as a tool description and 16% of the bill as a system-prompt line. In this agent, the layer, not the wording, owned the remedy.

6.4. 
Read together

Same loop, same judge, same split, same rule, one pass and one fail. The structural remedy, a cheaper nested model and an instruction about how to use a tool, survived questions the loop had never seen. The case-specific remedy, two paragraphs about ambiguities in particular dev questions, did not. Without the drift measurement and the written rule we would have reported “+14 points on dev”, and it would have been true and worthless. The same instrument that passes one change fails the other, and that is what makes the 0.34 worth reporting.

Two boundaries on this reading. The cost result is consistent with the diagnosis having pointed at a real remedy; it does not isolate the diagnosis, because the loop has no control arm (§
7.1
), and the test split is now closed, so none can be added to this study. And cost is the axis on which the instrument has resolution, 0.34 against a band of 0.83; on accuracy the band is the result.

7. 
Trust is an audit trail

7.1. 
Limits, and what we do not claim

Our judge scores the benchmark’s criteria; the benchmark’s own grader would score differently, and no number here is comparable with the Vals leaderboard. The judge is a single model family, and we have not measured how the pass-rate verdicts move under a second judge. We have not run that check; the stored final answers allow the six test reports to be re-scored by another model without re-running the agent or reopening the split. What we can say now is that the cost result does not depend on the judge’s verdicts, and that the judge’s three votes were unanimous on all but 4 of 1,080 test cells, a statement about within-configuration stability and nothing more. Nine test questions and twenty failed trajectories make an anatomy, not an estimate of a population share; the inferential unit is the question, 
. There is no control arm: the loop is the system as shipped, and the gain belongs to it end to end, not to the diagnosis in isolation; we validate the loop, not the causal efficacy of its diagnosis. One agent shows, and does not prove, that the approach transfers. The blind readers are language models of three families, not financial analysts; the owner-of-the-remedy question for a human analyst is open. The accuracy winner was compared against the baseline replicas of the first opening, reused, so the pair does not share a time window. One example figure survived the leakage read of the accuracy winner’s prompt. The dev split’s analyses were produced under the cost objective and Rule M was not applied to them. Two substrate failures live in the traces and not in any score: the nested retrieval call fails with a prompt-too-long error when one call spans several large filings, and the agent’s provider client waits 57 minutes before noticing a dead connection.

The Vals Finance Agent Benchmark 
(

(

8. 
Conclusion

We asked whether a shipped finance agent can be trusted and answered with measurements rather than a cure. The score is a draw from a distribution 18 criteria wide. When this agent fails, under our taxonomy, the model owns one fix in five and the harness owns the rest. The diagnosis that says so is corroborated blind by readers of three model families on what failed, and its own failure modes are listed. A change to the agent can be trusted only against the measured drift and under a rule written in advance, and when we did that, the same instrument passed a structural change and failed a case-specific one. Trust, on this evidence, is the trail that lets a reader check each of those sentences against a file. A practitioner can build that trail with nothing from us: freeze a split, run the baseline more than once, write the promotion rule against the measured drift, and read the trace before believing the diagnosis.

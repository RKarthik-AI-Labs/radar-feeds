Abstract

Random Network Distillation (RND) is a scalable novelty signal for RL based on a fixed random target network and a trained predictor. However, it can overgeneralize: the predictor extrapolates the random target off the data manifold, causing novelty scores to collapse on unfamiliar inputs. This paper analyzes this failure through a kernel-theoretic lens. Under a GP target model and an NTK-regime predictor, we show that the RND novelty score is a 
cross-kernel residual variance
: it is governed jointly by the 
target covariance kernel
 and the 
predictor NTK
, rather than by either one alone. This identifies overgeneralization as spectral under-excitation. Motivated by this view, we formulate spectral target design as a principle for shaping RND novelty geometry. As a concrete instance, we replace the implicit random-network target with a bandwidth-controlled Random Fourier Feature (RFF) target, redistributing residual signal toward modes less attenuated by the predictor. Experiments on offline D4RL, online Atari, and Fetch manipulation benchmarks show improved novelty discrimination and downstream RL performance. The code for the RL implementation is available at 
https://github.com/tuuktuc86/RFF-RND
.

1 
Introduction

Random Network Distillation (RND) 
(
Burda et al., 2019b
)
 is a simple yet effective novelty signal for reinforcement learning (RL), using the prediction error of a trained network against a fixed random target to measure familiarity. Its scalability and ease of integration have made it a standard component in modern RL pipelines, driving exploration in online settings 
(
Burda et al., 2019a
)
 and enforcing conservatism in offline ones 
(
Rezaeifar et al., 2022
; 
Nikulin et al., 2023
)
.

Despite its widespread adoption, RND suffers from a fundamental failure mode known as 
overgeneralization
: the predictor can extrapolate beyond the data manifold, assigning low novelty scores to inputs beyond the data coverage radius that should be flagged as novel 
(
Belkin et al., 2019
)
. This undermines the intrinsic reward in both settings — misallocating exploration bonuses toward already-familiar regions and failing to penalize out-of-support actions sufficiently. Prior work has addressed this through
distributional extensions 
(
Yang et al., 2024
)
 or architectural modifications 
(
Nikulin et al., 2023
)
, but these approaches do not explicitly characterize 
why
 overgeneralization occurs or provide principled control over the novelty signal’s geometric structure. This raises a natural question:

What governs the discriminative power of RND, and how can it be reliably controlled?

In this work, we answer this question through a kernel-theoretic analysis 
(
Rasmussen and Williams, 2006
; 
Schölkopf and Smola, 2002
)
. We show that overgeneralization arises not from the predictor or target network in isolation, but from their interaction: when the predictor extrapolates the target accurately beyond the data manifold, the novelty score collapses off-manifold. Motivated by this analysis, we propose a spectral target-design principle for RND.

Specifically, directly aligning the target with hard-to-predict modes is impractical, since the predictor spectrum is implicit, architecture-dependent, and generally unknown before training.
We therefore seek predictor-agnostic target families whose spectra can explicitly control the kernel geometry, and are lightweight to sample.
Random Fourier Features (RFF) 
(
Rahimi and Recht, 2007
; 
Rahimi and Recht, 2008
; 
Le et al., 2013
)
 provide one such concrete example, replacing the random MLP target with a target whose spectral density and coordinate-wise independence are directly specified.
This method provides an explicit control knob for shaping the novelty scale without changing the downstream RL algorithm.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows.
Section 
2
 defines the coverage-based failure mode we want RND to avoid.
Section 
3
 analyzes why the residual can collapse by expressing RND energy as a cross-kernel residual variance. Section 
4
 turns this mechanism into a target-design principle, introduces 
RFF-RND
, and provides a frequency-domain interpretation of the same mechanism. Section 
5
 diagnoses our theory and empirically evaluates our methods on downstream RL tasks.

2.3 
RND Variants through the Lens of Kernel Geometry

We next situate this coverage-based failure mode relative to existing RND variants.

Predictor- and target-focused RND variants.

Many RND variants implicitly modify one side of the kernel geometry. Conditional RND 
(
Nikulin et al., 2023
)
 conditions the predictor via FiLM-style modulation 
(
de Vries et al., 2017
; 
Perez et al., 2018
; 
Dumoulin et al., 2018
)
, inducing a condition-dependent feature map that deforms the resulting geometry. In contrast, Distributional RND (DRND) 
(
Yang et al., 2024
)
 acts on the target side. It replaces the prediction error induced by a single target network with the ensemble variance induced by multiple target networks, thereby capturing uncertainty across random target draws. Although these approaches showed favorable performance improvements, they did not identify the underlying cause.

Why cross-kernel geometry matters.

These variants suggest that 
both
 sides of the RND residual matter: the predictor geometry determines how target values are interpolated, while the target geometry determines what variation is available to be detected. In the following sections, we make this interaction explicit by characterizing RND energy as a cross-kernel residual variance.

6 
Conclusion and Future Direction

In this paper, we rigorously analyzed random network distillation (RND) overgeneralization under a GP target model and an NTK-regime predictor, showing that the RND score is a cross-kernel residual variance determined by the interaction between target covariance and predictor interpolation. This reveals residual collapse as a spectral under-excitation problem, where the target fails to excite modes in which predictor residuals can survive. Motivated by this mechanism, we proposed 
RFF-RND
, a bandwidth-controlled target design that makes the target spectrum and spatial novelty scale explicit. Diagnostics and RL experiments show improved novelty discrimination and downstream performance, highlighting target geometry as a central design choice for reliable RND.

The theoretical framework proposed in this paper imposes no specific restrictions on the input domain. Specializing it to multiple input modalities, such as images, text, and time series, and analyzing the additional structures that arise in each case is therefore an interesting direction for future research.

Stochastic nuisance variation and the noisy-TV problem.

Spectral target design does not by itself eliminate the classical noisy-TV
failure mode. Our analysis predicts persistent novelty whenever target
variation overlaps with directions that remain difficult for the predictor,
regardless of whether such variation is task-relevant or arises from
irreducible stochasticity. Hence, allocating more target power to
predictor-hard directions should not be interpreted as a monotone preference
for arbitrarily high-frequency target spectra.

NeurIPS Paper Checklist

The checklist is designed to encourage best practices for responsible machine learning research, addressing issues of reproducibility, transparency, research ethics, and societal impact. Do not remove the checklist: 
The papers not including the checklist will be desk rejected.
 The checklist should follow the references and follow the (optional) supplemental material. The checklist does NOT count towards the page
limit.

Please read the checklist guidelines carefully for information on how to answer these questions. For each question in the checklist:

•

You should answer 
[Yes] 
, 
[No] 
, or 
[N/A] 
.

•

[N/A] 
 means either that the question is Not Applicable for that particular paper or the relevant information is Not Available.

•

Please provide a short (1–2 sentence) justification right after your answer (even for 
[N/A] 
).

The checklist answers are an integral part of your paper submission.
 They are visible to the reviewers, area chairs, senior area chairs, and ethics reviewers. You will also be asked to include it (after eventual revisions) with the final version of your paper, and its final version will be published with the paper.

The reviewers of your paper will be asked to use the checklist as one of the factors in their evaluation. While 
[Yes] 
 is generally preferable to 
[No] 
, it is perfectly acceptable to answer 
[No] 
 provided a proper justification is given (e.g., error bars are not reported because it would be too computationally expensive” or “we were unable to find the license for the dataset we used”). In general, answering 
[No] 
 or 
[N/A] 
 is not grounds for rejection. While the questions are phrased in a binary way, we acknowledge that the true answer is often more nuanced, so please just use your best judgment and write a justification to elaborate. All supporting evidence can appear either in the main paper or the supplemental material, provided in appendix. If you answer 
[Yes] 
 to a question, in the justification please point to the section(s) where related material for the question can be found.

(b)

In the case of closed-source models, it may be that access to the model is limited in some way (e.g., to registered users), but it should be possible for other researchers to have some path to reproducing or verifying the results.

For example, it is legitimate to point out that an improvement in the quality of generative models could be used to generate Deepfakes for disinformation. On the other hand, it is not needed to point out that a generic algorithm for optimizing neural networks could enable people to train models that generate Deepfakes faster.

•

The authors should consider possible harms that could arise when the technology is being used as intended and functioning correctly, harms that could arise when the technology is being used as intended but gives incorrect results, and harms following from (intentional or unintentional) misuse of the technology.

•

If there are negative societal impacts, the authors could also discuss possible mitigation strategies (e.g., gated release of models, providing defenses in addition to attacks, mechanisms for monitoring misuse, mechanisms to monitor how a system learns from feedback over time, improving the efficiency and accessibility of ML).

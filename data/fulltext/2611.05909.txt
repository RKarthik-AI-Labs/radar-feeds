Do Saliency Maps Fail Dense Breasts? A Chance-Normalized,
Confounder-Aware Audit of Explanation Fairness in Mammography

Ahmed Jaheen

Abstract

Saliency maps, offered as evidence that mammography classifiers use the
cancer, are untested in dense breasts, which can hide cancers. We tested
whether they locate cancers worse there, and why. On 1,345 malignant
CBIS-DDSM views, we scored three CNNs’ Grad-CAM, Integrated Gradients and
occlusion maps by whether their peak hits the lesion and a
chance-normalized lesion-to-background saliency ratio (bits; 
1 doubles
it). Unadjusted gaps were large: Grad-CAM peaks hit 44% of dense vs. 58%
of non-dense lesions. Case mix, chiefly more calcifications in dense
breasts, explained about half or more of each gap. Occlusion kept a
significant average of 
0.56-bit gap. Controls also implicate the classifiers: unadjusted gaps
exceeded an image-only conspicuity map’s, and models relied less on
dense-breast lesions. We recommend reporting density gaps before and after
case-mix adjustment, per classifier, with conspicuity and lesion-reliance
controls.

Index Terms: 
Explainable AI, fairness, mammography, breast density, saliency evaluation

Introduction

A saliency map is a heat map of the image regions that most influenced a
classifier’s output. Such maps are routinely shown with mammography
classifiers: a map that lights up the lesion is read as evidence that the
malignancy score rests on the cancer 
[
21
]
. Breast density,
the share of fibroglandular tissue, is graded in four BI-RADS (Breast
Imaging Reporting and Data System) categories, from 1 (almost entirely
fatty) to 4 (extremely dense). Dense tissue looks white on a mammogram, as
many lesions do, and can hide them (masking) 
[
6
]
. Deep models
detect and classify lesions less reliably in dense
breasts 
[
10
, 
31
]
. Unlike demographic
attributes, density changes the image itself. If saliency maps also degrade
with density, the patients whose images are hardest to read would receive
the least reliable visual evidence.

Studies of saliency maps in mammography compare them with lesion
annotations only in aggregate or per finding
type 
[
20
, 
7
]
.
Pertuz et al. 
[
20
]
 called for analyses by density, and
a 2026 systematic review 
[
21
]
 found none. Group gaps in
explanation quality occur in tabular and text
data 
[
3
, 
9
]
 and can stem from the data
rather than from the saliency method 
[
19
]
. In
medical imaging, saliency maps have been compared between demographic
groups 
[
28
, 
11
]
,
but without lesion-level ground truth, so a difference between maps cannot
be read as a difference in quality. Subgroup audits in mammography that
adjust for case differences cover classifier performance
only 
[
31
]
.

Two common localization scores are the pointing game (does the map’s peak
fall on the lesion?) and the share of saliency inside the lesion mask, the
radiologist’s outline of the lesion. A fair comparison across density must
handle two problems. First, each score has a chance level, the value that a
map with no information reaches, which depends on lesion area and
location 
[
2
]
. A map of typical lesion locations can
outscore trained classifiers 
[
4
]
, and small lesions are harder to
localize 
[
24
]
. Second, dense and non-dense
breasts hold different cases. Among malignant CBIS-DDSM 
[
15
]

views, dense breasts are smaller (median 159 vs. 198 cm
2
). Their
lesions are more often calcifications (58.7% vs. 31.8%), clusters of tiny
calcium specks, and less often masses, which are solid lumps. Such
differences in case mix, the kinds of lesions and images in each group, are
confounders: they differ between the groups and also affect the score.

We test whether saliency maps are fair across density: whether, for
comparable lesions, they locate the cancer equally well, beyond chance, in
dense (BI-RADS 3 or 4) and non-dense (1 or 2) breasts. Localization alone is
a weak proxy for explanation quality 
[
8
]
, since a
map can mark the lesion while the classifier relies elsewhere. We therefore
also test whether the maps are faithful (mark the pixels the classifier
uses) and whether the classifier relies on the lesion. Fig. 
1
 outlines the study. Our
contributions are:

•

To our knowledge, the first audit of post-hoc saliency maps of
mammography classifiers against per-lesion masks across breast density,
for Grad-CAM, Integrated Gradients (IG) and occlusion. Maps are scored
against chance 
[
14
, 
2
]
 with the
pointing game and the energy lift, a lesion-to-background saliency ratio
in bits. We add a faithfulness test (ROAD 
[
23
]
) and a
lesion-reliance test.

•

Large crude gaps for every method (Grad-CAM: 14 fewer lesion hits
per 100 cancers). Case mix, chiefly lesion type, explains most of the
Grad-CAM and IG energy-lift gaps and about half of occlusion’s.
Occlusion keeps a significant gap in our primary test, concentrated in
one architecture; with method-specific case-mix adjustment
(exploratory), no gap is significant.

•

An audit recipe: crude and adjusted gaps with three controls. The
crude gaps exceed a model-free conspicuity map’s, vanish with randomized
network weights (Grad-CAM; IG on ResNet-50) and come with lower lesion
reliance, so they partly reflect what the classifiers use.

Methods

2.1 
Data and classifiers

CBIS-DDSM 
[
15
, 
25
]
 provides 3,568
radiologist-outlined lesions on 3,103 digitized film images of 1,566
patients. A view is one breast imaged from above (craniocaudal, CC) or from
the side (mediolateral oblique, MLO); merging 71 duplicates leaves 3,032
views. A view is malignant if any of its lesions is; its lesion mask 

combines its malignant outlines. The primary set 
 holds the 1,345
malignant views with known density (751 patients). Density was graded with
the BI-RADS lexicon that preceded the 5th edition; categories
1 and 2 are
non-dense (713 views), 3 and 4 dense (632). The breast mask 
 is the
largest region above 2% of the intensity range, joined with 
.

Three convolutional neural networks (CNNs), ResNet-50 
[
12
]
,
EfficientNet-B3 
[
30
]
 and
ConvNeXt-T 
[
17
]
, start from ImageNet
weights. Each outputs a malignancy logit 
, the
score before the sigmoid. Every view holds a lesion that a radiologist
found, so the task is to tell malignant from benign lesions. It is not
screening: cancers fully hidden by dense tissue are absent. Patient-grouped 5-fold
cross-validation gives 15 classifiers (one per architecture and fold), so
each view is classified and explained by a classifier that never saw its
patient. Training used AdamW with early stopping on the area under the
receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of a validation split inside
the training folds; settings were tuned on one fold. A temperature 
,
which calibrates the scores, and a decision threshold 
 are fitted on the
validation logits.

2.3 
Comparing dense and non-dense breasts

A gap is the dense-group value minus the non-dense value; a negative gap
means worse maps in dense breasts. The crude gap is the plain difference of
the group means (views averaged over architectures). It describes the maps
that dense-breast patients receive. Its 95% confidence interval (CI) comes
from 2,000 bootstrap resamples of patients.

To separate density from case mix, we regress each score on density and
other factors with generalized estimating equations
(GEE) 
[
16
]
, a regression that gives valid CIs when
several views come from one patient. One regression, with exchangeable
correlation and bias-reduced standard errors 
[
18
]
,
covers all architectures and methods, with one intercept per classifier and
method.
Its density coefficient 
 is the adjusted gap, an association and not
a causal effect. Each factor’s coefficient (slope) is shared across methods,
so all three get the same case-mix adjustment and their gaps are directly
comparable; method-specific slopes are a sensitivity analysis.
Four nested models add factors, and each answers one question
(Fig. 
3
):

•

M0 has density only: how much worse are maps in dense breasts?

•

M1 adds case mix: log lesion and breast area, lesion type (mass or
calcification), view, multiple lesions, an ill-defined margin, and an
acquisition proxy (image stored left-right flipped, a marker of scanner
or site).
Among comparable lesions, does density still matter? M1 is the main
model.

•

M2 adds classifier accuracy on the view: whether the classifier was
correct and how confident it was (calibrated margin 
). Is the
gap explained by how hard the view was for the classifier?

•

M3 adds lesion visibility: the radiologist’s subtlety rating and
how much the lesion stands out in the image (the DoG map’s 
,
Sec. 
2.4
). Is the gap explained by how visible the
lesion is?

Masking makes lesions harder to see and to classify, so the M2 and M3
factors may lie on the path from density to the map. Their gaps describe
what remains once these factors are held equal. Masking also affects which
lesions are detected, so lesion type, margin, lesion area and multiple
lesions may follow from density too, whereas acquisition, view and breast
area precede it. M1 may therefore remove part of a real density effect, and
an exploratory confounder-only model keeps only these three. For the
pointing game, a logistic GEE gives the
adjusted gap in hits per 100 cancers (percentage points, pp) by
g-computation 
[
22
]
, which compares predicted hit rates with
all views set to dense and then to non-dense. We also refit M1 within
masses and within calcifications (28 mixed views excluded).

The primary test is each method’s M1 
 gap, Holm-corrected over
the three methods 
[
13
]
. Holm’s method raises

that the chance of any false positive stays at most 5%. A second Holm
family of 25 tests covers the pointing-game and faithfulness (SRG) gaps,
the density

non-significant gap is not proof of no gap, so two one-sided tests
(TOST) 
[
26

equivalent to zero if its 90% CI lies within

marked exploratory. All results use one training seed, plus two more seeds
for Grad-CAM.

2.4 
Controls: image, classifier or saliency method?

The difference-of-Gaussians (DoG) conspicuity map,

, subtracts the image 
 blurred at 8 mm from 

blurred at 1 mm. It marks small spots that stand out from their
surroundings and uses no classifier, so a method’s gap larger than the DoG
gap is not explained by the image alone. Randomized networks, with every
learned weight replaced by a random one 
[
1
]
, test
whether a gap needs learned weights (Grad-CAM, all classifiers; IG,
ResNet-50).

Faithfulness asks whether a map marks the pixels the classifier uses.
ROAD (RemOve And Debias) 
[
23
]
 replaces 1% to 20% of the
breast pixels with values interpolated from neighbours, most- or
least-salient pixels first (the median lesion covers 1.3%). The symmetric
relevance gain (SRG) 
[
5
]
 is how much more the
calibrated score 
 drops when the most-salient pixels are removed first
than when the least-salient are, averaged over five removal fractions. A faithful map has SRG above 0; an uninformative
one, 0. Lesion-specific reliance (LSR) asks whether the
classifier uses the lesion. It is the drop in 
 when 
 is filled in
from the surrounding tissue, minus the mean drop for up to five same-shaped regions elsewhere in 
.
LSR above 0 means the classifier relies on the lesion more than on random
tissue.

4 
Discussion and Conclusion

In our data, dense-breast patients received worse maps, in large part
because their cancers were more often calcifications, which scored lower
than masses. Lesion type may be a confounder or, through masking and
detection, a step on the path from density to the map. If it is a
confounder, the M1 gap is the fair comparison; if it lies on that path, the
confounder-only gap is.

Limitations.
 We used a single digitized-film dataset with reader-assigned
density; label noise likely biases 
 toward 0, and replication on digital
mammograms is future work.

In conclusion, (i) the crude gaps were large for every method. (ii)
Accounting for case mix, chiefly lesion type, removed about half or more of
each gap; occlusion’s primary-test gap was concentrated in one
architecture, and with method-specific slopes (exploratory) all three
adjusted 
 gaps were about 
0.2 bit, none significant. (iii)
The controls point partly to the classifiers: the crude gaps exceeded the
conspicuity map’s, needed trained weights (Grad-CAM; IG on ResNet-50) and
came with lower lesion reliance. Audits should report crude,
confounder-only and adjusted gaps per classifier, with conspicuity,
randomized-network and lesion-reliance controls.

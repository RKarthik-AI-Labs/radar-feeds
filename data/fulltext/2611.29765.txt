Abstract

Source cues are central to political communication, yet reception studies with large language model (LLM) personas as synthetic audiences have not tested whether personas respond to who speaks and not only to what is said.
With Spain’s general election of 29 November 2026 in mind, we run a speaker-attribution experiment using 1,075 anonymised X posts by the leaders of PSOE, PP and VOX on four topics (the economy and employment, household costs, immigration and corruption) and 791 distinct value-grounded, LLM-generated personas. Each post was assigned to 300 topic-selected persona profiles, 150 grounded in the Inglehart–Welzel cultural map and 150 in Moral Foundations Theory, under four conditions: no author, Sánchez, Feijóo or Abascal, yielding 322,470 complete persona–post quartets.
Naming any leader lowered support for identical text,
with the penalty for Abascal nearly twice as large as those for Sánchez and Feijóo. The penalty follows the persona’s elicited left–right self-placement (ideological congruence): left personas spare Sánchez and right personas spare Feijóo, although ideology was never in the message-evaluation prompt.
Corruption posts were penalised under every name, whereas on other topics the ideologically closest leader generally produced little or no penalty.
The leader order matches public opinion in 48 CIS barometers (2022–2026) in all three groups and the sign of all twelve differences, but fits barometers before the model’s 2024 knowledge cutoff better than recent ones.
Our findings demonstrate that LLM personas evaluate political speakers through systematic, value-congruent priors:
however, while they replicate human candidate rank-orders across survey barometers,
this alignment is ordinal rather than quantitative,
highlighting their utility for controlled source-effect experiments though not as substitutes for empirical public opinion surveys.

Introduction

In political communication the speaker is part of the message. The same proposal is judged differently when it comes from one’s own party or from a rival, and party cues can outweigh the content of a policy 
(
Cohen, 2003
; 
Druckman et al., 2013
)
. Large language models are now used to simulate political audiences, with personas standing in for groups of citizens. To our knowledge, 
whether such personas respond to 
who
 speaks, and not only to 
what
 is said, has not been tested
, because reception studies with synthetic audiences have shown messages with their author either always visible or always hidden.
The study we extend 
(
Greco et al., 2026b
)
 examined how value-grounded personas receive the posts of a single fixed anonymized actor. Because speaker identity did not vary, that design could not identify source-cue effects independently of message content.
This paper runs the classic source-cue experiment on synthetic audiences. We take 1,075 anonymised X posts by the leaders of Spain’s three main parties, Pedro Sánchez (PSOE), Alberto Núñez Feijóo (PP) and Santiago Abascal (VOX), on four salient topics: the economy and employment, household costs, immigration and corruption. The experiment uses 791 distinct value-grounded personas instantiated with 
gpt-oss-20b
; for each topic, 150 persona profiles grounded in the Inglehart–Welzel cultural map (IW; 
Inglehart and Welzel, 2005
) and 150 grounded in Moral Foundations Theory (MFT; 
Graham et al., 2013
) evaluate every post under four conditions that differ only in the displayed author: no author, Sánchez, Feijóo or Abascal.
Because every post is shown under every name, the effect of a name is measured on identical text within the same audience. Spain is well suited to the question. Its party system has a centre-left government, a centre-right main opposition and a radical-right third party, so attribution can be crossed with ideological distance. The monthly barometers of the Spanish Centre for Sociological Research (Centro de Investigaciones Sociológicas, CIS) rate all three leaders and ask the left–right self-placement question we also put to the personas.

We ask four questions. 
RQ1
: how does attributing the same message to Sánchez, Feijóo or Abascal, rather than presenting it anonymously, change its evaluation? 
RQ2
: does the effect depend on the persona’s elicited left–right self-placement, so that personas respond more positively to the leader closest to them?

RQ3
: Does the magnitude or direction of the attribution effect vary across salient political topics? 
RQ4
: do left, centre and right personas rank the three leaders as the same groups of CIS respondents do?

Naming any leader lowered support for the same message, and Abascal’s name did so about twice as much as the other two (
, 
 and 
 on a 
 to 
 stance scale). The overall effect hides opposite reactions: left personas penalised Sánchez least and Abascal most, right personas penalised Feijóo least and Sánchez most, and all three congruence contrasts were positive, although the message-evaluation prompt never included the persona’s elicited ideological placement. Only on corruption did every name cost support in every group. Elsewhere the closest leader’s name carried no penalty. The order in which each group prefers the leaders is the order found in 48 CIS barometers from 2022 to 2026, in every group and in the sign of all twelve differences, but the match is dated: it holds in every barometer up to the model’s June 2024 knowledge cutoff and weakens among right respondents from 2025.

Our contributions are: (1) the first fully crossed speaker-attribution design for synthetic audiences, with anonymisation and counterfactual attribution, complemented by a pre-experimental audit of whether the model recognises the leaders;
(2) an elicited CIS-style self-placement whose associations with the personas’ underlying moral and cultural values align directionally with relationships observed in human samples;
(3) a comparison with 48 CIS barometers, limited to order and direction, which also dates the personas’ picture of the leaders; and (4) code, configuration, audit artifacts, post identifiers and derived labels, released without post text.

Related Work

LLM personas as synthetic audiences.

Language models conditioned on demographic or biographical profiles have been used to simulate survey respondents 
(
Argyle et al., 2023
)
 and, after fine-tuning on survey data, to predict how subpopulations distribute their answers 
(
Suh et al., 2025
)
. The limits of the approach are documented in the same literature: models reflect uneven defaults rather than the opinions of the groups they are asked to play 
(
Santurkar et al., 2023
)
, are harder to steer towards profiles whose traits rarely co-occur 
(
Liu et al., 2024
)
, flatten the groups they portray 
(
Wang et al., 2025
)
, learn as well from irrelevant or misleading prompts as from instructive ones 
(
Webson and Pavlick, 2022
)
 and change what they simulate with the form of the persona cue 
(
Lutz et al., 2025
; 
Weeber et al., 2026
)
. Synthetic samples can reproduce averages while missing the covariance structure of real opinion and adding a bias of their own 
(
Bisbee et al., 2024
; 
Boelaert et al., 2025
; 
von der Heyde et al., 2026
)
, and a review of 63 persona studies asks for personas grounded in empirical data and for a stated target population 
(
Batzner et al., 2025
)
. Two recent studies come close to our setting. Left- and right-leaning personas polarise the endorsement of persuasive content drawn from real media 
(
Antelmi et al., 2026
)
, and political personas judge evidence by its fit with their assigned identity 
(
Dash et al., 2026
)
, but neither varies the speaker. Our own use of personas is narrower than survey simulation. Every comparison in this paper is between conditions judged by the same audience, and the comparison with survey data concerns order and direction only.

Source cues in political communication.

That the messenger changes the reception of a message is one of the best-established results on political opinion. The same policy is judged differently when it carries a party label 
(
Cohen, 2003
)
, cues from the opposing party move evaluations even more than cues from one’s own 
(
Nicholson, 2012
)
, the effect grows with elite polarisation 
(
Druckman et al., 2013
)
 and can override the respondent’s stated ideology 
(
Barber and Pope, 2019
)
, although citizens who do have policy information weigh it at least as much as the cue 
(
Bullock, 2011
)
. Spain, which ranks among the most affectively polarised party systems in Europe 
(
Reiljan, 2020
; 
Torcal and Comellas, 2022
)
, is a strong case study. Exposure to the leaders’ own accounts is a separate matter: following a candidate on Twitter did not increase affective polarisation in a panel quasi-experiment during the 2019 European Parliament campaign 
(
Lorenzo-Rodríguez and
Torcal, 2022
)
. Language models already carry a view of these leaders: across 14 models, sentiment towards Sánchez is neutral to positive, towards Feijóo moderate and towards Abascal markedly negative 
(
Goyanes et al., 2025
)
. They are also sensitive to who is said to speak. Naming the source of a statement breaks the agreement between models that rate it 
(
Germani and Spitale, 2025
)
, and a party cue changes the sentiment label that models give the same statement 
(
Vallejo Vera and Driggers, 2025
)
. A news outlet’s name pulls the political bias that models assign to an unchanged article towards the outlet’s known leaning 
(
Park and Kim, 2025
)
. The politician named in a sentence shifts its sentiment classification, less so when the names are fictional 
(
Elbouanani et al., 2025
)
, and an audit of 1.9 billion data points in which only the entity name varies finds that models penalise right-wing politicians and favour left-wing ones 
(
Elbouanani et al., 2026
)
.
These studies generally frame source-induced shifts as model bias or annotation instability. We instead study them as persona-conditioned reception effects: holding message content fixed, we examine both the overall effect of a leader’s name and how that effect varies across value-grounded persona profiles. This design distinguishes the common speaker-attribution effect from heterogeneity associated with persona values, although it does not eliminate the political priors of the underlying model 
(
Feng et al., 2023
; 
Rozado, 2024
; 
Motoki et al., 2024
; 
Bernardelle et al., 2025
)
.

Value frameworks and moral reception.

The personas are selected with two frameworks, Moral Foundations Theory with its MFQ-2 measure 
(
Graham et al., 2013
; 
Atari et al., 2023
)
 and the Inglehart–Welzel cultural map 
(
Inglehart and Welzel, 2005
)
. Both are related to political ideology in human samples, where liberals and conservatives weigh the foundations differently 
(
Graham et al., 2009
; 
Kivikangas et al., 2021
)
, which lets us check whether the elicited placement of the personas follows their values. The persona resource is that of 
Greco et al. (2026a)
, who generate personas from World Values Survey variables and check their placement on the Inglehart–Welzel map and their moral profiles. The reception protocol comes from 
Greco et al. (2026b)
, who studied how such personas receive the messages of a single actor. This paper adds a speaker manipulation and an ideology layer to that protocol.

A persona–post pair enters the analysis only when all four conditions are observed, forming a complete quartet. Every effect is, therefore, a within-pair comparison.

The design is fully crossed: every post is shown under all three names, whoever wrote it. Therefore, differences between names cannot come from the posts, since each individual post appears in every condition, nor from the personas, since each persona judges the same posts in every condition, and must come from the name itself. The counterfactual attributions exist only inside the prompts: the real author of every post is known and used in the analysis, but never shown to the model.

Data

4.1 
Posts

We collected every post published by the official X accounts of the three leaders between 1 April 2022, when Feijóo became leader of the PP, and 2 October 2026, through the full-archive search of the X API v2 and excluding retweets. This gave 10,863 posts, 7,625 of them original.

 Only original posts are used as stimuli, because quotes and replies depend on another author’s text, which would be missing from the stimulus or add a second, often identifiable, voice. We applied the quality filters of 
Greco et al. (2026b)
, which discard very short or low-information posts (here, under 40 characters or 7 words), and removed duplicates.

4.2 
Topics

The topics were fixed by a rule before any data were downloaded, to tie them to public salience and to what the leaders actually post about. For each issue we averaged, over the 46 monthly CIS barometers in the window with a usable table, the share of respondents naming it among the three main problems of Spain (the CIS question asks for up to three), and we kept the most salient issues on which each leader had at least ten original posts. This selected four topics: (i) the economy and employment, (ii) household costs (housing, energy and prices), (iii) immigration and the border, and (iv) corruption. Health, third by salience, was dropped because Abascal had four posts on it.

Posts were matched to topics with a list of Spanish keywords and phrases for each topic, built for precision (Appendix

4.3 
Anonymisation and attribution

Before prompting, the three leaders, their parties, campaign hashtags and user handles were replaced by placeholders (
[POLITICAL_ACTOR]
, 
[PARTY]
, 
[CAMPAIGN_TAG]
, 
[USER]
). Other politicians’ names were kept, as replacing them would empty many posts, especially on corruption. Posts in which a name survived, or that became too short, were excluded, which left 7,299 of the 7,625 original posts (Appendix 
A.1
, Table 
6
). All four conditions show the same anonymised text and differ only in the attribution line of the prompt.

Anonymisation hides names but not positions. A post that speaks for the Government fits Sánchez and no one else. A keyword rule for such markers (speaking for or against the Government, attacking the right, the left or the PP) flags 46% of the stimuli as implausible under at least one name, though it misses more than it catches. Filtering on it would leave too few posts per cell, so we kept the fully crossed design and report the effect separately for true, plausible and implausible attributions (Appendix 
A.5
, Table 
13
).

4.4 
Personas

We use the persona resource of 
Greco et al. (2026a)
, as 
Greco et al. (2026b)
 did. Its personas are LLM-generated profiles built from ten World Values Survey variables, each with a short biography, Inglehart–Welzel (IW) coordinates and MFQ-2 moral-foundation scores 
(
Inglehart and Welzel, 2005
; 
Atari et al., 2023
)
. As in that paper, personas are selected under two frameworks, which also define what the model sees: IW personas are described by their biography and their survival–self-expression and traditional–secular-rational coordinates, and MFT personas by their biography, six foundation scores and 36 MFQ-2 item answers.
For each topic and framework, we selected 50 personas at each of three topic-conditioned profile levels (
high
, 
mid
 and 
low
; Appendix 
B.1
). This gives 600 slots per framework and 791 framework-specific persona profiles (441 IW and 350 MFT), as some personas serve several topics.
The levels are defined in the value space and never appear in the prompt (Appendix 
B.1
, Table 
15
). Ideology enters only through the self-placement described next.

The resource has no left–right variable, so we elicited one. Each persona answered the CIS self-placement question (from 1, furthest to the left, to 10, furthest to the right of Spanish politics) once, in a separate call with the same system prompt and persona text and no post. We grouped personas as left (1–4), centre (5–6) or right (7–10) and applied the same cut-offs to CIS respondents. The 1–10 scale has no single midpoint, so its two central values form the centre 
(
Aybar et al., 2024
)
, and studies of polarisation on the CIS series use the same left and right blocks 
(
Torcal and Carty, 2026
)
. Figure 
2
 also reports each placement separately. The groups hold 27%, 44% and 28% of IW personas and 39%, 26% and 35% of MFT personas. The answer is the model’s reading of the persona and serves only to group it, but it is far from arbitrary: although the persona descriptions contain no explicit ideological label and the elicited placement is never included in the message-evaluation prompt, self-placement follows the values shown to the model in directions consistent with relationships observed in human samples.
MFT personas sit further left with care and equality and further right with proportionality, authority and loyalty (Spearman

4.5 
CIS barometers

For RQ4 we use the microdata of the 48 monthly CIS barometers from April 2022 to October 2026, the period of the posts 
(
Centro de Investigaciones
Sociológicas, 2026b
)
. Every barometer asks respondents to rate each leader from 1 to 10 and to place themselves on the same left–right scale as the personas. We kept the 175,764 of 193,263 respondents who placed themselves and rated all three leaders, computed weighted mean ratings within each barometer, and averaged the barometers with equal weight (Appendix

5 
Experimental Setup

Model and decoding.

All judgments were generated with 
gpt-oss-20b
 
(
OpenAI, 2025
)
, an open-weight model with a stated knowledge cutoff of June 2024 and the model used by 
Greco et al. (2026b)
. It was served with vLLM through an OpenAI-compatible API, with temperature 0, a fixed seed, a limit of 4,096 tokens and JSON output. Each call presents a bundle of ten posts of its topic to one persona under one condition.
Generation was run on four NVIDIA A30 GPUs with 24 GB of memory each, with one independent vLLM replica serving the same model on each GPU. The economy-and-employment and immigration-and-border runs were completed on 27 October 2026, and the household-costs and corruption runs on 2 November 2026.

Prompt.

The system prompt asks the model to role-play the persona, to treat the displayed attribution as given, and, when no speaker is named, not to infer one from style, topic or outside knowledge. The user prompt contains the persona profile, a line stating that the messages concern current political issues in Spain, the condition line (“every message below is attributed to [name]” or “no speaker identity is provided”), the evaluation rules, the ten anonymised messages and the output schema. Prompts are in English and the posts stay in Spanish. For each message the model returns a stance (support, neutral or reject) and four 1–5 ratings: agreement, legitimacy, offensiveness and endorsement (whether the persona would share the message as written), plus two short free-text fields that we do not analyse (full template in Appendix 
A.3
, Figure 
3
). Of the 322,500 persona–post pairs, 322,470 (99.99%) were observed in all four conditions and form the analysis set (Appendix 
A.6
, Table 
14
).

Knowledge audit.

Before the experiment, we checked that the model knows the three names. We asked it 20 times about each leader and about three control names, two invented but plausible Spanish names and one opaque code. It recognised each leader and named the correct party every time, placed Sánchez, Feijóo and Abascal at 3, 8 and 9 on the left–right scale with a spread of at most one point, and never recognised a control name (Appendix 
A.4
, Table 
10
). This establishes the model’s left-to-right ordering of the leaders used in RQ2 and shows that the attribution line carries the intended identities.

Estimation.

Stance is coded

(

7 
Discussion and Conclusion

The name changes the judgment of identical text, and in the direction that source-cue research on human respondents would predict.
Outside corruption, attribution to the ideologically closest leader produced little or no penalty, whereas attribution to a rival reduced support. Overall, Abascal’s name produced the largest penalty, consistent with the negative sentiment towards Abascal expressed by language models 
(
Goyanes et al., 2025
)
 and with the broader penalty for right-wing politicians documented by 
Elbouanani et al. (2026)
.
The pattern is one of repulsion more than attraction. Personas were rarely more favourable to a message because their own side signed it, but they were less favourable when the other side did, as human respondents react more to out-party than to in-party cues 
(
Nicholson, 2012
)
. That any name lowers support relative to the anonymous version suggests that an attributed statement may be read as a partisan act. The reading is harshest on corruption, where every group penalised every leader, including its own.

The personas were not assigned an explicit political ideology:
they were conditioned on biographies and value profiles
, while their
left–right placement was elicited afterwards and was never shown
during message evaluation. That placement was systematically associated with the MFQ-2 and Inglehart–Welzel dimensions in directions consistent with relationships observed in human samples.

Greco et al. (2026b)
 found that value-grounded personas differ in how they receive the same messages. Here that differentiation turns out to be politically organised, so that a value profile carries a position on who may say what.

The comparison with the CIS shows how far that picture resembles Spanish opinion, and where it stops. Order and direction match in every group and every pairwise difference, and scale cannot be compared at all, since a speaker-attribution effect on a message and a rating of a leader are different quantities. The match is also dated. The personas reproduce the ordering of the barometers up to the model’s knowledge cutoff and miss the movement of right respondents towards Abascal since 2025.
A synthetic audience may carry dated political associations inherited from its training data.
Before personas are used as an audience in a live campaign, the date of the picture they carry should be checked, as RQ4 does here, and their output should not be read as current opinion.

Although the pooled leader order matches in all three ideological groups, agreement is not perfect wave by wave: the persona order matches 48 of 48 barometers for the left, 39 of 48 for the centre, and 32 of 48 for the right. Moreover, ordering only three ideologically separated leaders is a low-dimensional benchmark. The match may reflect broad learned associations about the Spanish political spectrum rather than fine-grained simulation of voter behaviour, and should therefore be treated as a limited external-validity check rather than evidence of survey equivalence.

Value-grounded personas respond to who speaks as well as to what is said, coherently and along ideological lines. That makes them useful for studying source effects, and it does not make them stand-ins for voters. Several extensions follow. The most immediate is to repeat the experiment with two further open-weight models, to see whether the pattern is specific to 
gpt-oss-20b
. Labelling the posts by electoral period would show whether the speaker effect grows during campaigns, and asking the personas the CIS leader-rating item would make a like-with-like comparison of scale possible. The keyword lower bound on how much an anonymised post still reveals its author could be replaced by a model-based plausibility check of each attribution.

Limitations

The results come from one model, 
gpt-oss-20b
, whose knowledge ends in June 2024 while the posts run to October 2026. The size of the penalties, the concentration of IW personas at the scale midpoint and the picture of the leaders may all be specific to it.
Posts about later events are judged by a model that does not know them, and its representation of the three leaders reflects information available only up to mid-2024, as the divergence from right-wing CIS respondents after 2025 suggests.
Two further open-weight models are planned.

The personas are generated profiles, not people, and their ideology is one elicited answer each. We did not test the stability of that answer under rewordings of the question, and a scale anchored to Spanish politics was put to personas most of whom are not Spanish. Among IW personas 41% answered exactly 5, against 22% of CIS respondents (Appendix 
B.2
), so the centre group is heterogeneous, although the by-placement results do not depend on the cut-offs.

We evaluate value-grounded personas based on IW and MFT profiles,
but do not benchmark them against alternative steering methods like simple demographic prompting or zero-shot baselines.
Because persona behavior in LLMs is highly prompt-dependent, our results characterize value-grounded synthetic audiences specifically, without extending to other persona conditioning techniques.

The anonymous baseline is not fully anonymous. Names and party labels were removed, but the content of a post often reveals its author’s position. The keyword flags that mark 46% of the stimuli as implausible under some name are a lower bound, and we ran no model-based plausibility or leakage probe. The design stays fully crossed, so the counterfactual attributions include implausible ones, and the split by true, plausible and implausible attribution is descriptive, because the three categories hold different posts.

The fully crossed design also creates source–content combinations that are implausible and, in some cases, internally inconsistent
The especially large penalties in these cases cannot be attributed uniquely to partisan rejection: they may also reflect sensitivity to source–content mismatch or difficulty sustaining the assigned role. Future work should separate these mechanisms using independent plausibility ratings and explicit contradiction measures.

The stimuli are original X posts on four topics (the economy and employment, household costs, immigration and corruption), assigned by a keyword rule whose main-topic precision was 86% in a manual check, with thin cells for Abascal on household costs and Sánchez on corruption. The design has one leader on the left and two on the right. The space to the left of the PSOE had no single leader over the period: Yolanda Díaz stepped down as leader of Sumar in June 2024, and the parties of the Frente Amplio named Ada Colau their candidate on 12 October 2026, after the posts were collected. That space is the natural place for a fourth leader, and the CIS rates Díaz in all 48 barometers. The prompt template and the persona profiles are in English while the posts are in Spanish, and a fully Spanish run is left for future work.

The CIS comparison is limited to order and sign, since a speaker-attribution effect on a message and a rating of a leader cannot be put on one scale. The CIS intervals ignore the clustering of the sample design, and agreement in order does not make the personas representative of Spanish citizens.

Ethical considerations

The study uses public posts by three public officials, collected through the official X API under its terms, and the public microdata of the CIS, reused under its conditions of use with attribution. Public statements by politicians are still personal data under the GDPR. They were processed for academic research only, stored in institutional storage, and not used to profile any private individual. We release code, configuration, audit artifacts, post identifiers and derived labels, but no post text.

Anonymisation is an experimental control. We make no claim that the posts are private or unidentifiable, and the counterfactual attributions exist only inside the prompts: the paper never presents a post as a statement by a leader who did not write it, and the example in Appendix 
A.2
, Table 
9
 names its real author.

The main ethical risk is overinterpretation. The personas are not voters, their elicited placement is the model’s reading of a value profile, the left, centre and right groups make no claim about real electorates, and the resemblance to the CIS concerns the order of the leaders only. Since this preprint appears before a general election, we stress that nothing in it forecasts votes, evaluates the leaders or measures public support for them. The attribution penalties describe the behaviour of one model and say nothing about what the public thinks.

The sixth issue, education, had a mean salience of 7.1%.

Posts were assigned to the five issues with a Spanish keyword registry, matched after removing URLs and accents, with a few phrases removed first because they contain a topic word with another meaning (“cordón sanitario”, “inquilino de la Moncloa”, homes evacuated in fires and floods). In the spot check of 80 matched posts (20 per retained topic, one annotator), the matched term was used in the topic’s sense in 19, 18, 20 and 20 of 20 posts on the economy, household costs, immigration and corruption, and the post was mainly about the topic in 17, 16, 19 and 17. The three word-sense errors were fixed in the registry; the remaining errors are passing mentions that keywords cannot separate (“empleo” or “vivienda” in a list of laws, “corrupción” in posts about the floods or the Gaza war).

A.4 
Knowledge audit

Before the experiment, the model was queried 20 times about each of the three leaders and each of three control names: two invented but plausible Spanish names and one opaque code (Table 
10
; 120 requests, using the same decoding settings as the experiment). For each name, the 20 requests comprised five semantically equivalent prompt variants in each of four independent probe families: recognition, party and political role, left–right ideology, and issue positions. The ideology probes used a 1–10 scale, where 1 denotes the political left and 10 the political right. The issue-position probes used four further 1–10 scales: state–market orientation (1 = greater government intervention; 10 = greater market orientation and tax reduction), housing policy (1 = greater regulation and tenant protection; 10 = greater emphasis on housing supply, market mechanisms and home ownership), immigration restrictiveness (1 = a more open or humanitarian position; 10 = a more restrictive position emphasising border control), and institutional trust (1 = anti-establishment discourse or low institutional trust; 10 = institutional continuity or high institutional trust). The model also differentiated the three leaders on several of these dimensions; for instance, their median immigration-restrictiveness scores were 3, 6 and 10. The ideological and policy-position scores describe the model’s internal representations and are not treated as ground truth about the politicians. The audit checks whether the names activate stable and distinct political identities; it does not estimate the effect of speaker attribution.

. B.2

 This is an English adaptation of the CIS barometer item.

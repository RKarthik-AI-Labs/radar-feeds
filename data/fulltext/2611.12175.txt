Abstract

Robotic manipulation has advanced rapidly in mapping visual observations and language instructions to actions. However, reliable manipulation in open-world environments requires robust 
spatial understanding
: the ability to identify targets in complex layouts (
spatial reasoning
) and adapt to scenes that continuously evolve through interaction (
spatial memory
). Yet existing embodied benchmarks fall short in evaluating spatial understanding: they often introduce strong visual cues that bypass spatial reasoning, and focus on static scenes that offer little insight into an agent’s capacity for spatial memory. To address this gap, we introduce 
GeoMind
, a systematic benchmark for structured spatial understanding through tabletop manipulation. 
GeoMind
 comprises 54 evaluated task variants organized around spatial reasoning and spatial memory. It also incorporates a scalable pipeline that automates the generation and annotation of task instances with verified targets and executable interactions. Extensive evaluations across diverse embodied agents reveal their consistent weaknesses in spatial understanding. Our findings highlight that these agents struggle to track identities after spatial changes and convert relational or geometric constraints into executable actions. We will release the 
GeoMind
 benchmark at 
https://github.com/Notfound-JH/GEOMIND-benchmark.git
.

However, overall task success provides limited insight into the spatial capabilities underlying an agent’s behavior. Similarly, tasks involving scene changes may be solved from the current observation without tracking previous spatial states. Conversely, manipulation failures may arise from execution errors even when the intended spatial target has been correctly identified. These limitations motivate a diagnostic evaluation that makes spatial requirements explicit and examines agents’ spatial capabilities beyond overall task success.

To enable a more targeted assessment of spatial understanding, we focus on two key dimensions,

 and

These dimensions specify the spatial capabilities that each task is designed to assess.

We introduce

, a benchmark for systematically evaluating spatial understanding through tabletop robotic manipulation. It comprises six task families: (1) 
target identification
, (2) 
relational grounding
, (3) 
constraint composition
, and (4) 
geometric inference
 for spatial reasoning, and (5) 
scene updating
 and (6) 
identity tracking
 for spatial memory. Together, these families span 54 evaluated task variants: 20 Base, 7 Semantic, 7 Visual, and 20 Execution variants. Tasks are designed around structured object layouts and controlled scene changes, with explicit spatial rules for determining target objects or goal locations. Many base tasks feature visually identical or near-identical objects to reduce reliance on appearance cues. To enable reliable evaluation of these diverse spatial objectives, we develop an agent-assisted pipeline that translates spatial constraints into executable grounding code, computes ground-truth spatial targets, and verifies the resulting task instances through simulation.

We evaluate generalist and spatially enhanced visuomotor policies alongside modular planning methods on 
GeoMind

GeoMind
 combines a base task suite (
GeoMind-Base
) with controlled semantic, visual, and execution augmentations (
GeoMind-Aug

Our results show that current methods can solve some basic grounding tasks but struggle to reason about structured spatial relationships and track spatial states across scene changes. Performance is sensitive to semantic augmentations, while visual and execution perturbations have more model-dependent effects.

The main contributions of this work are threefold: 
(1) A systematic benchmark for structured spatial understanding.
 We introduce 
GeoMind
, which organizes spatial understanding into six task families and provides controlled base and augmented evaluations. 
(2) An agent-assisted spatial grounding and verification pipeline.
 We develop a pipeline that translates spatial constraints into executable grounding programs, derives ground-truth targets, and verifies task instances through simulation. 
(3) A comprehensive diagnostic evaluation.
 We evaluate generalist and spatially enhanced policies alongside modular planning methods using diagnostic spatial metrics and oracle comparisons, revealing limitations in spatial reasoning, memory, and robustness.

3.1 
Taxonomy of Embodied Spatial Understanding Tasks

Rather than categorizing tasks solely by manipulation actions, we organize them according to the spatial capabilities required to determine task-relevant targets and goals. Following the dimensions introduced above, the taxonomy organizes structured spatial understanding around 
spatial reasoning
 and 
spatial memory
.
The former concerns how agents infer targets or goals from spatial relations and scene structure, while the latter concerns how agents maintain and update task-relevant spatial information as scenes change.

•

Target Identification
 focuses on static object selection from structured layouts.
The agent resolves a spatial description to the correct target object in a fixed scene.

•

Relational Grounding
 requires the agent to realize an explicit spatial relation through action, such as placing one object beside, in front of, or inside another reference object.

•

Constraint Composition
 tests whether the agent can jointly satisfy multiple spatial constraints that define a target object or a valid goal region.

•

Geometric Inference
 requires the agent to infer implicit target locations or regions from scene geometry, such as alignment, symmetry, or pattern continuation across objects.

•

Scene Updating
 evaluates whether the agent can update task-relevant spatial relations and re-ground its target after objects have moved or been manipulated.

•

Identity Tracking
 requires the agent to maintain object identity over time by linking a previously identified object to its current state across scene changes, such as object rearrangement or position exchanges.

Fig. 
1
 qualitatively illustrates the spatial reasoning and memory demands of the six task families and presents representative examples with initial scenes, robot trajectories, and ground-truth spatial targets.
The high/low positions in Fig. 
1
 indicate intended capability demands.
These tasks remain close to common robotic behaviors, such as selecting, placing, and retrieving objects, while exposing the spatial reasoning and memory demands behind seemingly simple actions.

3.2 
Spatial Structure Modeling and Benchmark Construction

Figure 2
: 
Visualization of the six fundamental structural layouts and their derived variants.

GeoMind
 constructs benchmark episodes through spatial structure modeling and an agent-assisted, rule-verified pipeline. Spatial structures allow object configurations to vary while preserving task-relevant constraints. The pipeline combines task template generation, executable spatial grounding, and simulation-based verification to produce structured manipulation tasks with deterministic ground-truth targets (Fig. 
3
).

Spatial Structure Modeling.

To evaluate spatial understanding across different scene configurations, 
GeoMind
 represents object layouts using spatial structures that are not tied to a fixed set of coordinates. For example, objects arranged in a line can preserve their left-to-right order while varying in position and spacing.

A task such as selecting the second object from the left therefore follows the same spatial rule across different layouts. We formalize a spatial structure 
 as a set of relational, topological, and geometric constraints over objects. Given 
, 
GeoMind
 generates diverse scene configurations by sampling object poses

where 
 controls allowable geometric variations, such as positional jitter and variable spacing, without violating the constraints encoded in 
. The structure library 
 includes linear, grid, and other canonical layouts (Fig. 
2
). Sampled configurations must satisfy structural validity and collision-free workspace constraints. This representation supports controlled evaluation across variations in object positions and spacing. Formal definitions are provided in 
Appendix C
.

LLM-Assisted Task and Structure Proposal.

Given a task category and a candidate set of spatial structure types, the LLM proposes matching instructions and structure templates within a predefined schema. Each template specifies the structure type, object roles, layout constraints, sampling ranges, and optional scene-update rules. Candidate templates are screened to remove ambiguous, logically conflicting, or task-incompatible specifications, with human review during template selection. The retained templates form a fixed library for subsequent programmatic scene instantiation.
The benchmark uses a fixed set of tasks selected through human review, with task instances generated programmatically from the corresponding templates.

Spatial Target Grounding.

An LLM-based coding agent translates spatial rules into programs that deterministically resolve target objects or goal regions from simulator states. Directional predicates use the canonical front-camera frame, with object coordinates transformed from the robot base frame. Reasoning tasks ground targets in the current configuration. In memory tasks, instructions precede scripted object motion, and evaluated actions begin after it ends: Scene Updating re-grounds targets in the updated scene, while Identity Tracking preserves their initial identities. Each agent can store earlier observations or maintain an internal memory to identify the target after the objects move.

Closed-Loop Verification.

Each candidate episode is executed in a physics simulator to check physical feasibility and target consistency. For tasks involving scene changes, objects move through scripted motion or simulated robot manipulation. Throughout execution, we record simulator object states and construct a dynamic interaction graph 
, where nodes represent objects and edges encode discretized pairwise directions and distances. The graph sequence is used to check whether scene transitions and resulting spatial states are consistent with the task specifications and ground-truth targets. Episodes failing state-validity or target-consistency checks are discarded.

Overview of the benchmark construction pipeline.
 The pipeline first proposes task descriptions and structure templates, then resolves spatial queries over instantiated scenes to derive ground-truth annotations, and finally validates candidate episodes through interaction-graph-based simulation verification.

3.3 
Task Suites and Dataset

GeoMind
 is organized into two complementary evaluation suites: 
GeoMind-Base
 and 
GeoMind-Aug
.
Together, they provide 
54 evaluated task variants
 for structured spatial understanding.
A task variant is a specific test setting that pairs a spatial objective with a scene structure, governed by specific grounding rules and evaluation conditions.

GeoMind-Base
 evaluates the six spatial task families under canonical tabletop settings, while 
GeoMind-Aug
 introduces controlled perturbations to assess robustness under additional semantic, visual, and execution challenges.

GeoMind-Base
 consists of 
20 evaluated base variants
 with diverse language instructions and multiple spatial structure types.
To reduce visual shortcuts, many 
GeoMind-Base
 variants contain multiple visually identical or near-identical objects.
In these settings, target selection or placement depends on spatial cues such as relative position, order, geometric relations, or scene updates, rather than object appearance alone.

GeoMind-Base
 serves as the core benchmark suite and evaluates the spatial reasoning and memory capabilities defined in our taxonomy.
Fig. 
4(b)
 presents the distribution of evaluated task variants across task families and augmentation types.

GeoMind-Aug
 contains 
34 augmented variants
: 7 Semantic, 7 Visual, and 20 Execution variants. Semantic augmentation modifies language grounding, visual augmentation modifies visual conditions, and execution augmentation tightens action precision requirements. Together with the 20 Base variants, these form 54 evaluated variants. These counts refer to evaluated task variants rather than unique spatial objectives or evaluation episodes. Examples and task details appear in Fig. 
4(a)
 and 
Appendix D
.

Evaluation Protocol.
 All experiments are conducted in the IsaacLab 
[
59
]
 simulator using a Franka Research 3 (FR3) robotic arm. All methods are evaluated on the same task instances, random seeds, episode horizons, and success criteria. The evaluated VLA policies use fixed checkpoints without 
GeoMind
-specific fine-tuning, and their outputs are adapted to a common action interface. Privileged scene information, including simulator-resolved object identities and ground-truth target coordinates, is used for evaluation and the oracle baseline but is not provided to the evaluated VLA policies or modular agents during inference. Each episode is limited to 1000 control steps and terminates early upon task success or workspace violation. We evaluate 
 episodes per task variant, with evaluation seeds saved for reproducibility. The physics simulation runs at 480 Hz, and the policy control frequency is 24 Hz. Detailed observation and action interfaces, deployment configurations, and evaluation protocols are provided in 
Appendix B
.

4.2 
Main Results

We organize the results around five questions concerning overall performance, robustness, failure modes, spatial memory, and spatial reasoning.

Q1. How well do embodied agents perform in structured spatial understanding?

We begin with 
GeoMind-Base
, which evaluates six spatial capabilities under standard task conditions without the additional perturbations introduced in 
GeoMind-Aug
. Table 
2
 reports the success rates of different agents across the six task families. The best-performing learned policy, InternVLA-M1, achieves 26.4% success, followed by Spatial-Forcing at 24.0%, substantially below the oracle’s 99.3%. Spatially enhanced policies achieve the highest overall scores, although their improvements over general policies are uneven across models and task families. Modular agents achieve 4.0–16.9% success, indicating that explicit VLM-based grounding and scripted execution also struggle with structured spatial tasks.

To better understand these performance gaps, we examine the contributions of target grounding and execution. The oracle achieves near-perfect success using ground-truth targets and scripted control, demonstrating the feasibility of the benchmark tasks under correct spatial grounding. More directly, in a paired diagnostic evaluation of 
, explicitly specifying the target through its name and a visual cue improves success from 11.3% to 39.2%, while keeping the policy, task instances, action interface, and success criterion fixed. This result provides evidence that target identification contributes substantially to the observed failures, although execution and deployment limitations may also play a role.

Finding
: Current embodied agents remain far from reliable on structured spatial manipulation tasks. The controlled target-cue intervention suggests that inaccurate spatial target grounding is an important contributor to these failures, beyond execution limitations alone.

Q2. How robust is structured spatial understanding under semantic, visual, and execution perturbations?

Table 
3
 shows that success in 
GeoMind-Base
 is fragile under augmented variants of the same spatial goals.
The clearest drop comes from semantic augmentation: averaged over the six embodied policies, success decreases from about 22.6% on the corresponding Base tasks to about 14.2%, and every policy degrades.
This indicates that many Base successes still rely on simple instruction grounding rather than robust spatial-language understanding.
The effects of visual and execution augmentation are more uneven.
OpenVLA falls to 12.5% under both settings, whereas 
 remains unchanged under visual perturbation and decreases by 6.5 percentage points under execution. These results suggest that structured spatial understanding degrades under complex instruction semantics and increased visual noise.

Finding
: Spatial success in embodied agents is condition-dependent rather than goal-invariant: the same spatial objective often fails once its linguistic, visual, or execution support is perturbed.

Q3. Why do embodied agents fail on 
GeoMind
?

We examine representative failure cases in Fig. 
5
 to connect the quantitative trends with concrete trajectories.
For embodied policies in Fig. 
5(a)
, the illustrated failures fall into three categories: 
spatial relation grounding errors
, where policies bind the correct anchor but execute the wrong relation; 
spatial relation reasoning errors
, where policies fail to infer structure-defined targets; and 
scene-updating errors
, where policies fail to ground the requested target in the updated configuration. These examples suggest that some failures involve converting spatial observations into appropriate action targets, although the trajectories alone do not isolate grounding errors from control errors.
For modular embodied agents in Fig. 
5(b)
, the illustrated failures include object localization errors that can propagate to planning and control. This suggests a different bottleneck: modular agents may expose explicit reasoning steps, but remain vulnerable when intermediate spatial grounding is inaccurate.

Finding
: The illustrated failures suggest difficulties in translating spatial observations into correct action targets. These examples identify possible failure mechanisms, but do not establish their prevalence across the benchmark.

Scene Updating requires selecting a target from the updated configuration, whereas Identity Tracking requires linking an object identified before the change to its new position. An agent may therefore fail an identity-tracking task by selecting an object that matches the current spatial relation but is not the original target. This provides a possible explanation for failures beyond imprecise execution. However, aggregate SGE and TTE cannot distinguish these causes: both are affected by scene geometry, initial target distance, and rollout duration. We use them as auxiliary diagnostics rather than direct measures of identity preservation or evidence that one task family is intrinsically harder.

Finding
: Identity Tracking requires preserving object correspondence across scene changes; the current aggregate results do not establish how much failure is attributable to incorrect target selection versus execution.

Q5. What makes spatial reasoning difficult for embodied agents?

Table 
4
 reports the highest failure rate for relational grounding among the reasoning families in that diagnostic summary, at 98.33%, compared with 72.08% for target identification. Constraint composition and geometric inference are also difficult, with failure rates of 87.50% and 86.67%, respectively.
This indicates that current agents do not only struggle with recognizing spatial targets; they also fail to convert explicit spatial relations into correct embodied actions.
Table 
2
 shows that geometric inference has the lowest best-agent success rate, with the strongest method reaching only 27.5%, suggesting that implicit structure-based reasoning remains hard even for the best agents.
Together, these results suggest that agents remain weak when they must jointly satisfy multiple spatial constraints or infer targets from implicit geometric structure.

Finding
: Spatial reasoning is difficult not only because agents struggle to identify the correct spatial target, but because they fail to transform spatial constraints into executable manipulation behavior.

5 
Conclusion

We present 
GeoMind
, a systematic benchmark for evaluating spatial understanding in embodied agents. By organizing tasks around spatial memory and spatial reasoning, 
GeoMind
 enables a more fine-grained analysis than overall task success alone. We further emphasize how spatial structure underpins spatial understanding, and present an automated generation pipeline that allows scalable benchmark construction.
Our experiments show that current embodied agents remain far from achieving consistently high success rates on 
GeoMind
, with large performance gaps and highly uneven abilities across task families. These findings suggest that spatial understanding remains a core limitation of embodied agents.

Limitations.

GeoMind
 is limited to simulated, single-arm tabletop manipulation, without real-robot validation. Its controlled layouts do not capture the full diversity of real-world environments. Heavy occlusion, viewpoint changes, sensor noise, and complex contact dynamics are not systematically evaluated, although incidental occlusion may occur during execution. Semantic and visual augmentations cover selected transformations rather than exhaustive robustness conditions. Our experiments evaluate frozen pretrained policies, and their performance may also depend on observation and action-interface compatibility. We do not report benchmark-specific fine-tuning results; learning spatial skills and generalizing to held-out structures or variants remain untested. The results therefore characterize frozen-policy performance in the evaluated setting and do not establish transfer to real robots.

B.5 
Modular Baselines and Diagnostic Use

We additionally evaluate modular agents based on COPA 
[
57
]
 and MOKA 
[
58
]
.
These methods represent a different embodied-agent paradigm from end-to-end VLA policies: instead of directly predicting low-level actions from visual observations and language instructions, they decompose manipulation into explicit visual-language grounding, target selection, and scripted execution.
We include them as modular baselines to compare different design paradigms for structured spatial manipulation, and also use their intermediate predictions to diagnose where failures arise.

Each modular agent receives the task instruction and RGB observations, and uses a VLM backbone to identify the task-relevant object or spatial target from the scene.
We instantiate the modular agents with three VLM backbones: GPT-4o, Gemini-2.5-Flash, and Claude-Sonnet-4.6.
The predicted target is then converted into Cartesian waypoints or a target region and executed with the same Franka differential-IK controller used in the VLA evaluation.
For pick-and-place tasks, the scripted routine follows a top-down sequence of approach, grasp, lift, place, release, and retreat.
For reaching tasks, the controller moves the end effector toward the predicted target region and evaluates success using the same task evaluator as other methods.

All modular agents are evaluated on the same task instances, random seeds, workspace constraints, episode horizon, and success criteria as the end-to-end policies.
They do not receive privileged simulator information during inference: oracle target coordinates, simulator-resolved object identities, ground-truth task variables, segmentation masks, and task metadata are not exposed to the VLM or the planner.
These privileged states are used only by the evaluator for success checking and diagnostic logging.

Because modular agents use a different inference and execution structure from VLA policies, their results should be interpreted as a comparison across embodied-agent paradigms rather than as a fully controlled comparison of identical policy interfaces.
This distinction is important: end-to-end policies test whether a learned visuomotor model can directly map observations and language to actions, whereas modular agents test whether explicit VLM-based grounding and scripted execution can solve the same spatial objectives.
Their low success rates therefore provide complementary evidence that structured spatial understanding is difficult not only for end-to-end action generation, but also for explicit target grounding and target-to-action conversion pipelines.

B.6 
Fairness and Information Access

The main comparison in GEOMIND covers multiple embodied-agent paradigms under the same task instances, random seeds, workspace constraints, episode horizon, and success criteria.
For end-to-end VLA policies, all models receive the same observation modalities and are evaluated as frozen policies without 
GeoMind
-specific fine-tuning.
For modular baselines, the VLM grounding stage receives the same task instruction and RGB observations, while execution is performed by a scripted controller after target selection.

No evaluated method receives privileged simulator information during inference.
Object poses, simulator-resolved object identities, oracle target coordinates, ground-truth task variables, segmentation masks, and task metadata are excluded from both VLA policies and modular baselines.
These states are used only for evaluation, success checking, and diagnostic logging.

Since VLA policies and modular baselines differ in inference structure, their results should be read as a cross-paradigm comparison rather than a comparison of identical policy interfaces.
This is intentional: GEOMIND aims to evaluate whether different embodied-agent designs can solve the same structured spatial objectives under the same task conditions.
The modular results complement the VLA results by revealing whether explicit visual grounding and scripted execution can overcome the spatial failures observed in end-to-end policies.

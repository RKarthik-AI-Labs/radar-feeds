Abstract
NormAd-ETI scores a model’s judgment of ev-
eryday actions as acceptable, unacceptable, or
neither as the cultural context shrinks from a
specific rule to a country name. We audit what
that three-way score measures. It combines
two decisions—whether a supplied norm ap-
plies, and whether the action conforms to it.
Separating them shows that when only a coun-
try is shown, applicability falls to near chance
while conformity stays well above it, because
the prompt withholds the rule a Neutral label
depends on. Because one answer carries both
decisions, the score follows the steadier confor-
mity and hides the weaker applicability, and no
single prompt recovers the two separately. Ded-
icated forced-choice probes separate them for
frontier models but not for small open models,
where the applicability judgment still tracks
the hidden acceptability label; adding a written
rationale reduces that conflation without remov-
ing it. We recommend reporting the two results
separately and measuring the conflation gap.
1 Introduction
NormAd was introduced to test whether models
can adapt judgments of social acceptability as the
available cultural context changes, from a specific
Rule-of-Thumb (RoT), to an abstract value, to a
country name (Rao et al., 2025). NormAd-ETI op-
erationalizes this goal with everyday stories labeled
Yes, No, or Neutral.
The benchmark’s headline result is easy to state
and easy to misread. Accuracy falls as context is
removed, and it falls fastest for the Neutral class.
We ask what that fall means. The Neutral label is
constructed by pairing a story with a norm from
outside the story’s cultural cluster, so its intended

meaning is that the supplied normdoes not apply.
The prompt, however, asks only whether the action
is acceptable and offers “Neither” without saying
what it means. A single answer therefore stands
in for two decisions:applicability(does the norm
bear on this action?) andconformity(if it does,
does the action follow it?).
We make two claims on the same items and mod-
els. The first is about theCountrycondition: a
Neutral item is built from a rule that does not apply
to the story, and at the Country level that rule is
never shown, so the construct depends on an in-
put the prompt withholds. Our experiments show
the shortfall lands on applicability, which drops
far more than conformity. The second is about
bundling and separation: one answer carries both
decisions, so the score follows the stronger con-
formity and hides the weaker applicability, while
scoring each decision with its own probe separates
them at the frontier level but not in the five small
open models. We read this conflation as consistent
with a representational limit in the small models
rather than a flaw in the probes.

• A context gap at Country applicability: the
Country deficit is dominated by a loss of appli-

• Conflation in the separate probes at small

2 Related Work
Cultural-adaptability benchmarks.NormAd
(Rao et al., 2025) belongs to a family of bench-
marks that test cultural competence in language
models. CulturalBench (Chiu et al., 2025) probes
cultural knowledge with multiple-choice questions;
GlobalOpinionQA (DURMUS et al., 2024) com-
pares model responses to cross-national survey
opinion, and other work groups countries into
value clusters through the World Values Survey and
the Inglehart–Welzel map (Inglehart and Welzel,
2023); Naous et al. (2024) probe culturally inappro-
priate associations. Each of these reduces cultural
judgment to a single score or label. Our audit asks
what the score of one such benchmark actually mea-
sures, rather than proposing another benchmark.
Benchmark validity and evaluation reliability.
Construct validity asks whether a measurement pro-
cedure tracks the construct its label names (Cron-
bach and Meehl, 1955), and for machine-learning
systems this is a property of the measurement
model encoded in the data and the prompt (Jacobs
and Wallach, 2021). Recent work questions the
reliability of cultural-alignment evaluation: Khan
et al. (2025) show that decoding and small prompt
changes can outweigh any real cross-cultural sig-
nal, and Sorensen et al. (2024) argue that evaluation
should represent more than one value system. Our
concern is complementary and orthogonal to decod-
ing noise: with decoding held fixed, one condition
(Country) asks a question the prompt leaves un-
derdetermined. NormAd is also reused in larger
multi-task studies of cultural alignment—Agarwal
et al. (2026) compare Indian-focused and general-
purpose models across several tasks including Nor-
mAd.
Social norms and acceptability.Datasets such
as Social Chemistry 101 (Forbes et al., 2020),
Social IQa (Sap et al., 2019), and ETHICS
(Hendrycks et al., 2021) annotate social or moral
judgments over everyday situations. NormAd’s
Neutral label instead turns on relevance rather than
normative ambiguity. Separating relevance from
conformity is a measurement distinction that other
social-judgment datasets may also blur, but we test
it here only on NormAd.
Decomposing judgments and eliciting rationales.
Chain-of-thought prompting (Wei et al., 2022) and
self-consistency (Wang et al., 2023) elicit interme-
diate reasoning, but such explanations need not be
faithful: Turpin et al. (2023) show models rational-
ize biased answers, while models can also report
calibrated uncertainty in words (Kadavath et al.,
2022; Lin et al., 2022). Our two forced-choice
probes are not a reasoning method but a measure-
ment decomposition; and our paired rationale runs
ask whether writing a rationale before the answer
changes the labels, a question distinct from whether
the rationale is faithful.
Metrics for two-class evaluation.Isolating one
decision makes class imbalance and degenerate
response margins central. We use Youden’s J
(Youden, 1950) rather than the Matthews correla-
tion coefficient (Matthews, 1975; Gorodkin, 2004;
Chicco and Jurman, 2020), because J does not
renormalize by the predicted-positive marginal and
so cannot be inflated by a skewed prediction rate;
paired comparisons use the exact McNemar test
(McNemar, 1947) with Benjamini–Hochberg cor-
rection (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995).
3 NormAd-ETI as a Measurement
Instrument
Dataset.NormAd-ETI contains 2,615 everyday-
etiquette stories from 75 countries, each labeled
Yes, No, or Neither: 943 Yes, 875 No, and 797
Neutral items, so 1,818 (69.5%) are applicable and
797 (30.5%) are Neutral. Every story is presented
at three levels of context: a Rule-of-Thumb (RoT),
a country plus an abstract value (CV AL), or a coun-
try alone (Country). A Neutral item is one whose
paired rule comes from outside the story’s own cul-
tural cluster (Inglehart and Welzel, 2023), so the
label is meant to say that the rule does not apply.
The model answers a single question with the three
labels, and “Neither” does not say whether it means
“does not apply” or “is ambiguous.”
Why the distinction matters.Construct valid-
ity asks whether a score supports the interpretation
attached to it (Cronbach and Meehl, 1955); for com-
putational systems the measurement model should
be explicit and tested (Jacobs and Wallach, 2021).
2
The ternary score combines two decisions: appli-
cability (does the norm bear on the action?) and
conformity (does the action follow it?). A Neither
given because the norm could not be found does
not show that an ambiguous case was resolved, and
a NotApply given because the action seems wrong
contaminates applicability with conformity. We
therefore score the two decisions separately and
report the conflation gap alongside.
4 Experimental Design
4.1 Data and models
We use the corrected normad_v2 release ( N=
2,615), distributed by the benchmark’s own au-
thors in a later update to the dataset and paper.
It regenerates 219 of the 797 gold-Neutral stories,
and we use it as published without altering any
item (Appendix B.1). For its 797 gold-Neutral
items, normad_v1 and normad_v2 disagree on the
Country field in every case (they agree on every
Yes/No item): v1 holds the story’s source country,
v2 the incidental country used to build the Neutral
story, which is what the model is shown. So the
Country prompt never names the country a Neu-
tral story was written for. Cluster-level analyses
use the source country to stay comparable to the
benchmark’s tables.
We evaluate seven models under greedy decod-
ing, one variant per model: five open-weight and
two frontier models, all listed with their reports in
Table 1. Two of the families were also run with a
written reasoning field, giving a paired contrast on
the same weights—gpt-5.6-luna (frontier) and
Gemma-4B (open-weight); the main analysis uses
the no-reasoning variant of each and the paired
contrasts are analysed separately (Section 5.5).
gemini-3.8-flash has hidden reasoning that can-
not be turned off and also returns a post-hoc expla-
nation, so its one variant is used as it is. The two
frontier models are markedly stronger, so pooled
means average two deliberately different groups;
we report the two groups separately wherever the
distinction matters (Table 7, Figure 4).
4.2 Experimental design
Table 2 defines the seven instruments; each is run
at all three context levels, so the design has 21
fully specified prompts. The original instrument
is e1. Three variants add a definition of Neither:
correcteddef defines it in one clause, full de-
fines all three options, and vague gives a content-
free clause that permits answering C without defin-
ing anything, which separates the effect of a def-
inition from the effect of permission. The four-
cell joint4 makes relevance an explicit part of
every option. Two dedicated probes score one deci-
sion each: apply3strip_verbose for applicabil-
ity, which states its options as full sentences and
removes the story’s own trailing acceptability ques-
tion so that the probe does not contradict its own
instruction to judge relevance, and binaryonly for
conformity, a forced Yes/No choice scored on gold-
applicable items only (“gold-routed”).
We use these instruments in five steps: (1) repli-
cate the benchmark with e1; (2) test whether defin-
ing Neither helps ( correcteddef, full, vague);
(3) score applicability and conformity with the two
probes and compare them; (4) test whether the
applicability probe is independent of acceptabil-
ity by comparing its NotApply rate on gold-Yes
and gold-No items, which are equally applicable
by construction; and (5) ask whether any single-
prompt variant—joint4 or a ternary rewording—
can replace the two probes. As secondary de-
scriptive material, carrying no headline claim, the
appendix also breaks accuracy down by culture
cluster and subaxis and gives the Latin-America
gift case, alongside worked example items (Appen-
dices A, B, and C).
4.3 Metrics and statistics
For ternary answers we report accuracy and class-
conditional recall. For the two probes we use
Youden’s J (sensitivity + specificity −1; zero is
chance, negative values reported as they are), with
NotApply as the positive class for applicability and
Yes versus No on gold-applicable items for con-
formity. We report the two capabilities separately
and never as a composite, because they come from
different instruments and a composite would hide
which axis failed.Conflationis the signed gap, in
percentage points, between the NotApply rate on
gold-No items and that on gold-Yes items—zero
when the applicability probe is independent of the
acceptability label—tested with an exact test and
Benjamini–Hochberg correction within each instru-
ment’s model-by-level cells. This is distinct from
the ternary answer’s bundling of the two decisions,
where the stronger conformity masks the weaker
applicability (Section 5.2). Intervals use 20,000
bootstrap resamples with seeds from a stable check-
sum; cluster-level accuracy uses label-balanced,
country-clustered resampling.

5.1 The Country deficit is dominated by
applicability
The original instrument reproduces the reported pat-
tern for every model: Country accuracy and Neutral
recall are far below RoT (accuracy in Table 3, Neu-
tral recall in Figure 1). Mean accuracy falls steadily
(0.784, 0.612, 0.546), and the decline is dominated
by the Neutral class: mean Neutral recall collapses
from 0.470 to 0.141 to 0.048, whereas mean Yes
recall stays high (0.92 to 0.86) and mean No recall
falls only to 0.66. Qwen3.5-9B and Gemini-3.8
recover no gold-Neutral item at Country.
Two facts make this an applicability problem
rather than a general difficulty. First, the label: the
country shown for Neutral items is incidental (Sec-
tion 4), so it carries no information about the label.
Second, the decision: the loss is in the Neutral class
while Yes/No calls stay confident, so a low Coun-
try accuracy is mostly adoes not applyjudgment
read as an acceptability verdict. Appendix C works
through a gold-Neutral item, giving the rule, the
incidental country, and the models’ answers at each
level.
The dedicated probes confirm that this is specif-
ically applicability. Figure 2 and Table 4 put the
two capabilities on the same items and models. Ap-

5.2 The ternary answer bundles the two
decisions
The Country result is visible only because the two
decisions are scored separately. In the benchmark’s
single answer they are not: because conformity is
the stronger signal, the score follows it and hides
the weaker applicability, which we callbundling.
Two pieces of evidence show it. First, the aggregate
is carried by conformity: the Yes/No rows stay
strong at every level (mean J 0.83 and 0.74 at RoT,
still 0.46 and 0.43 at Country) while the Neutral
row is already weak at RoT ( +0.42) and zero at
Country (−0.01); a model that answers “Yes” or
“No” on an item whose gold label is “does not apply”
is not making a conformity error, it never took the
applicability step. Second, the two capabilities
are measured with different instruments and are
demonstrably dependent in the small open models
(Section 5.4); a single number therefore cannot be
read as either one.
5.3 Defining Neither helps where the rule is
available
Defining Neither raises mean Neutral recall by
0.333 at RoT and 0.358 at CV AL, and the frontier
models reach about 0.87 at RoT once it is defined
(Table 5, Figure 3). The gain is larger at RoT than
at Country for six of seven models. At Country
it is only 0.119, which is what underdetermina-
tion predicts: there is no rule in the prompt for
the definition to attach to. A content-free control
that defines nothing still reaches 0.66 Neutral re-
call at RoT, so part of the RoT and CV AL gain is
permission to answer Neither rather than a sharper
definition, and at Country the full definition even
yields less Neutral recall than the control for four of
seven models. The improvement is not free: mean
Yes/No accuracy falls by 0.130, 0.134, and 0.061.
A different format fares no better as a single
replacement. Across e1, correcteddef, vague,
full, and the four-cell joint4, the mean Country
Neutral J stays between −0.01 and +0.10 (Ta-
ble 6). The four-cell prompt, which makes rele-
vance explicit in every option, raises Country Neu-
tral recall to 0.69—but only by also sending 59%
of gold Yes/No items to Neutral, with Yes/No accu-
racy falling to 0.36. A Neutral-recall gain bought
by routing most applicable items to Neutral is not
better applicability, and it is why we use no ternary
variant as the measurement of record.

5.5 The written-rationale effect in one
frontier and one open-weight model
We ask two models to write a short rationale be-
fore the answer, without enabling the provider’s
hidden chain-of-thought, and compare each against
the same model answering directly: one frontier
(gpt-5.6-luna) and one open-weight (Gemma-
4B). Each pair uses the same weights, and neither
pair enters a number above. Full tables are in Ap-
pendix D.
For gpt-5.6-luna we only ask for a reasoning
field before the answer, and the apply probe stays
clean: its conflation gap remains near zero, at most
+2.2 pp, against +6 to +60 pp for the small open
models.
For Gemma-4B we use the same weights but
ask for a reasoning field as well (the two runs dif-
fer slightly in serving stack: a single-token GBNF
completion from a BF16 GGUF versus transform-
ers with outlines JSON). The one change that
matters here is that its RoT conflation gap drops
sharply, though it does not vanish: from +56.0 to
+15.2pp, a change of−40.8pp.
So a written rationale roughly quarters the small
model’s conflation without removing it. Other num-
bers change only modestly and are not load-bearing
for the audit, so they are relegated to Appendix D.
6 Discussion
The Country deficit is dominated by applicabil-

7 Conclusion
NormAd-ETI’s Neutral result is not one phe-
nomenon. At Country the prompt withholds the
rule that defines a Neutral label, and applicabil-
ity is what falls furthest: measured with separate
probes it reaches chance while conformity stays
well above it. The single ternary answer bundles
the two decisions, so the score is carried by confor-
mity; scoring each on its own separates them at the
frontier level, while the small open models retain
conflation consistent with a representational limit
in the small models. We recommend treating the
ternary accuracy as a legacy diagnostic and report-
ing the two results separately with the conflation
gap. More generally, cultural benchmarks should
validate not only whether labels are reproducible,
but also whether the label names, construction pro-
cedure, prompts, and reported interpretations refer
to the same construct.
Limitations
Scope.This is an analysis of a single benchmark,
using our own model runs across the instruments
of Section 4. Its main limitation is generality: the
findings are about NormAd-ETI and need not trans-
fer to other cultural-reasoning datasets, and we do
not reannotate the data with independent human
judgments.
No human baseline.The Country condition may
contain genuine cultural-knowledge demands in ad-
dition to the applicability problem, and the bench-
mark reports no human baseline for Country alone.
We therefore claim underdetermination by the
8
prompt, not a human gap.
Measurement, not mechanism.The applicabil-
ity/conformity decomposition is a measurement
design, not a claim about processes inside a model;
the reading of the probe’s conflation as a represen-
tational limit in the small models is suggestive, not
demonstrated.
Model set and the separation gap.The set
mixes five small open models with two strong
frontier models, so pooled means are driven by
between-group differences. The two frontier mod-
els differ from the open models in size and capa-
bility, and only gemini-3.8-flash reasons inter-
nally while gpt-5.6-luna does not; both frontier
models are nonetheless clean, so the absence of
reasoning alone does not create conflation, and we
cannot fully separate size, training, and reasoning
as causes.
Written rationale.The rationale runs are anal-
ysed separately and enter no main number. Two
paired contrasts use the same weights within each
pair—gpt-5.6-luna (frontier) and Gemma-4B
(open-weight). Luna’s two runs share the API stack
and differ only by the reasoning field; Gemma’s
no-reasoning run is a single-token GBNF comple-
tion from a BF16 GGUF while its reasoning run
uses transformers with outlines JSON decoding,
so that pair also varies in serving stack. There is
one run per condition, so the paired differences
include any nondeterminism at temperature 0, and
gemini-3.8-flash has no no-rationale counter-
part.
Cluster mapping.The Inglehart–Welzel map-
ping is used as published; it places Malta and the
Philippines in Latin America, and removing them
shifts the cluster’s balanced accuracy by 0.9 pp and
its gift false-accept rate by 2.2 pp on 16–22 items
(Appendix B).
Data-quality heuristics.The story-leak screen-
ing is a marker heuristic that flags 10 of the 219
Neutral stories the authors regenerated in the cor-
rected release (against 1 of the same 219 before
regeneration); removing them changes no cluster
mean by more than 0.27 pp (Appendix B).

F Reproducibility
We use the released normad_v2 data (2,615 items),
each presented at three levels of context (RoT,
CV AL, Country) and scored with the seven instru-
ments above; the exact prompts are given in Ap-
pendix E. Every run is greedy (temperature 0) and
holds one variant per model, so a given item is
always seen under the same prompt word for word.
The five open-weight models and the no-
rationale Gemma run are served locally as BF16
GGUF weights through a llama.cpp OpenAI-
compatible chat endpoint, with a GBNF grammar
that restricts the completion to exactly one of the
allowed answer letters (A/B/C, A/B, or A/B/C/D); the
recorded answer is that single letter, so no free-text
parsing is needed. The two frontier models are
called through a hosted API (the Kaggle Bench-
marks API) instead: the answer is requested as
JSON under a fixed schema, so every response is a
single option field. Hidden chain-of-thought was
disabled for the GPT-5.6 runs (confirmed in the
call traces); Gemini-3.8 always reasons internally
and never has it disabled. Prompts are identical
across models apart from the final response-format
instruction.

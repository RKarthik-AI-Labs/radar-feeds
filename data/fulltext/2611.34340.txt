Abstract

With the increasing adoption of privacy-preserving techniques in dynamic graph analysis, local differential privacy (LDP) has become a fundamental paradigm for protecting user data in decentralized environments. However, despite its strong privacy guarantees, LDP-based dynamic graph protocols remain vulnerable to data poisoning attacks through malicious data injection. In this paper, we present the first systematic study of data poisoning attacks against privacy-preserving dynamic graphs under the LDP framework. Considering the temporal evolution of dynamic graphs, we identify key challenges in attack design and evaluation, including how to schedule attack resources across time and how to measure cumulative attack impact. To address these challenges, we introduce an absolute gain metric to quantify the overall deviation of graph statistical metrics before and after attacks across time. Based on this metric, we further propose a unified poisoning attack framework for dynamic graphs, which consists of target node selection, dynamic fake node scale scheduling, and fake edge injection. Within this framework, we design a temporal enhanced attack (TEA) strategy that dynamically schedules attack resources to maximize attack effectiveness, along with two baseline strategies, equal-scale attack (ESA) and random-scale attack (RSA), for comparison. Extensive experiments on real-world and synthetic datasets demonstrate that the proposed attacks can significantly degrade the accuracy of graph metric estimation for target nodes.

 Local differential privacy; Poisoning attacks; Dynamic graphs

However, dynamic graphs often contain highly sensitive personal information, such as social relationships or purchase histories, raising significant privacy concerns and limiting their safe sharing.

Local differential privacy (LDP)

 has emerged as a promising paradigm for decentralized graph data protection.
Unlike traditional approaches (central differential privacy, CDP

This model shifts control of privacy to the user side and eliminates the reliance on a central trusted authority.
LDP has already been applied successfully in graph analysis such as degree distribution

, subgraph counting 
[

[
16
,

, enabling privacy-preserving data analysis and collection without compromising user confidentiality.

Despite its strong privacy guarantees, data poisoning attacks have recently emerged as a significant threat to LDP protocols.
Prior studies have shown that LDP aggregation mechanisms can be misled by manipulated user reports 
[
18
, 
19
, 
20
]
.
For instance, an adversary may inject a small number of malicious users or submit carefully crafted fake reports, thereby altering the distribution of the collected statistics and significantly biasing the final aggregated estimations.
Existing poisoning attacks against LDP protocols mainly focus on tabular data 
[
18
, 
19
]
 or static graphs 
[
21
]
, including tasks such as frequency estimation, heavy-hitter detection, and mean estimation.
However, these studies are largely limited to static settings, where the underlying data structure does not evolve over time.

In contrast, the vulnerability of dynamic graphs under LDP to poisoning attacks remains largely unexplored.
Compared with static graphs, dynamic graphs 
[
6
, 
22
, 
23
]
 consist of a sequence of evolving network snapshots, which introduces additional complexities for adversarial manipulation.
Specifically, two key challenges arise in dynamic graph settings:
(1) Attackers need to determine how to evaluate the effectiveness of poisoning attacks in dynamic graphs: the influence of attacks may accumulate across multiple snapshots over time, making it difficult to directly measure the overall attack gain.
(2) Attackers have to determine how to allocate malicious actions across multiple time steps to maximize the overall attack impact: overly concentrated attacks in a single snapshot may produce strong short-term effects but increase the risk of detection, whereas overly dispersed attacks may significantly weaken the overall effectiveness under limited attack resources.

In this paper, we explore the data poisoning attacks to local differential privacy protocols for dynamic graphs for the first time.
To solve the challenge (1), we design an absolute gain metric for dynamic graph attacks to quantify the changes in graph statistical metrics before and after the attack.
To handle the challenge (2), based on this metric, we design a temporal enhanced attack (TEA) strategy that schedules attack resources across time steps to maximize the overall attack gain in dynamic graphs.
In addition, we construct two baseline attack strategies, namely equal-scale attack (ESA) and random-scale Attack (RSA), for comparative evaluation.

Building upon these designs, we propose a unified poisoning attack framework for LDP-based dynamic graphs, as illustrated in Fig. 
1
. The framework consists of three key components: (i) target node selection, which randomly selects target nodes from the final graph snapshot according to a predefined target node ratio 
; (ii) dynamic fake node scheduling, which determines the total number of fake nodes and allocates them across different time slices using three scheduling strategies, namely ESA, RSA, and TEA; and (iii) fake edge injection, which constructs and injects forged edges through three topology attack strategies, namely RVA, RNA, and MGA, to manipulate the graph metrics of target nodes. Together, these components form a complete pipeline for effective adversarial manipulation under privacy constraints.

Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We conduct the first systematic investigation of data poisoning attacks against LDP protocols in dynamic graph settings, revealing previously unexplored security vulnerabilities.

•

We propose a general poisoning attack framework tailored to dynamic graphs under LDP, along with three attack strategies (TEA, ESA, and RSA) designed to effectively manipulate graph statistics over time.

•

Extensive experiments on both real-world and synthetic datasets demonstrate that our proposed attacks can significantly degrade the accuracy of graph metric estimation for target nodes, highlighting the urgent need for robust defense mechanisms in privacy-preserving dynamic graph systems.

The introduction of Local Differential Privacy (LDP) effectively addresses this issue by allowing users to add noise directly at the data generation stage, eliminating the dependence on a trusted third party at the mechanism level

LDP has been widely studied for protecting sensitive information in graph analysis

AsgLDP

II-B
 
Dynamic Graph Analysis under LDP

Under the LDP framework, recent studies have begun to explore decentralized graph data publishing in dynamic settings.
Such dynamic graphs are typically represented as a sequence of static graph snapshots over time. By continuously applying local perturbations across consecutive time slices, these methods achieve privacy protection without relying on a trusted central server.
For instance, Hou et al. 
[
22
]
 constructed a node-level privacy scope and perform LDP perturbation within this scope, updating only the affected nodes when the graph evolves, thereby reducing noise and improving efficiency.
Yuan et al. 
[
30
]
 adaptively allocated the privacy budget by analyzing structural changes between adjacent snapshots and generate synthetic graphs by perturbing node degrees and inter-community connections at the community level.
Similarly, Hao et al. 
[
31
]
 exploited community structures to mitigate perturbation impact by restricting perturbation ranges, applying differentiated strategies for edges, and updating only the changed communities to preserve structural properties.
Li et al. 
[
23
]
 modeled temporal evolution patterns using a Hidden Markov Graph (HMG) and inject noise into model parameters under differential privacy to generate synthetic graphs that retain dynamic structural characteristics.

While these approaches effectively provide privacy guarantees and preserve key structural properties, they primarily focus on the design of privacy-preserving mechanisms.
In contrast, the vulnerability of such systems to poisoning attacks during the data publishing process remains largely underexplored, potentially introducing significant security risks under adversarial manipulation.

Data Poisoning Attacks on Dynamic Graphs

Recent studies on graph data poisoning primarily focus on graph learning models, especially graph neural networks (GNNs), by introducing structural perturbations.
For instance, Zügner et al. 
[
32
]
 proposed Nettack, which misleads node classification by modifying a small number of edges or node features around a target node. Zügner and Günnemann 
[

 further introduced Meta-Attack, which leverages meta-learning to simulate the training process and identify globally effective perturbations. Dai et al. 
[
34

 proposed RL-S2V, which employs reinforcement learning to automatically learn attack strategies, improving adaptability. In addition to modifying existing structures, Sun et al. 
[
35
]
 proposed NIPA, which injects malicious nodes and connects them to target nodes to mislead predictions, while Li et al. 
[
36
]
 further optimize the connection strategies to enhance attack effectiveness.
With the development of temporal models, poisoning attacks have also been extended to dynamic graph learning tasks.
For example, Sharma et al. 
[
37
]
 proposed Dyn-PGD, which progressively perturbs edges across time steps, and Chen et al. 
[
38
]
 introduced GDA to identify critical edges using gradient information. For temporal graph neural networks, Dai et al. 
[
39
]
 proposed MemFreezing, which degrades model performance by disrupting memory mechanisms.

Beyond graph learning models, recent studies have explored poisoning attacks in statistical analysis under LDP. Cao et al. 
[
40
]
 provided the first systematic investigation of poisoning attacks against LDP protocols, demonstrating that attackers can manipulate statistical results by injecting fake users. Building on this, Li et al. 
[
41
]
 proposed OPA attacks, enabling more fine-grained manipulation of statistical outputs. In the context of graph data, He et al. 
[
21
]
 extended such attacks to LDP-based graph analysis protocols and designed multiple strategies (e.g., RVA, RNA, and MGA) to manipulate graph statistics such as degree centrality and clustering coefficient by forging adjacency information.

As shown in Table 
I
, these works primarily focus on either adversarial attacks against graph learning models or poisoning attacks in non-graph or static graph settings under LDP.
However, poisoning attacks against LDP-based dynamic graph statistics remain largely unexplored.
In particular, under decentralized settings, how to characterize the impact of attacks on released data and design effective temporal attack strategies have not been systematically studied. This gap motivates the focus of this work.

IV-C
 
Dynamic Graph Data Poisoning Attack

IV-C
1 
Overall Framework of Attack Methods

In this paper, we propose an attack method for privacy-preserving dynamic graphs, which influences the gain of target nodes by injecting fake edges. The overall framework is illustrated in Fig. 
1
, and the attack process mainly consists of three stages:

(1) Target Node Selection:
 The target node set is selected from the last time slice of the dynamic graph. All subsequent attacks are performed based on this set, and the gain of graph metrics is evaluated exclusively on these target nodes. To ensure the generality of the proposed method, attack target nodes are randomly selected.

(2) Dynamic Selection of Fake Nodes:
 At each time step, the attacker dynamically controls a number of fake nodes according to the attack strategy 
, where both the quantity and distribution are determined by the strategy.
These controlled nodes perform connection-based attacks on the target nodes within the corresponding time slice. To ensure general applicability, fake nodes are randomly selected from the node set.

(3) Fake Edge Injection:
 The attacker uploads forged connection information through controlled fake nodes, i.e., constructs fake edges associated with target nodes to alter their graph metrics and achieve the attack objective.

The specific procedure is described in Algorithm 
1
. First, a set of target nodes 
 is randomly selected from the last time slice 
 according to the ratio of the target nodes 
. Then, HMG perturbation is applied to the original dynamic graph 
, and the average degree 
 of the perturbed graph at each time slice is computed as the upper bound on the number of edges that fake nodes can establish. Based on this, the total number of fake nodes 
 is determined according to the ratio of fake nodes 
. Combined with the time steps 
, the attack scheduling strategy 
 and the temporal decay factor 
, the number of fake nodes in each time slice is dynamically controlled. Subsequently, under the joint influence of the attack strategy 
, the target node set 
, and both structural and temporal information of the graph, a fake edge set 
 is generated and injected into the perturbed dynamic graph, resulting in the attacked graph 
. In practice, fake nodes 
 upload forged adjacency vectors to stabilize and enhance the attack effect, while real nodes 
 still upload their perturbed true adjacency vectors, thereby achieving effective attacks under privacy constraints. Finally, the overall gain of the target nodes is computed by incorporating both the target node set and temporal decay weights. Overall, this method forms a complete attack pipeline, including target node selection, fake node scheduling, fake edge injection, and gain evaluation.

Dynamic graphs released by systems are typically subjected to noise injection mechanisms to ensure privacy protection. Unlike static graphs, noise injection in dynamic graphs must not only perturb structural information but also account for the temporal correlations introduced by the evolution of nodes and edges over time. In this work, we adopt the HMG method, which is specifically designed for dynamic graph perturbation, to process the data, thereby enhancing privacy protection while preserving the utility of graph structural information. It is worth noting that applying noise to the adversarial data uploaded by fake users would significantly weaken the attack effectiveness; therefore, this portion of the data is not perturbed in our approach.

IV-C
4 
Attack scheme

We propose three attack strategies: Equal-Scale Attack (ESA), Random-Scale Attack (RSA), and Temporal Enhanced Attack (TEA). These strategies manipulate the estimation of graph metrics for target nodes across different time slices by controlling varying numbers of fake nodes (see Algorithm 
2
), which generate forged edges injected into the original adjacency matrix (see Algorithm 
3
) and are subsequently uploaded to the central server.

Specifically, ESA assigns an equal number of fake nodes to each time slice; RSA controls a randomly varying number of fake nodes within a predefined range across different time slices; and TEA adaptively adjusts the number of controlled fake nodes according to the importance of each time slice, thereby enabling more targeted and intensified attacks on critical time slices.

Equal-Scale Attack (ESA):

This strategy controls the same number of fake nodes to launch attacks across all time slices, thereby imposing approximately uniform attack intensity over time. ESA does not consider the relative importance of nodes across different time slices; thus, it exhibits relatively stable overall gain performance and can serve as a baseline for evaluating and comparing other attack strategies.

Random-Scale Attack (RSA):

This strategy enhances attack stealthiness by randomly controlling a certain number of fake nodes to launch attacks across different time slices. Even if the attack is detected, it is difficult to accurately infer the distribution of attack intensity over time. However, the effectiveness of RSA exhibits considerable variability. When attacks are primarily concentrated in earlier time slices, their contribution to overall metrics is relatively limited, resulting in weaker attack performance. In contrast, when attacks are concentrated in later time slices, the attack effectiveness can be significantly improved, but at the cost of an increased risk of detection.

Temporal-Enhanced Attack (TEA):

This strategy takes into account that the relative importance of nodes varies across different time slices in dynamic graphs, i.e., the importance of time slices evolves over time. As time progresses, it gradually increases the number of controlled fake nodes, thereby maximizing the impact on the estimation of graph metrics for target nodes without significantly increasing the risk of detection. Compared with ESA and RSA, TEA more effectively captures temporal evolution characteristics and achieves a better balance between attack effectiveness and stealthiness.

VI 
Conclusion

In this work, we conduct a systematic study of data poisoning attacks against privacy-preserving dynamic graphs under the local differential privacy (LDP) framework, and further explore their potential security implications. To capture the temporal characteristics of dynamic graphs, we introduce a novel attack effectiveness metric, namely absolute gain, which quantifies the cumulative impact of attacks on graph statistical metrics over time. Based on this metric, we design a temporal enhanced attack (TEA) strategy that adaptively schedules attack resources across time steps. We also develop two baseline strategies, equal-scale attack (ESA) and random-scale attack (RSA), to enable comprehensive evaluation and comparison.
Extensive experimental results on both real-world and synthetic graphs demonstrate that the proposed attack methods can significantly degrade the accuracy of graph metric estimation for target nodes. The findings reveal critical security vulnerabilities in existing LDP-based dynamic graph mechanisms. In particular, we observe that attackers can exploit temporal correlations in dynamic graphs to amplify attack impact, while current privacy-preserving approaches remain insufficiently robust against such adversarial behaviors.

For future work, it is important to design more robust LDP mechanisms and effective attack detection strategies to mitigate poisoning attacks in dynamic graph settings. Moreover, extending the proposed framework to a broader range of dynamic graph analysis tasks and evaluating its performance on larger-scale real-world graphs would further enhance the practical applicability and security of privacy-preserving dynamic graph systems.

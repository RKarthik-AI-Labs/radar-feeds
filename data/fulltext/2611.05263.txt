Abstract

Multimodal web agents have shown promising capabilities for interacting with websites through graphical user interfaces (GUIs), yet realistic web tasks often require long-horizon, multi-step planning that remains challenging for monolithic policies. Such agents must jointly infer high-level plans, navigate through intermediate states, track progress over extended interaction histories, and execute visually grounded actions. Although prior work has explored modular multi-agent systems with a separate planner, the planner is typically not updated jointly with the actor. We investigate whether explicitly separating and jointly training planning and execution provides a stronger inductive bias for long-horizon web navigation. We introduce JHiMA-WebRL, a joint hierarchical multi-agent reinforcement learning framework for web agents that couples a task decomposer with an actor. To train the decomposer, we use a strong teacher model to construct structured subtask supervision from multimodal GUI trajectories, and use these annotations to fine-tune both the decomposer and the subtask-conditioned actor. We then extend Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) to jointly optimize the decomposer and actor in the interactive WebArena environment through hierarchical rollout grouping and role-specific advantages. On WebArena-Lite-v2, JHiMA-WebRL-14B, consisting of a 7B actor and a 7B decomposer, achieves an average success rate of 37.2%, compared with 24.0% for a ScaleCUA-32B agent that we fine-tune with RL on the same training tasks and evaluate under the same protocol. Ablations show that jointly updating the decomposer during RL outperforms updating the actor alone. These results suggest that jointly learned role factorization with smaller models can be a competitive alternative to scaling monolithic agents for long-horizon web tasks.

1 
Introduction

Multimodal web agents interact with websites by perceiving screenshots, interpreting page elements, and executing actions such as clicking, typing, scrolling, and navigating between webpages. Such agents could support workflows in online shopping, web-based administration, repository management, and information retrieval. Benchmarks such as WebArena 
(
Zhou et al., 2024
)
 and VisualWebArena 
(
Koh et al., 2024
)
 evaluate this setting through tasks that require interactive execution across multiple steps. These tasks combine high-level planning with low-level visually grounded control: for example, to cancel an order, an agent must navigate intermediate pages, identify relevant interface elements, track progress over a long action history, and avoid repeated or irrelevant actions.

Many web agents use a monolithic policy that maps the task, observation, and action history directly to the next action, learning planning, state tracking, visual grounding, and execution within a single model. ScaleCUA 
(
Liu et al., 2025b
)
 and UI-TARS 
(
Qin et al., 2025
)
 demonstrate the value of large-scale GUI data for training such policies, while WebAgent-R1 
(
Wei et al., 2025
)
 and WebRL 
(
Qi et al., 2025
)
 improve web interaction through online reinforcement learning (RL). Modular systems instead introduce a separate planner: Agent S uses a manager to generate subtasks from retrieved knowledge and experience for workers to execute 
(
Agashe et al., 2025a
)
, and Agent S2 adds proactive replanning as execution provides new observations 
(
Agashe et al., 2025b
)
. Even though an explicit planner module can support long-horizon behavior, the planner’s parameters are not updated from the outcomes of action execution. Recent studies 
(
Erdogan et al., 2025
; 
Wu et al., 2026
; 
Kong et al., 2026
; 
Lu et al., 2025
)

focus on training modular agents for long-horizon tasks, but either train the modules separately, apply supervised learning or RL to only one module while freezing the other, or alternate updates between modules. To our knowledge, prior work has not studied simultaneous RL training of a separately parameterized planner and actor from a shared rollout batch in web environments.

We therefore introduce JHiMA-WebRL, a joint hierarchical multi-agent reinforcement learning framework for long-horizon web interaction. Figure 
1
 presents an overview of the three stages in our framework: decomposer data generation, supervised fine-tuning, and joint hierarchical multi-agent RL. First, a strong teacher model converts multimodal GUI trajectories into ordered subtasks, supplying planning annotations that existing GUI trajectory datasets typically lack. Second, these annotations support supervised fine-tuning of two Qwen2.5-VL-7B models: a decomposer that predicts subtasks from the instruction and starting screenshot, and an actor that predicts the next GUI action conditioned on those subtasks. Finally, we jointly train both modules using terminal task success rewards while interacting with web environments, so that the decomposer can adapt its plans to the actor’s capabilities while the actor adapts to executing them. We extend Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) through the hierarchical rollout structure: the decomposer proposes 
 candidate decompositions, and the actor performs 
 complete environment rollouts for each decomposition. Actor-level advantages compare executions under the same plan, while decomposer-level advantages compare plans by the average terminal reward of their executions, and both policies are updated from this shared batch without dense process rewards.

We evaluate JHiMA-WebRL-14B, our instantiation with a 7B actor and a 7B decomposer, on WebArena-Lite-v2 
(
Liu et al., 2025b
)
, a refined subset of WebArena tasks for purely visual agents. JHiMA-WebRL-14B achieves an average success rate of 37.2%, compared with 24.0% for a ScaleCUA-32B agent that we fine-tune with RL on the same training tasks and evaluate under the same protocol. Ablation studies further indicate that jointly updating the decomposer during RL is beneficial relative to updating the actor alone. These results suggest that joint training of role-factorized modules can be a competitive alternative to scaling monolithic agents.

(
Erdogan et al., 2025
)
. Our approach also uses trajectory-derived planning supervision, then jointly adapts both modules through RL. Navigating WebAI pairs a supervised hierarchical planner with an RL-trained controller

PPO and GRPO provide the basic optimization tools for recent LLM and agent RL, while multi-agent RL methods such as MADDPG, COMA, QMIX, and MAPPO study credit assignment across interacting policies

. For web and GUI agents, WebAgent-R1, WebRL, OpenWebRL, and OpAgent apply online multi-turn RL to browser-based tasks, optimizing a single agent policy from terminal or outcome-based rewards 
(
Wei et al., 2025
; 
Qi et al., 2025
; 
Yang et al., 2026a
; 
Guo et al., 2026
)
, while WorkForceAgent-R1 applies rule-based RL to single-step web decisions 
(
Zhuang et al., 2025
)
. Other works improve credit assignment with process or progress signals, including Web-Shepherd, WebArbiter, TGPO, GraphGPO, and MiRA 
(
Chae et al., 2025

Zhang et al., 2026

Chen et al., 2025

3 
Method

We propose JHiMA-WebRL, a joint hierarchical multi-agent reinforcement learning framework for long-horizon web navigation. Our approach factorizes interaction into two specialized but jointly optimized modules: a task decomposer and an actor. The decomposer maps the task instruction and initial screenshot into an ordered sequence of executable subtasks. The actor then conditions on the task, current observation, interaction history, and generated subtasks to produce visually grounded actions in the web interface. Below, we first describe the problem formulation, followed by the three stages of our framework: data generation for training the decomposer, supervised fine-tuning of both the decomposer and actor, and a joint hierarchical multi-agent extension of GRPO.

4 
Experiments

4.1 
Datasets

The task decomposer was trained with supervised fine-tuning using approximately 
K task-decomposition examples, which were generated from ScaleCUA trajectories using GPT-5.2-Codex as described in Section 
3.2
. The actor was fine-tuned on all ScaleCUA trajectories augmented with the teacher-annotated subtasks, along with grounding data spanning web, mobile, and desktop platforms, resulting in 2,535,552 training examples. Unlike standard ScaleCUA SFT, the actor thus receives a task decomposition in its context as a planning scaffold.

Both modules were then jointly trained through online reinforcement learning using the training tasks associated with WebArena-Lite, following the setup of WebAgent-R1 
(
Wei et al., 2025
)
. WebArena contains 
 interactive web tasks spanning five domains—Reddit, GitLab, e-commerce administration, maps, and online shopping. WebArena-Lite 
(
Liu et al., 2025a
)
 selects a cleaned subset of 
 tasks for evaluation, leaving 
 tasks from the original WebArena benchmark for reinforcement-learning interaction. No WebArena-Lite evaluation task (and hence no WebArena-Lite-v2 task) is used during SFT or RL. We also employed the flexible evaluation protocol of 
Liu et al. (2025b)
, including LLM-based fuzzy matching using GPT-5.2-Codex, to compute the binary task outcome reward at the end of web interaction.

For evaluation, we used WebArena-Lite-v2, introduced by ScaleCUA 
(
Liu et al., 2025b
)
. WebArena-Lite-v2 is a refinement of the evaluation subset of WebArena-Lite designed specifically for evaluating purely visual GUI agents; it removes 
 tasks that require multi-tab interaction and revises task instructions and evaluators. It contains 
 tasks across five website environments: Shopping, ShoppingAdmin, Reddit, GitLab, and OpenStreetMap. The benchmark includes heterogeneous task objectives such as question answering, navigation, page-content matching, and state-changing operations, with success determined by the flexible evaluation criteria of 
Liu et al. (2025b)
.

4.2 
Baselines

We compared against native agents at several model scales ranging from 7B to 72B, allowing comparisons between our 14B modular system and both smaller and larger monolithic systems. We also compared against multi-agent systems consisting of a proprietary planner (GPT-4o) with a grounding module. These provide comparisons with systems that separate planning from visual grounding, but do not jointly train a learned decomposer and actor as in JHiMA-WebRL-14B. The UI-TARS and GPT-4o planner–grounder results are taken from ScaleCUA 
(
Liu et al., 2025b
)
 (15-step budget). The ScaleCUA-7B/32B SFT and GUI-Libra-8B results are taken from the re-evaluation of 
Yang et al. (2026b)
, which uses a 15-step budget, averages four runs, and replaces the GPT-4o fuzzy-match judge with GPT-5. Under its original evaluation setup, ScaleCUA reports higher scores for its own models (37.7% for ScaleCUA-7B and 44.2% for ScaleCUA-32B at 15 steps). We also trained a ScaleCUA-32B RL baseline, specifically fine-tuning the SFT model on the WebArena training set, using standard multi-turn GRPO with 7 rollouts and a maximum of 15 interaction steps. For a fair comparison with our method, we only included baselines that do not use HTML or DOM information.

Results

As shown in Table

4.5 
Ablation Study

We analyzed the contribution of the components of JHiMA-WebRL-14B through ablations (Table 
2
). First, we trained only the actor with RL while keeping the SFT decomposer frozen (‘w/o decomposer RL’), using the same number of rollouts per task (
, 
) as JHiMA-WebRL-14B. This reduced the average success rate to 33.4%, a drop of 3.8 percentage points, indicating that updating the decomposer jointly with the actor matters. Second, to assess the role of the decomposer, we trained the actor alone using standard multi-turn GRPO without subtask conditioning. The actor was initialized from Qwen2.5-VL-7B and fine-tuned using supervised learning on released ScaleCUA trajectories and grounding data, without any subtasks. We trained two variants: one with the same total number of rollouts per task as JHiMA-WebRL-14B (21), and one with 7 rollouts, matching the per-decomposition group size of our actor. The two variants reached 34.9% and 35.2%, i.e., 2.3 and 2.0 percentage points below JHiMA-WebRL-14B. Finally, we removed RL entirely and evaluated the SFT decomposer and SFT actor together (‘w/o RL’), which reduced the average success rate by 2.1 percentage points. Notably, actor-only RL with a frozen SFT decomposer (33.4%) scored below this SFT-only system (35.1%), whereas joint RL improved on it; the SFT-only modular system also performed on par with the decomposer-free actors trained with RL (34.9% and 35.2%).

4.6 
Subgoal Quality Analysis

We also analyzed whether the generated decompositions provide meaningful intermediate structure. Following the subgoal-quality analysis of

Wang et al. (2026)
, we measured whether partial subgoal completion was
predictive of eventual task success and whether task success increased
monotonically as more subgoals were completed.

For each WebArena-Lite-v2 trajectory from the four evaluation runs, we used GPT-5.2-Codex to judge whether
each subgoal generated by the decomposer was completed, based on the sequence of predicted actions and resulting screenshots, and computed the fraction of
generated subgoals that were completed.
We first evaluated whether the fraction of completed subgoals discriminated successful
trajectories from failed ones. We treated the fraction as a continuous
prediction score for the binary task outcome and computed the area under the
receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC).
As shown in Figure 
2(a)
, the subgoals generated by
JHiMA-WebRL-14B achieved an AUROC of
0.838, well above chance (0.5) and comparable to the SFT-only system (‘w/o RL’, 0.836). Progress through the generated decompositions is therefore predictive of downstream task success, both before and after joint RL.

A useful decomposition should also provide a monotone notion of progress, so we grouped
trajectories by subgoal completion fraction and computed the
empirical success rate per group. For JHiMA-WebRL-14B, empirical task success increases monotonically with subgoal
completion fraction (Figure 
2(b)
), with Kendall’s rank correlation
coefficient 
, compared with 
 without RL. Together with the AUROC analysis, these
results suggest that the generated subgoals act as intermediate milestones associated with downstream task completion.

5 
Limitations

Our study has several limitations. (i) 
Train–test overlap:
 RL is performed on the remaining WebArena tasks, which are disjoint from the evaluation tasks but can share intent templates and websites with them; results thus reflect in-environment generalization, not transfer to unseen websites. (ii) 
Supervision and cost:
 decomposition supervision is generated by a proprietary teacher (GPT-5.2-Codex) without human verification of annotation quality, and hierarchical rollouts require 
 complete episodes per task, which is costly in live environments. (iii) 
Open-loop planning:
 the decomposer plans once from the initial observation and cannot revise the plan during execution. (iv) 
Coarse credit:
 terminal rewards assign the same advantage to every token of a decomposition or trajectory, without identifying which subtask or action caused success or failure. (v) 
Evaluation scope:
 we evaluate on a single benchmark of 154 tasks, and the ablation differences (2.0–3.8 percentage points) are of a size where run-to-run variance matters.

6 
Conclusion and Future Directions

We introduced JHiMA-WebRL, a framework for jointly training a task decomposer and a visually grounded actor. The framework generates subtask supervision from multimodal GUI trajectories to train the decomposer and extends GRPO with hierarchical rollout grouping and role-specific advantages. JHiMA-WebRL-14B achieved an average success rate of 37.2% on WebArena-Lite-v2, 13.2 percentage points above a ScaleCUA-32B RL baseline trained and evaluated under the same protocol. In ablations, removing decomposer updates during RL, removing the decomposer together with subtask conditioning, or removing RL each lowered the average success rate, by 2.0–3.8 percentage points.

Our work suggests several future directions. First, the decomposer could be made 
adaptive
, revising subtasks as execution deviates from the plan. Second, joint optimization can be combined with intermediate progress signals such as milestone rewards 
(
Wang et al., 2026
)
 or graph-based credit assignment 
(
Cheng et al., 2026
)
. Finally, jointly trained decomposer–actor systems could be extended to cross-domain and live-web settings, where online RL has recently been shown to be feasible 
(
Yang et al., 2026a
)
. This would test whether modular role factorization transfers better than monolithic policies.

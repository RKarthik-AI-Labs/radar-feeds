Abstract

Agents built on frontier language models are expensive. At every step, the frontier model re-reads its entire context and
executes every subtask regardless of its difficulty. This paper explores the simple thesis that much of this
cost can be removed by delegating easy subtasks to a small language model, or SLM, that runs on hardware one already owns.
To test it, we build Delegent, a simple and effective agent harness built on Claude Code, in which the frontier model stays in charge of
every task but hands focused subtasks, such as investigating code, running tests, and searching a corpus, to SLM
subagents. Existing approaches such as cascades and model routers usually choose which model executes the entire task, whereas in Delegent, the frontier model always leads the execution and delegates easy subtasks to the SLM. No router or model is trained, so either model can be swapped freely. With the help of
a 27B FP8 SLM, Delegent reduces the frontier cost of long-horizon coding by 67% and of agentic search by 59%, with no loss
in task performance, and even a 2B SLM that fits on a laptop reduces the coding cost by 66%. The savings grow with the legwork that the frontier model would otherwise do itself, and a short
instruction, which leaves the main coding tasks to the frontier model, accounts for most of the saving and keeps the 2B SLM
from losing tasks. Code: 
https://github.com/castorini/delegent
.

1 
Introduction

Agents are simply models using tools in loops. The harness invokes the model, executes its tool calls, returns the
results, and invokes the model again, until the task is done. The loop is simple but costly, because at every step the model
re-reads its entire context, which grows with every tool call. A frontier coding agent that takes a hundred steps to
resolve an issue thus pays for a hundred ever longer prompts. Long-horizon agents are therefore expensive for organizations that pay per
token, and individuals on subscription plans exhaust their quota and have to wait for the next reset.

Yet much of what a frontier model does at each step is easy legwork, such as locating the relevant code, reproducing a bug, running
tests, issuing searches, and reading documents. Little of this work seems to require a frontier model, yet existing agents use the
frontier model for all of it, even in subagents, billing every step at frontier prices. A small language model, or SLM, costs far
less, and nothing extra on a GPU that one already owns. An SLM on its own falls well short of a frontier model on these tasks and cannot
replace one, but it does not need to compete with the frontier if the frontier model stays in charge and delegates only easy subtasks. This leads to a simple thesis:

A small language model running on local hardware can take over the easy subtasks of a frontier model, cutting its
cost substantially without hurting task performance, and without any training.

To test this thesis, we build Delegent (
Figure
 
1
), an agent harness that runs a frontier model and a local SLM as a team. For
coding, Delegent is derived from Claude Code.

Claude Code, but routes each model call by role, sending the calls of the main loop to the frontier model and the calls

final answer. Since nothing is trained, either model is a drop-in component.

As the headline result, we find that the thesis holds on both long-horizon coding and agentic search. With GPT-6.1-Sol at
high reasoning effort as the frontier model and Qwen3.8-27B-FP8 as the SLM, the frontier model’s cost on DeepSWE falls to a third,
with no loss in resolved tasks. On 694 search questions, its cost falls to 41%, and it answers exactly as many
questions correctly as on its own. Even a 2B model, MiniCPM5-2B, cuts frontier cost on DeepSWE to a third, and with Qwen3.8-27B-FP8 paid for at API prices, the
total cost of a DeepSWE task is still only 38%. Delegation adds
some latency, which is bounded by the local hardware that serves the SLM.

Contrary to the dominant approach to combining cheap and expensive models, we do not let the SLM answer first and then
decide whether to trust it 
(
Chen et al., 2024
; 
Aggarwal et al., 2024
; 
Yue et al., 2024
)
, nor do we train a router that
picks one model per query 
(
Ding et al., 2024
; 
Ong et al., 2025
)
. In both cases, a model is given the entire task and must solve it by itself, which a 2B SLM is rarely capable of. In Delegent, the frontier model always produces the
final answer, and the SLM only does the legwork. No output of the SLM is accepted without the frontier model reading it
first. Our contribution is thus a piece of harness engineering: the coordination of two models of very
different cost.

To better circumscribe our claims, let us be more precise:

•

By 
cost
, we mean what the frontier model is charged, with prompt caching taken into account. The SLM
runs on a GPU under the user’s control, and for coding, we also price Qwen3.8-27B-FP8 at API prices.

•

By 
local
, we mean an SLM served on a single GPU under the user’s control. The 27B FP8 model needs a
workstation GPU, and the 2B model fits on a laptop.

•

By 
training-free
, we mean that no model is trained or fine-tuned, so the SLM and the frontier
model can each be swapped freely.

This paper makes three contributions. First, we build Delegent, an open-source, training-free harness in which a frontier
model delegates work to a local SLM, which we describe in 
Section
 
2
. Second, we evaluate it on three coding and
five search datasets with two SLMs of very different sizes, and show in 
Section
 
4
 that frontier cost falls by
more than half in eight of nine evaluations while task performance holds. Third, we analyze in 
Section
 
5
 where the savings come from and how they depend on our instruction and
the frontier model.

2 
The Delegent Harness

Delegent is built on Claude Code and coordinates two models of very different cost, as 
Figure
 
1
 illustrates. The 
frontier model

leads every task. It plans, decides what to delegate and whether to believe what comes back, and writes the final patch
or answer. 
SLM subagents
 on a local GPU do the legwork, each in its own tool loop and with a fresh context.
Delegent lets the frontier model judge the task type, coding or search, and supplies the matching tool set.

Routing model calls by role.

In Claude Code, the main loop calls a model at every step, and so does each subagent that it starts with the built-in Agent
tool. Minor jobs go to a small auxiliary model. For each task, Delegent places a relay between Claude Code and the models. The relay speaks
the Anthropic Messages API, so Claude Code runs unmodified, and it routes each call according to the model that the call
names. Calls from the main loop go to the frontier model, and calls from the subagents and the auxiliary model go to
the SLM. Every frontier call within a task carries the same prompt-cache key, so consecutive steps hit the provider’s
cache. SLM calls go to vLLM 
(
Kwon et al., 2023
)
 through a proxy that pins each subagent to one GPU replica, so
its growing prefix stays cached.

Running subtasks.

For coding, the frontier model delegates a subtask through its Agent tool and writes a brief, the instructions for the
Claude Code subagent. The subagent works in the same container with Claude Code’s default tools. For search, Delegent adds a 
Delegate
 tool for delegating one focused search
subtask, such as finding or checking a fact. Instead of designing the search subagent from scratch, we borrow from
AREX 
(
Lu et al., 2026
)
, a family of deep research agents. Its smallest member, AREX-Turbo, is built around a 4B
model, so its prompts and tools are already written for a small model to follow. An AREX-Turbo agent works in a loop
with four tools: it searches the corpus, visits the documents it finds, compresses its own history into notes when the
history grows long, and finishes with an answer, the evidence that supports it, and a confidence. Our SLM subagent sees only the subtask
and the question, and its final answer, evidence, and confidence form the report that returns to the frontier model. In both coding and search, subtasks from one turn run concurrently.

Returning reports.

The frontier model’s context receives only the final report of a subagent and none of its tool outputs. For search, the harness
also looks up each evidence line in the cited document and notes whether it was found verbatim.

Steering the frontier model.

A short instruction (
Appendix
 
A
) explains the economics to the frontier model. Since every step re-reads the entire context, the
cheapest plan has the fewest steps of the frontier model’s own, so the instruction asks it to delegate tool-heavy work
and to keep its own tool use to a few targeted checks. It covers both kinds of delegation, Claude Code’s subagents for
coding and the 
Delegate
 tool for search.

Coding.

The thesis holds on all three coding sets (
Table
 
1
). With Qwen3.8-27B-FP8 as its subagent, the
frontier model resolves no fewer tasks than on its own, at between a third and a half of the cost. On DeepSWE, the frontier model’s cost falls
from $2.11 to $0.70 per task. The results with MiniCPM5-2B are much the same: task performance holds, and on DeepSWE the 2B
model saves as much as the 27B FP8 model.

Paying for the SLM changes little on long tasks. At API prices, Qwen3.8-27B-FP8 adds about 10 cents to a DeepSWE task, so its total cost stays at 38%, and even on the short tasks of SWE-bench Verified, the total is 57%.

Search.

Across all 694 search questions (
Table
 
2
), the frontier model answers 663 correctly with Qwen3.8-27B-FP8 as its subagent, exactly as many as on its own, at
41% of the cost. On no set do the two systems differ by more than three questions. The savings, however, vary
considerably. On ClimbMix, frontier cost falls to between a quarter and a third, but on BrowseComp-Plus
over its own corpus, it falls only to 63%.

The difference lies in how much the frontier model has to read on its own. On BrowseComp-Plus, the corpus is a set of
files, and a command such as 
rg
 returns only the lines that match. On ClimbMix, the only access is BM25
search, which returns whole documents that the model then reads a page at a time. Answering the same questions thus
takes more than twice as much reading on ClimbMix, and delegation moves most of it to the SLM. Since a web agent also
finds pages through a search engine and opens them, we consider the ClimbMix savings more representative.

Prompt caching.

Every system, including the frontier model alone, uses the provider’s prompt cache with a stable key per task, as any
practical deployment would, so all savings are measured against a cached baseline. Nearly all frontier input on coding is cached, with or without delegation.

Latency.

Delegation adds latency that depends mainly on the local hardware serving the SLM. With Qwen3.8-27B-FP8, the median DeepSWE task takes 38 minutes instead of 25,
and a short SWE-bench Verified task about twice as long.

5 
Analysis

The results leave two questions open, namely where the savings come from and why task performance holds. We answer
them with statistics computed from the transcripts of our runs. Unless noted otherwise, we use DeepSWE, the hardest of
our task sets. Its tasks are long, and the frontier model alone resolves just over half of them, so DeepSWE leaves the
most room for savings and would also reveal any loss in task performance.

How do the savings vary across tasks?

The saving on a task depends on how much work the frontier model would do on it alone. 
Figure
 
2
 groups the
coding tasks by the number of calls that the frontier model makes without delegation. On tasks that need fewer than 25
calls, delegation brings frontier cost to about 60% of working alone, and on 11% of these tasks, it even costs more.
The relative cost keeps falling as tasks grow longer, to about a quarter for tasks that need more than 200 calls, where
delegation never costs more. On search, the corresponding quantity is how much the frontier model reads (
Section
 
4
). The
saving can thus be anticipated from how long the frontier model works on a task alone.

Where do the savings come from?

Claude Code already delegates by default, but to the frontier model itself. Without our harness, GPT-6.1-Sol starts
subagents on 98% of DeepSWE tasks, and these subagents, which also run on GPT-6.1-Sol, make more calls than the main loop
and account for 53% of its cost. Delegent moves this work to the SLM, which explains 80% of the saving. The remainder
comes from the frontier model itself, which now takes 46 to 48 LLM turns per task instead of 84. It reads and edits code
about as often as before, but its test runs drop from about eight per task to one or two, and it no longer makes nearly all of the 23
calls per task that it spent on maintaining a task list and messaging its subagents. Since only reports return, the tool output that reaches the frontier model also shrinks, by 11 to 12% on DeepSWE and by 13 to 24% on 
SWE-bench
 Pro.

What work does the SLM take over?

The SLM takes over the legwork of finding and checking, while the frontier model handles the main coding tasks itself. On
coding, a subagent typically searches the repository for the code involved in an issue, reads the relevant files, and
runs the tests or a small script to reproduce a bug or confirm a fix. Per DeepSWE task, the subagents of Qwen3.8-27B-FP8 issue
about 24 code searches, read about 30 files or parts of files, run the tests 12 times, and run 5 scripts of their own.
The files they write, about four per task, are nearly all such 
throwaway
 scripts outside the repository. MiniCPM5-2B works
the same way with more steps, and in 9 of 50 episodes, it also edits source files. On search, a subagent issues its own
BM25 searches, reads the documents it finds, and returns an answer with quoted evidence, its sources, and a confidence,
and the harness checks each quote against its source. In both cases, the frontier model receives findings that it can
verify cheaply, and it decides the answer on its own.

Is the instruction necessary?

Table
 
3
 varies the instruction and the model that runs the subagents, and shows that the instruction is necessary, particularly for the weaker SLM.
Without the instruction, delegating to an SLM about halves frontier cost. With Qwen3.8-27B-FP8, the frontier model resolves 28 of
the 50 tasks at 55% of the cost, but with MiniCPM5-2B, it resolves only 22, seven fewer than on its own. With the
instruction, both SLMs match the frontier model alone at a third of the cost, so task performance no longer depends on
the SLM. With subagents still running on GPT-6.1-Sol, the instruction alone brings frontier cost to 60% with no loss in
task performance, and replacing these subagents with an SLM removes a further 27 points. Roughly three fifths of the
saving can thus be attributed to the instruction and two fifths to the SLM.

Not much in our experiments, even though the two SLMs are far apart on their own: on the same 30 DeepSWE tasks, Qwen3.8-27B-FP8 resolves 5
and MiniCPM5-2B none. As subagents, however, they lead to the same task performance and nearly the same frontier cost
(
Table
 
1
). Since the frontier model hands out narrow subtasks and handles the main coding tasks itself,
a weaker SLM mostly costs more SLM calls, 106 instead of 82 per DeepSWE task, rather than accuracy.

What changes with a different frontier model?

The saving depends on how the frontier model works, as 
Table
 
4
 shows for GPT-6.1-Sol and Claude Opus 5.5. On its own,
GPT-6.1-Sol works in a long loop of editing, testing, and fixing. It takes 84 LLM turns per DeepSWE task and makes its
first edit at turn 18. Claude Opus 5.5 instead plans before it acts, taking 18 turns and making its first edit at turn 7.
Delegation follows these habits. GPT-6.1-Sol hands off much of its loop, mostly after it has started editing, so about
94 turns per task move to the SLM and its cost falls to 34%. Claude Opus 5.5 delegates mainly before its first edit, to learn
about the code it is about to change, and writes longer briefs, about 130 words instead of 45. Only about 21 turns move
to the SLM, and its cost falls to 69%, roughly in proportion to its own turns, which fall from 18 to 13. With either
SLM, Claude Opus 5.5 also resolves slightly more tasks than on its own. Thus, Delegent saves the most for a frontier model
that does much of its own legwork, while a frontier model that plans first uses the SLM mainly to inform its plan.

7 
Conclusion

On Delegent, a frontier model spends a third as much on DeepSWE and 41% as much on agentic search, with no loss in
task performance, and the saving grows with the legwork the frontier model would otherwise do. Claude Code
already delegates much of this legwork, but to the frontier model. A short instruction that leaves the main coding tasks
to the frontier model makes this work, and with it, even a 2B SLM loses no tasks. How much is saved also depends on the
frontier model. One that iterates at length saves the most, while one that plans first uses the SLM mainly to inform its
plan. These results support our thesis that a local SLM can take over much of the work of a frontier model, cutting its
cost without hurting task performance and without training.

Limitations

Reliance on Claude Code.

For coding, Delegent reuses the agent loop and subagents of Claude Code as they are, and adds only the harness around them: routing each model call by role to a frontier model or a local SLM, cache-aware serving on both
sides, and the instruction that steers delegation. For search, it also adds the delegation tool and the evidence check, and its SLM subagent uses the prompts and
tools of AREX-Turbo, whose final report cites a source for every claim, which is what the evidence
check needs.
The amount of harness engineering is thus modest, and our finding is that it suffices to remove most of the frontier cost.

Cost of the SLM.

The SLM is not free. We report frontier cost because it is the metered, scarce resource, and both of our SLMs fit on hardware that a
user can own. At the API price of the same FP8 model on Chutes, $0.039 per million input tokens,
a tenth of that for input repeated from the subagent’s previous request, which is 86 to 90% of its input in our runs, and $1.25 per million output tokens, Qwen3.8-27B-FP8 adds 3 to 10 cents per coding task, and the total stays at 38 to 57% of the frontier model alone. No API serves MiniCPM5-2B. We price the SLM for coding only, since
our search logs keep no per-call token counts.

Coverage of tasks and models.

We study agentic coding and agentic search only, though these are two of the most important capabilities of today’s
models. Our main results use GPT-6.1-Sol at high reasoning effort. We test Claude Opus 5.5 as the frontier model on DeepSWE only, and the 2B
SLM on coding only. For coding, Delegent builds on Claude Code, which Claude Opus 5.5 is likely tuned for, so it may favor
Claude Opus 5.5 there. On agentic search, the frontier model uses each corpus’s standard setup, and GPT-6.1-Sol alone answers
193 of 200 BrowseComp-Plus questions, so it is not held back there. How much work is delegated depends on how well the
frontier model follows the instruction, which may differ for other frontier models.

Latency.

Delegation makes tasks slower, about twice as slow at the median on short coding tasks, which may not suit interactive
use. How much slower depends on the hardware that serves the SLM. Our latencies were measured on shared GPUs of several
kinds and will vary with the GPU, the serving stack, and the load.

No claim of improved performance.

Agent runs are not deterministic, and different frontier models use delegation differently. GPT-6.1-Sol hands off
its legwork to save cost, while Claude Opus 5.5 uses its subagents to check its own work. A gain in task performance is
therefore not guaranteed for every frontier model or task set. We claim only that delegation causes no loss in task
performance, even where the delegating system solves more tasks than the frontier model alone.
The reduction in frontier cost, on the other hand, is consistent across task sets and models.

Appendix A 
Prompts

Coding instruction.

We supply this instruction to Claude Code as a user-level 
CLAUDE.md
 file.

# Working with subagents

Subagents (the Agent tool) run on a small, fast model and cost far less than you. Every step you take re-reads the whole
conversation, so the cheapest plan is the one with the fewest steps of your own.

- Delegate investigation (find the code involved in the issue and explain how it works; write a minimal script
that reproduces the bug and report its output) and verification (run the reproduction and the relevant tests; report
only what fails). Make the code changes yourself.

- Run subagents in the foreground (do not use run_in_background) and launch independent ones in the same turn,
so their reports come back together and nothing needs polling.

- Ask each subagent for a short report, at most 150 words: only the facts you need, with file paths, line numbers
and exact error messages.

- Do not keep a task list (TaskCreate / TaskUpdate / TaskList / TaskGet) for this job; keep your plan in your own
messages.

- Subagent reports can be wrong: check a fact yourself before your fix depends on it.

Search instruction.

We append this instruction to the system prompt of the frontier model.

You have a research assistant: the Delegate tool hands it a focused subtask; it searches the same corpus with its own
search engine and returns a short report with evidence, the docids and its confidence. It is fast, and its
high-confidence reports are usually right. Work through it:

- Delegate all searching, including checks of a candidate ("does the corpus confirm that X ...?"). Run independent
subtasks in parallel.

- Do not run broad searches or read whole documents yourself. To confirm one fact, use a single targeted search, or
read only the part of the document around it (get_document with an offset).

- Each report ends with an automatic lookup of its evidence in the cited documents: a located passage is verbatim
there.

- Give your final answer as soon as the evidence covers every condition of the question.

Example briefs.

Below is the first brief that each frontier model wrote for a Qwen3.8-27B-FP8 subagent on the same DeepSWE
task, which adds module loading flags to the interpreter of the ABS language. The brief of GPT-6.1-Sol:

Inspect ABS module require loader, cache, environments, tests and project instructions. Read-only investigation; do
not edit. Identify files/functions to change and relevant test commands, including how environment stderr and runtime
values work. Short report at most 150 words with paths and line numbers.

The brief of Claude Opus 5.5, abridged:

In the Go repo at /app (the ABS programming language interpreter), report facts only (max 200 words, with file paths
and line numbers; do not modify files):

- How the builtin require(path) is implemented today: the function name/file, how it resolves the path (relative
to what directory? how the current file’s directory is tracked [...]), whether there’s a module cache (variable name,
key type), and how the module file is lexed/parsed/evaluated (which helper it calls).

- How builtin functions are registered (the map name/file, function signature, how they access the environment
[...]).

- The CLI entrypoint: main.go and repl/repl.go BeginRepl(args []string, version string) [...]

The later testing brief of Claude Opus 5.5 asks the subagent, for every failing test outside the new test files, to check whether it also
fails on the base branch and to report whether the failure is pre-existing.

Search subagent.

On search, the SLM subagent works in our own loop of at most 80 steps with four tools from AREX-Turbo:

•

search
 takes up to five queries and returns the top 10 BM25 hits for each with their best-matching
passage, through the ClimbMix search API or, on BrowseComp-Plus, a BM25 index of the corpus files with the
benchmark’s default parameters.

•

visit
 returns the passages of a document that best match a stated goal.

•

update_context
 replaces the history of the subagent with its own notes.

•

finish
 returns the answer, its evidence with sources, and a confidence.

We took these prompts and tools as published, leaving out the Google Scholar tool of AREX-Turbo, and did not tune them.

Frontier model on search.

The frontier model runs on the BrowseComp-Plus corpus with the system prompt and the Bash and Read tools of Claude
Code, taken from a recorded Claude Code session, in our own loop, which adds the 
Delegate
 tool. On ClimbMix, it
uses the 
search
 and 
get_document
 tools of the official BrowseComp-Plus agent. With delegation, the
output of the frontier model’s own tool calls on BrowseComp-Plus is cut at 12,000 characters instead of Claude Code’s
30,000. The cut applied to 18 of its 395 own calls.

Hardware.

Each SLM replica serves several concurrent episodes, almost always from a single GPU. For coding,
Qwen3.8-27B-FP8 ran on NVIDIA H200 and RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell GPUs, and MiniCPM5-2B on H200, RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell, RTX 6000 Ada, and
L40S GPUs. The Claude Opus 5.5 runs also used H100 and RTX A6000 GPUs, with one Qwen3.8-27B-FP8 replica split across two A6000s. For
search, Qwen3.8-27B-FP8 ran mostly on RTX PRO 6000 and H100 GPUs. Due to budget constraints, we served Claude Opus 5.5 through Claude
subagents, each call with a clean context.

Choosing the tools.

In our experiments, the tools were set by the task set. Afterwards, we asked GPT-6.1-Sol at low effort to choose between
the two tool sets with the following prompt:

Here is a task. <task> ... </task> Do not do the task. Decide which kind of task it is and answer with exactly one
word: coding if it asks you to change code in a software repository, or search if it asks you to find the answer to a
question.

For all 869 tasks and questions, it chose the tools that our experiments used. The question costs about
$0.0005 per task, under one point of relative cost on every task set, which our cost figures leave out.

Reruns and later runs.

Claude Code compacts a long conversation to keep its context short. An episode of the frontier model that compacted
and then failed was rerun with its full 1M-token context, in every system alike. Search questions that failed for
infrastructure reasons (8 of 694) were rerun with identical settings, and no question was rerun because of its answer.
Runs added after our main experiments, namely the reruns after compaction and the runs of

both SLMs on a shared vLLM cluster with the same weights, behind a proxy that applies the same request conversion as

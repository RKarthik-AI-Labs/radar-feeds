Abstract

TRIBE v2 
(
d’Ascoli et al., 2026
)
 predicts fMRI responses across the cortical surface from
video, audio and text. Its authors validated it with category-selective localizers (faces,
places, bodies, words) and language contrasts, but not with the classic topographic
organization of sensory cortex: retinotopy, tonotopy, and motion selectivity in area MT+.
There is a clear architectural reason to expect retinotopy to fail: TRIBE’s video features
are averaged over space before the response head, which should discard visual-field
position. I present mapping stimuli, a fitting pipeline adapted from standard fMRI mapping
analyses, and a stand-in model with known ground truth that serves as a positive and
negative control, and apply them to TRIBE v2’s predictions, comparing the fitted maps with
human atlases using spin tests. TRIBE v2 shows a sharp split. There is no contralateral
hemifield response and no polar-angle map, robustly across stimulus controls. There is no
tonotopic gradient in primary auditory cortex: best frequencies span an interquartile range
of only 0.14 octaves across core and belt, and the null persists with loudness-equalized
tones. An eccentricity-like gradient appears only when the ring geometry changes, consistent
with global image statistics rather than a positional code. In contrast, motion selectivity
is strong and precisely localized: 22% of the 1% most motion-selective vertices fall in
MT+, which covers 0.42% of cortex. In-silico experiments that depend on feature identity
appear viable with TRIBE v2, while those that depend on visual-field or frequency position
are not supported by the current model.

1 
Introduction

TRIBE v2 
(
d’Ascoli et al., 2026
)
 is a foundation model trained to predict voxel/vertex-wise
fMRI responses on the fsaverage5 cortical surface (20,484 vertices) from naturalistic
video, audio and text input. Its original evaluation covered category-selective visual
localizers (faces 
 FFA, places 
 PPA, bodies 
 EBA, words 
 VWFA) and
language contrasts, where it performed well. It did not evaluate three of the
best-replicated organizational principles in sensory neuroscience: retinotopy in early
visual cortex, tonotopy in primary auditory cortex, and motion-selective responses in area
MT+.

There is a specific architectural reason to expect retinotopy to fail: the paper’s own
methods describe averaging V-JEPA 2 patch tokens over space before they reach the response
head, which by construction removes the positional information a polar-angle or
eccentricity map requires. No prior work has quantified whether this prediction holds, or
whether motion selectivity (which does not depend on position, so should survive spatial
averaging) and tonotopy (whose audio features are not pooled in the same way) fare
differently.

descending logarithmic tone sweeps spanning 175 Hz–5.6 kHz in 10 six-semitone bands
(tonotopy). Full stimulus parameters are in 
config.yaml
 of the project
repository.

2.2 
Model and inference

I ran the released

 checkpoint (video encoder: V-JEPA 2 giant;
audio encoder: Wav2Vec-BERT 2.0) on each stimulus, bypassing TRIBE’s text/transcription path
entirely since no stimulus contains speech. Predictions were produced per-vertex at 1 Hz
(TR = 1 s), with TRIBE’s built-in 5-second hemodynamic-lag correction already applied.

2.4 
Reference maps and null models

Fitted maps were compared against published human atlases on the same fsaverage5 mesh: the
Benson retinotopy template for polar angle, eccentricity and V1–V3 boundaries

(
Benson et al., 2014
; 
Benson and Winawer, 2018
)
, the Glasser HCP-MMP parcellation for MT and
MST 
(
Glasser et al., 2016
)
, and the posteromedial-to-anterolateral axis of A1 (the
best-replicated human tonotopic gradient) for tonotopy 
(
Saenz and Langers, 2014
; 
Moerel et al., 2014
)
. Because TRIBE is deterministic, there is no trial-to-trial sampling
noise to test against; instead I ask whether a map’s spatial layout is aligned with known
anatomy, using a spin test that rotates each map on the sphere while preserving its spatial
autocorrelation 
(
Alexander-Bloch et al., 2018
)
, and, for tonotopy, a stimulus-label
permutation that shuffles which frequency band is which.

2.5 
Positive/negative control

Before running the real model, I validated the entire stimulus-generation, fitting and
evaluation pipeline against a stand-in model with two configurable response modes: a

retinotopic+tonotopic
 mode that plants known pRFs (from the Benson template) and an
A1 frequency gradient, and a 
pooled+flat
 mode where visual vertices see only global
image statistics (stimulated area, wedge orientation) and auditory vertices see only
sound on/off – i.e., exactly what TRIBE’s architecture is expected to produce if H1 and H4
(null) hold. This lets me confirm the pipeline recovers a map when one is planted, and
correctly reports its absence when it is not.

3.2 
Tonotopy: TRIBE v2 predicts no tonotopic gradient

I ran the real TRIBE v2 model on the ascending and descending tone-sweep stimuli. Auditory
cortex responds robustly to the tones: every core and belt vertex is well fit (T0: 100%
clear the fit-quality threshold), and A1 is strongly suppressed during silence
(
). However, I find no evidence of a tonotopic map: the spread of best
frequencies across core and belt is only 0.141 octaves (T2), against 2.0 octaves in the
planted-map stand-in control, and there is no gradient along A1 (T1: Spearman

; spin 
; label-permutation 
). The posteromedial and
anterolateral halves of A1 show near-identical band-by-band response profiles
(Figure

audio stimuli alone (T2

six-stimulus run reported here – the numbers are essentially unchanged. Predicted responses
peak 2–3 s later than TRIBE’s built-in 5-second hemodynamic-lag correction assumes, which I
accommodate with the widened lag search described in Methods.

H4 verdict: no tonotopic gradient.
 H4 was stated as an open question; the answer
is that TRIBE v2 predicts none. Because my main tones are equal in amplitude rather than in

headline finding does not depend on this, since every vertex is exposed to identical sounds,
and the loudness-equalized control (Section

still absent under equal loudness. I do not interpret the specific preferred frequency
(

5 
Discussion

Across all four hypotheses, TRIBE v2 shows a consistent pattern: codes that depend on

where
 something is (visual-field position, tonotopic frequency position) are absent,
while a code that depends on 
what kind of feature
 is present without requiring
position (motion) survives and is correctly localized. H1 (retinotopy) is confirmed: as the
architecture predicts, the spatially averaged video stream carries no usable positional code.
H4 (tonotopy), stated as an open question, resolves as absent – more surprisingly, since the
audio pathway has no equivalent spatial-pooling step. The tonotopic null therefore cannot be
explained by the video pathway’s pooling. It may reflect what the model’s audio training data
(mostly speech and film sound) taught it to represent, the audio encoder’s features, or how
the response head aggregates sequence-level audio features; this audit does not distinguish
between these. H3 (motion) is confirmed cleanly: MT+ selectivity
is both strong and anatomically precise (M2), showing that TRIBE v2 can support
in-silico experiments that depend on 
feature identity
 even in domains where
position-dependent experiments fail.

The one result that resists a clean story is R4 (Section 
3.3
):
wedge-locked periodic power is present (0.744, above the position-coding threshold) despite
the complete absence of a coherent polar-angle map (R2) or contralaterality effect (R1). I
see two readings. First, R4 may simply be a weaker, noisier signal than R1–R3: it requires
only that 
some
 wedge-synchronized response exists somewhere in V1–V3, not that it be
spatially organized, so it is a much lower bar to clear and may reflect overall
stimulus-locked arousal/attention-like modulation rather than a retinotopic code. Second, it
leaves open the possibility that some coarse spatial information survives averaging in a form
the circular-correlation metric (R2) is not sensitive to – for instance, information about
stimulated area or aggregate screen-side energy that correlates with wedge phase without
reconstructing a true angular map. Distinguishing these two readings is a natural next step
and does not change the paper’s central claim, since neither reading implies a usable
positional (polar-angle or eccentricity) code.

R3 (eccentricity) shows a related pattern and is, if anything, a cleaner case of the same
phenomenon: it is a non-significant, slightly negative null at the default ring geometry, but
becomes a significant, template-aligned correlation under every control that regenerates the
ring with different geometry (area-matched, 5°, 20°; Section

4
). Because its sign and significance depend on how the ring is rendered,
and holding stimulated area constant does not remove it, R3 does not behave like a stable
positional code. I read it as consistent with the H2 prediction – an eccentricity-
like

gradient carried by global image statistics that covary with ring geometry – rather than
evidence that TRIBE v2 retains true positional information, although these controls do not
identify the statistic responsible, and a weak genuine eccentricity signal cannot be fully
excluded. This matters for practitioners: an apparent eccentricity gradient in TRIBE v2’s
predictions is not by itself evidence of a retinotopic code, and should be checked across
several stimulus geometries before being interpreted.

For practitioners using TRIBE v2 for in-silico neuroscience, the practical implication is
direct: experiments that depend on visual-field or tonotopic position (e.g., contralateral
hemifield localizers, pRF mapping, frequency-selective auditory designs) should not be
trusted with this model, while experiments that depend on category- or feature-level
selectivity (as in the original paper’s face/place/body/word localizers, and as I
demonstrate for motion) remain viable. For vision, the missing positional code is most
likely architectural: the video features are averaged over space before the response head,
so further training of the current design would not be expected to restore it, and a TRIBE
version aiming to support position-based in-silico experiments would likely need to preserve
spatial structure through to the response head. For audition the cause remains open, as
discussed above. Ablations that modify the pooling step or the audio features are the
natural way to test these explanations directly.

6 
Limitations

•

Resolution.
 fsaverage5 vertices are spaced approximately 4 mm apart; A1
comprises only about 45 vertices in total (22 left, 23 right) at this resolution,
limiting sensitivity to fine-grained tonotopic structure.

•

Average subject.
 TRIBE predicts a group-average subject’s response, so
fitted maps are necessarily compared against group-level templates rather than
individual-subject maps.

•

Visual angle is undefined for TRIBE.
 I define the stimulus frame to span

max_ecc_deg
 degrees (10 by default). The contralaterality and polar-angle tests
(R1, R2) are unaffected by this choice, but the eccentricity correlation (R3) is not
(Section 
4
), which is one reason R3 is treated as unresolved.

•

Mechanisms are inferred, not tested.
 The architectural explanation for the
visual null comes from the published model description; I did not modify the model to
test it.

•

Tonotopic axis.
 The orientation of tonotopic gradients relative to
Heschl’s gyrus remains debated in the human-neuroimaging literature; T1 specifically
tests the posteromedial-high/anterolateral-low pattern, while T2 (best-frequency spread)
is direction-free.

Abstract

Multimodal (
e.g. 
vision–language, in this work) attribution aims to interpret the models without access to ground-truth explanatory supervision. Existing multimodal attribution methods typically rely on paired modalities as proxy supervision, implicitly assuming that image–text pairs are semantically aligned. Information bottleneck (IB)–based attribution methods follow this general paradigm and instantiate it through a global sufficiency–compression objective. In realistic multimodal datasets, however, this assumption is frequently violated due to partial semantic misalignment and unreliable cross-modal correspondences. Through theoretical analysis, we show that, under such misalignment, information-bottleneck-based attribution objectives exhibit a structural limitation: by assuming equal reliability across all proxy supervision signals, they induce posterior over-contraction and forced explanations. To address this issue, we propose TIB, a sample-wise 
T
empered 
I
nformation 
B
ottleneck framework that adaptively modulates attribution strength according to cross-modal reliability, without assuming semantic alignment. Extensive experiments on controlled benchmarks and large-scale datasets show the effectiveness of the proposed approach, while also validating the identified failure mode. Project page: 
https://qiming-huang.github.io/TIB/
.

Introduction

Recent advances in multimodal learning, exemplified by large-scale vision–language models such as CLIP 
[
21
]
, have enabled powerful joint representations across diverse modalities and achieved strong performance on tasks such as image–text retrieval and visual question answering. As these models increasingly serve as general-purpose foundations, it becomes essential to understand which modalities and components contribute to their predictions.

This growing demand has motivated increasing interest in multimodal attribution, which aims to explain model predictions by identifying salient elements across different modalities. Unlike unimodal settings—where unimodal attribution can be evaluated against well-defined supervision signals such as class labels for images or next-token prediction for language models—multimodal attribution lacks explicit ground-truth explanations. Consequently, existing approaches typically operate on paired multimodal samples and implicitly assume cross-modal consistency, treating one modality as a proxy ground truth to attribute or explain the other 
[
26
, 
8
, 
20
, 
32
, 
37
]
.

However, this assumption of cross-modal consistency is inherently fragile. As shown in Fig. 
1
, in most real scenarios, multimodal data, paired modalities may be missed, replaced, or only weakly correlated. Empirical studies on large-scale video–text datasets report that over 50% of purportedly aligned clip–caption pairs are in fact misaligned 
[
16
]
. Such conditions are widely studied in multimodal learning and formally defined as 
multimodal misalignment
 
[
5
, 
3
]
. While prior work has extensively examined how misalignment affects representation learning, either as detrimental noise 
[
14
, 
30
]
 or as a form of regularization 
[
33
, 
17
, 
7
]
, its implications for multimodal attribution remain largely underexplored. From the perspective of attribution, this raises a fundamental question: 
Does multimodal misalignment systematically affect the stability and reliability of multimodal attribution?

In light of this, we aim to study multimodal attribution through an information-theoretic lens. In detail, we analyse the attribution objectives derived from the information bottleneck (IB) principle under a unified misalignment data generation assumption (see Assump. 
3.1
). Under this assumption, multimodal pairs are drawn from a mixture of aligned and misaligned components. We then characterise IB-style attribution objectives as enforcing representational sufficiency with respect to the entire data mixture, without distinguishing aligned from misaligned samples. However, the mutual information between modalities conditioned on misalignment is generally non-zero, despite the absence of genuine semantic correspondence. Consequently, the sufficiency constraint inevitably allocates representation capacity to such spurious cross-modal dependencies, diverting explanatory capacity toward noise and leading to increasingly concentrated and unstable attribution maps.

Through experimental analysis, we reveal that this failure mode is not an artefact of optimisation or hyperparameter choices, but a structural limitation of standard homoscedastic bottleneck formulations. In particular, homoscedastic information bottlenecks impose a trade-off between sufficiency and compression across all samples, treating aligned and misaligned pairs as equally reliable under the unified data generation assumption. As a result, attribution objectives cannot adapt to heterogeneity in cross-modal reliability. Besides, explanatory learning remains driven by uniform fitting pressure even when semantic sufficiency becomes statistically degenerate. In contrast, we show that stable multimodal attribution under misalignment becomes attainable by adopting heteroscedastic information bottlenecks, named as Tempered Information Bottleneck (TIB), which allows effective information capacity to vary across samples. This flexibility enables the attribution objective to suppress contributions from misaligned components within samples, while preserving explanatory capacity for their reliably aligned semantic content.

Beyond theoretical analysis, we empirically validate our approach on a synthetic benchmark that allows precise control over the structure and severity of misalignment, enabling systematic evaluation of attribution behaviour. Our contributions are summarised as follows:

•

We study multimodal attribution from an information bottleneck (IB) perspective under a unified misalignment data generation assumption, and show that classical IB objectives are contaminated by multimodal misalignment pressure.

•

We propose a heteroscedastic bottleneck, TIB, that enables more robust multimodal attribution by adaptively suppressing misaligned components while preserving reliably aligned semantic content.

•

We validate the proposed approach on both synthetic and real-world multimodal datasets, including a controllable misalignment benchmark for systematic evaluation.

Related Work

Unimodal Interpretability Methods.

Traditional interpretability methods were primarily developed for unimodal deep learning models and face significant limitations when applied to multimodal architectures. Early gradient-based approaches, such as Saliency Maps, compute input-output gradients to highlight important regions in the input. However, they are highly sensitive to noise and often produce low-resolution or unstable explanations. Grad-CAM 
[
26
]
 improves upon this by using gradients of class-specific activation maps in convolutional layers, resulting in more focused and human-interpretable heatmaps. LIME 
[
22
]
 adopts a black-box approach by perturbing the input and fitting a local surrogate model, offering model-agnostic explanations. Despite its generality, LIME may fail on complex models due to its reliance on local linearity assumptions. More recent attribution methods, such as AGI 
[
19
]
 and MFABA 
[
36
]
, utilise adversarial perturbations to generate more robust saliency maps. These approaches satisfy formal interpretability criteria like Sensitivity and Implementation Invariance 
[
29
]
, offering theoretical guarantees.

Nevertheless, most of these methods are designed for unimodal tasks and require access to downstream task labels or internal gradients, making them less suitable for large-scale vision-language models. Attempts to extend such methods to the multimodal setting—such as CLIP—face challenges due to architectural differences and a lack of task-specific supervision. As shown in our experiments, these methods often fail to provide meaningful explanations when applied directly to multimodal models.

Multimodal Interpretability Methods

CLIP 
[
21
]
 learns joint image-text representations by training separate encoders on large-scale image-text pairs, aligning the two modalities in a shared embedding space. This design enables zero-shot transfer, allowing the model to perform various tasks based solely on natural language prompts, without requiring task-specific annotations. In related open-world settings, semantic ambiguity complicates the evaluation of open-vocabulary segmentation 
[
10
]
. However, the complexity of multimodal reasoning raises the need for interpretability to ensure that the model’s predictions are based on semantically meaningful features. Improvements in zero-shot action recognition through atypical video data 
[
28
]
 further highlight the potential of multimodal interpretability to reveal which visual and textual cues support generalisation to unseen actions. Investigating CLIP’s interpretability is thus crucial to determine whether it captures genuine vision-language associations or exploits spurious correlations.

A range of methods have been proposed to interpret vision-language pre-trained models, yet many introduce limitations in fidelity, scalability, or practicality. M2IB 
[
32
]
 applies a multimodal information bottleneck to filter irrelevant features, but increases architectural complexity. COCOA 
[
13
]
 modifies Integrated Gradients with contrastive learning, requiring additional positive and negative samples that may introduce irrelevant context. TEXTSPAN 
[
6
]
 relies on constructing sample-specific sets or selecting neighbours in embedding space, making it dependent on external data and less generalizable. LICO 
[
12
]
 retrains models to maintain cross-modal alignment, but its explanations apply to the altered model, not the original one, and are affected by training randomness. FALCON 
[
11
]
 explains individual features via highly activating examples, but lacks per-instance interpretability. PIDReg 
[
15
]
, a PID-based method, seeks to improve interpretability by decomposing information across modalities on a multimodal regression task. FIxLIP 
[
2
]
 introduces faithful interaction explanations of CLIP and SigLIP models, offering a game-theoretic perspective on interpreting image–text similarity.

Recent work 
[
1
]
 provides a comprehensive review of multimodal attribution methods, but largely focuses on fixed paired settings and does not account for multimodal misalignment arising from the data generation process itself. In practice, many existing interpretability methods for vision–language models rely on strong assumptions, such as clean image–text alignment, access to contrastive examples, or task-specific supervision, and often require additional sampling, surrogate retraining, or architectural modifications, which limit their robustness and practicality. As a result, when image–text pairs are noisy or mismatched, these methods may still produce forced explanations, undermining the reliability of the interpretability results, as illustrated in Fig. 
1
. By contrast, our method does not assume reliable modality alignment or strict pairwise correspondence; instead, it employs a soft alignment mechanism that adapts to varying degrees of semantic consistency, yielding more effective and credible multimodal attribution under misalignment.

Having identified that the failure of the classical IB objective originates from the decomposition above, we next seek a surrogate mechanism that can play a role analogous to the unobserved latent state 
. Since

To this end, we first reformulate the objective itself rather than the architecture, replacing uniform fitting pressure with a sample-wise tempered objective in which each sample is weighted according to its estimated reliability. Sec. 
5.2
: Do real-world multimodal datasets conform to our assumption?

Largely yes.
 The similarity-distribution evidence and appendix analyses support our heterogeneous-mixture assumption; at the same time, we do not claim that it fully captures the complexity of real-world data, which may involve even richer heterogeneity.

Sec. 
5.3
: Why does the classical homoscedastic IB objective fail under multimodal misalignment?

Because
 uniform fitting pressure forces the model to fit both aligned and misaligned pairs indiscriminately, leading to unstable and over-confident attribution behaviour. 
Why can TIB avoid this failure?
 TIB may mitigate it by providing a more informative sample-wise reliability signal than the imperfect reliability ordering induced by homoscedastic weighting, allowing it to better identify lower-risk samples and achieve improved selective-risk behaviour.

This failure can be traced to incorrect reliability ordering induced by homoscedastic weighting.
 As shown in Fig. 
6
, we evaluate different reliability-ranking strategies using a risk–coverage curve. Samples are first ordered from most to least reliable under each strategy. We then compute the average attribution risk over the retained prefix of this ordering as the coverage increases from 0 to 1. Lower curves indicate better reliability ordering because they retain lower-risk samples at smaller coverage. Thus, at coverage 
, the plotted value corresponds to the mean attribution risk of the top 20% of selected samples.

The compared ranking strategies have the following meanings. 
Oracle
 uses the ground-truth risk itself as the ranking score and therefore represents the ideal upper bound on selection quality. 
TIB
 ranks samples by the learned uncertainty score and corresponds to our method. 
Fixed-type Weight
 ranks samples using coarse-grained group averages defined by caption type and perturbation slot, serving as a structured baseline. 
Random
 applies a random ordering and therefore represents a baseline with no reliable selection ability. 
Inverted Reliability
 reverses the reliability ranking induced by TIB and serves as a counterexample or lower-bound reference. We observe that TIB consistently approaches the oracle ordering and clearly outperforms the coarse-grained and random baselines, whereas inverted reliability leads to substantially higher selective risk. This confirms that attribution failure under misalignment is primarily driven by incorrect reliability ordering: once unreliable samples are down-weighted or deferred, attribution quality is substantially improved.

6 
Conclusion

In this paper, we investigated multimodal attribution from an information bottleneck (IB) perspective under cross-modal misalignment, revealing how violations of conventional alignment assumptions introduce spurious cross-modal dependencies into standard IB objectives. Such contamination can distort the optimisation process, leading to over-confident and unstable attribution explanations that fail to reflect genuine image–text correspondence. To address this limitation, we propose a sample-wise heteroscedastic information bottleneck that explicitly models predictive uncertainty and adaptively adjusts fitting strength according to cross-modal reliability. By reducing the influence of unreliable image–text pairs, TIB mitigates the effects of misalignment on attribution optimisation. Our theoretical analysis and extensive experiments on synthetic benchmarks and large-scale real-world datasets showed that the proposed TIB effectively suppresses fitting to misaligned image–text pairs. These findings highlighted the importance of incorporating data-dependent uncertainty into information bottleneck objectives for reliable multimodal interpretability.

Appendix Contents

Appendix A 
Limitation

Our analysis relies on a simplified mixture-based misalignment model that distinguishes only between aligned and misaligned components. While this abstraction is sufficient to reveal the contamination mechanism in IB-style attribution, real-world multimodal misalignment can be more complex (
e.g. 
partial, hierarchical, or temporal misalignment), which is not explicitly modelled here. Nevertheless, we believe this simplified formulation captures the essential source of misalignment and is sufficient to highlight the non-negligible impact of misaligned data on multimodal attribution.

Appendix B 
Broader impacts

Many existing multimodal attribution methods implicitly assume that paired modalities in real-world datasets are well aligned and can serve as a reliable proxy supervision for explanation. In practice, however, large-scale multimodal data are often collected with minimal filtering and contain substantial semantic misalignment. Under such conditions, attribution methods may produce over-confident or forced explanations driven by spurious cross-modal correlations, reducing the reliability and stability of interpretability results and limiting their practical usefulness.

This work highlights the potential risks of applying attribution methods under unrealistic alignment assumptions and emphasises the importance of accounting for data quality and uncertainty when interpreting multimodal models. The proposed analysis operates as a post-hoc interpretability framework and does not introduce new predictive capabilities or application domains. We do not anticipate specific societal risks arising directly from this work beyond those already associated with the use of large-scale multimodal models.

C.2 
Evaluation metrics for real-world multimodal attribution

We evaluate real-world multimodal attribution using three groups of metrics: ROAR, Drop/Incr., and grounding. For ROAR, we follow the evaluation protocol of M2IB with a unified percentile-based threshold across modalities. Specifically, we remove the top 25% most important features, corresponding to the 75th percentile of the attribution scores. For images, the selected pixels are replaced with the channel mean, whereas for text, the selected tokens are replaced with the blank token. The corrupted sample is then evaluated by the relative similarity drop between the original and corrupted image–text similarities, where a larger drop indicates that the removed features are more important. For ROAR retraining, we fine-tune the CLIP backbone rather than the MLP head, using an 80/20 train–validation split for 50 epochs with a learning rate of 
.

For Drop and Incr., we follow the definitions introduced in Grad-CAM++ and adopt the same percentile-based thresholding strategy as above. Features are perturbed one modality at a time. Drop measures how much the model confidence decreases after removing regions considered unimportant, where lower values indicate better attribution quality. Incr. measures the proportion of samples for which restricting the input to only the explained regions increases the model confidence, where higher values are preferred.

For grounding, we compute the intersection-over-union (IoU) between the binarised attribution map and the ground-truth mask. The attribution map is first normalised to 
 and then binarised with a threshold of 0.5, which provides a balanced decision boundary between salient and non-salient regions. In addition, the insertion and deletion curves are computed following the official implementation of Grad-ECLIP

Appendix F 
Synthetic misalignment data generation

Given an image and its original caption, we first extract a set of semantic components using rule-based matching over predefined vocabularies, including objects, entities, actions, attributes, and compositional phrases. Based on these extracted components, we generate two types of textual misalignment. 
SELECTIVE BIAS
 is constructed by randomly removing one to three semantic components with a weighted preference toward contextual information, resulting in captions that are fluent but semantically incomplete. 
PERTURBATION BIAS
 is constructed by replacing a single semantic component with another value from the same semantic category or a synonym, producing captions that remain grammatical but contain incorrect semantics. This procedure yields controlled and interpretable image–text misalignment without altering the visual content. The pseudocode is provided in Algorithm

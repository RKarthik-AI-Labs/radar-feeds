Can AI Judge You? An Empirical Audit of

Deployment Readiness in LLM-assisted Hiring Systems

Abstract

Delegating resume screening to large language models (LLMs) requires organizations to establish whether they can distinguish candidates by relevant qualifications while allowing only authorized users to change screening rules or approve exceptions. To this end, we develop a system architecture that separates candidate evidence, evaluation, permission controls, and assessment record, and empirically examine two complementary dimensions of its deployment readiness. First, we assess its operational readiness on four LLMs using a controlled experiment on 210 resumes across 14 job roles and six decision interfaces. Second, we assess its compliance readiness by testing how it handles unauthorized user requests and instructions embedded in resumes. We find that operational performance depends on the choice of evaluation interface: scalar scores generally distinguish qualification changes better than simple pass/fail judgments, while comparative judgments are sensitive to presentation order. Explicit permission rules and separation of resume content from instructions reduce unauthorized compliance, to a varying degree of effectiveness across LLMs and usage contexts. Finally, we find that LLM evaluators often diverge between their decisions and offered justifications. Going beyond simply evaluating LLMs for hiring tasks, our framework supports an audit of a fully configured resume evaluation system to enable its deployment decision.

Keywords:
 Large Language Models, AI Governance, Algorithmic Hiring, Responsible AI, Organizational Decision Systems, Human-AI Delegation

1  Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have moved quickly from experimentation to routine organizational use. In the 2025 survey by 
McKinsey & Company (2025)
, 88% of the respondents reported regular use of AI in at least one business function, up from 78% a year earlier, and 62% reported that their organizations were at least experimenting with AI agents. Estimates of task exposure suggest that LLMs can affect a substantial share of work across occupations 
(
Eloundou et al., 2024
)
, and field evidence documents productivity gains where they are embedded in service workflows 
(
Brynjolfsson et al., 2025
)
. A growing share of these deployments assigns the model an evaluative role. LLMs review contracts against organizational guidelines, extract and reconcile supplier invoices, summarize incident histories for IT service management, and screen applicants against stated job requirements. In each case, the organization delegates a judgment on which it will act 
(
Baird and Maruping, 2021
; 
Berente et al., 2021
)
.

Delegating judgment involves assigning a task together with responsibilities and limits on authority 
(
Berente et al., 2021
)
. A delegate is expected to perform the assigned task competently and to act within the authority granted. Delegating to an LLM-based evaluator carries the same expectations, but creates a tension between the interpretive flexibility that makes LLMs useful and the organizational control needed to govern their judgments. Evaluating varied, unstructured evidence requires interpretation beyond matching a fixed set of terms. Yet the evaluator’s latitude to interpret evidence must preserve the distinction between applying a decision rule and changing it. An organization may delegate these responsibilities to different parties. Assessing deployment readiness of the evaluator, therefore, requires evidence that it can perform its assigned task while respecting that allocation of authority.

Information systems make organizational rules operative in everyday work 
(
Lessig, 1999
; 
Scott, 2003
)
, including through systems that evaluate conduct and shape decisions 
(
Yeung, 2018
)
. In an LLM-based evaluator, both these rules and the material to be evaluated may be expressed in natural language. The model must interpret their content while distinguishing the authority of their sources. Research on organizational guardrails emphasizes specifying acceptable behaviour, monitoring deviations, and enabling intervention 
(
Grisold et al., 2025
)
; fair-AI design similarly spans technical and organizational choices 
(
Rai et al., 2026
)
. These arrangements require evidence that the configured evaluator responds to relevant information and permitted instructions while resisting influences that fall outside its assigned role.

Hiring is a concrete setting for examining this problem. Applicant tracking systems collect applications, construct candidate profiles, and coordinate progression through screening stages 
(
Workday, n.d.
)
. LLM-based tools extend these functions by extracting skills and employment histories for matching 
(
Greenhouse, 2026
)
 and assessing candidate evidence against recruiter-approved qualifications 
(
Gu et al., 2025
)
. A human recruiter entrusted with screening applications is expected to interpret qualification evidence, apply the organization’s job requirements and screening rules, and respect the permissions governing exceptions. An LLM assigned this role must fulfill the same responsibilities. Even when recruiters retain formal authority over hiring decisions 
(
Jose, 2026
)
, the screening system can influence which applications receive attention and how candidates are characterized before human review.

The distinction between interpreting evidence and changing a rule is consequential here. Candidates may describe similar qualifications through different accounts of education, experience, and skills. Recognizing that these accounts satisfy a requirement is an evaluation task; waiving the requirement is an exercise of authority. The evaluator must also distinguish relevant evidence from cues that should not affect the judgment. Correspondence studies have documented discrimination in screening in the United States 
(
Bertrand and Mullainathan, 2004
)
 and in Indian private-sector hiring 
(
Thorat and Attewell, 2007
; 
Banerjee et al., 2009
)
. Human performance consequently provides no automatic guarantee of appropriate evaluation. For an LLM-based screener, the requirement is to assess job-relevant evidence consistently under the organization’s screening rules. Establishing whether a particular configuration does so gives empirical content to otherwise abstract governance requirements 
(
Seidel et al., 2025
)
.

Hiring also makes the allocation of authority explicit. An ordinary recruiter may be permitted to only assess a candidate, while a Human Resource (HR) administrator may additionally waive a requirement or authorize an exception. Research linking manager exceptions to test-based recommendations with shorter employee tenure illustrates why organizations must deliberate over such rights 
(
Hoffman et al., 2018
)
. Candidate materials create another source of attempted intervention: applicants can embed instructions addressed to AI screeners in their resumes 
(
The New York Times, 2025
)
. A screening system must distinguish such (unauthorized) instructions from qualification evidence, while remaining responsive to interventions that the organization permits.

We therefore ask:

How can organizations configure and assess LLM-based evaluators to interpret candidate evidence faithfully while preserving the authority governing their judgments?

To address this question, we distinguish two complementary dimensions of deployment readiness for an LLM-based evaluator. 
Operational readiness
 concerns whether the evaluator assesses candidate evidence appropriately against the job requirements and screening rules. 
Compliance readiness
 concerns whether it respects the permissions governing intervention and reports the resulting assessment consistently with the evidence, screening rules, and any authorized exception. These requirements concern different aspects of faithful delegation and require complementary evidence: competence in evaluating resumes does not establish that the system will resist unauthorized intervention, and adherence to permissions does not establish that its qualification judgments are sound.

We examine these requirements through two sets of configuration choices. First, the organization specifies a 
decision interface
: whether the evaluator returns a shortlist recommendation, a score, qualification-level assessments, or a comparison among candidates. This choice determines the form of judgment requested from the model and, together with the surrounding workflow, how that judgment informs selection. Operational readiness therefore requires examining whether the chosen interface captures relevant qualification differences without introducing sensitivity to irrelevant cues. Second, the organization specifies 
authority and provenance controls
, which communicate who may intervene and distinguish governing instructions from candidate-supplied evidence. Compliance readiness requires examining whether these controls preserve permitted interventions while resisting unauthorized influence.

We thus treat readiness as a property of a configured evaluation system—comprising screening rules, prompts, decision interfaces, provenance handling, and permissions—rather than of the underlying LLM alone. We position this approach relative to existing work on LLMs in hiring in Section 
2
 and develop an audit within a reference architecture for the wider screening system. The audit provides evidence on operational and compliance requirements that are necessary for deployment. This yields two research questions:

RQ1 (Operational Readiness).
 How do decision interfaces affect a configured LLM evaluator’s responsiveness to job-relevant qualification evidence and its invariance to irrelevant cues, including names and presentation order?

RQ2 (Compliance Readiness).
 How do authority and provenance controls affect a configured LLM evaluator’s responsiveness to authorized interventions, resistance to unauthorized interventions, and consistency of reported assessments with evidence and permissions?

We address both questions through a controlled resume screening experiment comprising 210 resumes, 14 job descriptions spanning information technology, legal services, healthcare operations, medicine, and education, and four LLMs. We contribute a diagnostic framework for auditing delegated LLM evaluation that links organizational responsibilities to distinct tests of resume assessment, permitted interventions and consistency of assessments.

Each of the questions above corresponds to a specific mechanism the decision system aims to reliably automate. RQ1 concerns how a resume is deemed fit to move on to the next stage in a hiring pipeline. A human performing this task is expected to be sensitive enough to rate a resume higher (or lower) depending on whether it contains job-relevant qualifications in higher levels and amounts, and would not be sensitive to information irrelevant to the job description. We construct our corpus of resumes to evaluate the extent to which an LLM can faithfully emulate this behaviour. Each resume contains a known pattern of required and preferred qualification evidence. Demographic cues are varied separately, while the qualification evidence is held fixed. Fifteen domain experts reviewed the job descriptions and paired resume variants to check the suitability of the hiring criteria and the intended order of qualifications. We evaluate six decision interfaces on these resumes: binary decisions, ordinal judgments, scalar scores, qualification-level rubrics, pairwise comparisons, and listwise rankings. Our findings reveal important caveats for each scoring method. For example, in pairwise comparison, the order of presenting sample resumes in a pair significantly alters which resume the LLM selects. As a representative case of job-irrelevant sensitivity check, we consider name-based demographic bias measurement based on race and ethnicity, as well as Indian surnames associated with constitutionally protected castes, and find little or no bias in any of the LLMs we tested.

To address RQ2, we measure whether an LLM-based evaluator complies with decision override requests 
only when
 they come from authorized parties, and whether the reasons given by the system behind such decisions remain faithful in the face of such nonstandard requests. Our findings suggest that without architecture-level policy enforcement mechanisms, LLMs pose a considerable risk of being exploited by malicious hiring principals and candidates to produce decisions in their favour. Effectively delimiting user authentication and compliance rules, evaluation policy, and resume text in the input prompt to the LLM inside an evaluation system can reduce this risk from 
65% to 1% for override requests from unauthorised insiders, and from 
96% to below 6% for requests embedded in the resume body. Beyond influencing decisions, we find that successful attacks can introduce inconsistencies between the returned decision, qualification assessments, and stated reasons. Such inconsistencies can also occur when the final decision remains unchanged.

2  Relevant literature

2.1  AI Adoption in Organizations

Information systems research has theorized how organizations delegate to and govern AI. Agentic IS artifacts reallocate roles and responsibilities between humans and machines 
(
Baird and Maruping, 2021
)
, and managing AI involves interdependent choices about autonomy, learning, and inscrutability 
(
Berente et al., 2021
)
. Studies of AI in expert work show that model
development and domain expertise are interdependent

(
van den Broek et al., 2021
)
, that the ground-truth used to evaluate
AI tools can misrepresent what experts know 
(
Lebovitz et al., 2021
)
,
and that working with AI can raise individual accuracy while eroding the
diversity of human judgment 
(
Fügener et al., 2021
)
. Research on fairness in
human-ML augmentation shows that a human in the loop is not a uniform
safeguard 
(
Teodorescu et al., 2021
)
, and experimental evidence from
resume screening shows that biased LLM recommendations alter the decisions of
the people who review them 
(
Wilson and others, 2025
)
. This explains why governance must attend to the configured sociotechnical arrangement rather than algorithm alone, but it offers few instruments for verifying that a particular LLM configuration behaves as its governance design intends. Our audit supplies such an instrument for the two channels of decision influence.

2.2  Use of LLMs in Hiring

A sizeable body of recent literature extends correspondence-study logic to algorithmic and LLM-based screening 
(
Raghavan et al., 2020
)
. Name-substitution audits
report racial and gender disparities in GPT-based resume ranking

(
Yin et al., 2024
; 
Armstrong et al., 2024
)
 and in retrieval-based
screening 
(
Wilson and Caliskan, 2024
)
, and a large experiment across several
LLMs finds higher scores for female-associated names and lower scores for
Black male-associated names at comparable qualifications

(
An et al., 2025
)
; 
Gallegos et al. (2024)
 survey bias in LLMs more
broadly. Evidence for India is thinner and mixed. Studies document caste
stereotypes in LLM outputs 
(
Vijayaraghavan et al., 2025
)
 and
representation, association, and selection biases by caste in occupational
settings, including higher shortlisting of dominant-caste names

(
Zaveri and Shah, 2025
)
, whereas controlled substitution of gender-, caste-,
and region-associated names in Indian interview transcripts detects no
significant effect 
(
Rao et al., 2025
)
. Fairness frameworks built on
Western axes of identity also transfer imperfectly to the Indian context

(
Sambasivan et al., 2021
)
. Two measurement problems complicate this
evidence. First, outcomes depend on the elicitation instrument. LLM outputs
are sensitive to prompt format and wording

(
Sclar et al., 2024
; 
Mizrahi et al., 2024
)
, comparative judgments
exhibit position bias 
(
Zheng et al., 2023
; 
Wang et al., 2024
)
, and a recent
preregistered audit across hiring, lending, and triage finds that ties and
order preferences rival the demographic effects being measured

(
Vohra and Ravikiran, 2026
)
. Second, audits frequently report representational
associations, such as which groups a model links to which occupations,
without establishing allocational consequences for decisions

(
Blodgett et al., 2020
)
. Our design addresses both problems. In the
spirit of behavioural testing 
(
Ribeiro et al., 2020
)
, it evaluates the
same qualification states across six decision interfaces and measures representational associations separately from allocational effects on scores and shortlist recommendations.

2.3  Security Vulnerabilities in LLMs

LLM applications are vulnerable to instructions that arrive through data channels, generally termed prompt injection attacks, which feature prominently in practitioner risk lists for LLM applications 
(
OWASP Foundation, 2024
)
. Such attacks include direct attacks that override a developer’s prompt 
(
Perez and Ribeiro, 2022
)
, and indirect attacks that embed instructions in user-supplied content 
(
Greshake et al., 2023
)
. Broadly, multiple benchmarking studies 
(
Liu et al., 2024
)
 and attack simulation tools 
(
Derczynski et al., 2024
)
 formalize classes of attack and defence. Proposed defences include marking the provenance of untrusted content through delimiting, datamarking, or encoding 
(
Hines et al., 2025
)
, training models to prioritize privileged instructions over others 
(
Wallace et al., 2024
)
, and external input and output guardrails 
(
Ayub and Majumdar, 2024
; 
Vijil, 2025
)
. The above body of work generally frames instruction conflicts as a contest between an attacker and a developer. Organizational deployments differ in a respect that matters for
governance: authority is graded across legitimate principals, so the same
instruction, to waive a mandatory qualification and shortlist a candidate,
is an attack when an ordinary evaluator issues it and a valid policy
exception when an authenticated administrator does. A defence that refuses all decision-changing requests prevents legitimate exceptions, whereas one that accepts them all permits unauthorized changes. We therefore evaluate attack success and authorized
compliance jointly, and treat authentication as application-level information
supplied to the model outside human-authored content.

2.4  Fidelity in LLM Reasoning

Finally, a decision is only as auditable as its stated basis. Explanations produced by LLMs need not reflect the factors driving an output 
(
Jacovi and Goldberg, 2020
; 
Turpin et al., 2023
)
, and proposals for AI auditing call for assessment of models, applications, and the governance around them 
(
Raji et al., 2020
; 
Mökander et al., 2024
)
. Where affected persons may be owed an explanation 
(
European Union, 2024
)
, the fidelity of reported qualification assessments is itself a compliance property. We therefore audit not only if an attempted manipulation changes the decision but also whether the assessments and reasons remain consistent with the resume and the permissions in force. Here, fidelity refers to this observable consistency; the audit does not establish whether an explanation faithfully represents the model’s internal computation.

3  System Design for an AI Resume Screener

An LLM-based resume screener must distinguish the responsibility to interpret candidate evidence from the authority to change screening rules or authorize exceptions. Its architecture should specify how evidence reaches the evaluator, how permissions govern interventions, and how assessments inform subsequent action. These design choices implement the allocation of responsibilities associated with delegation to agentic information systems 
(
Baird and Maruping, 2021
)
 and establish where organizational rules, monitoring, and review enter the screening process 
(
Grisold et al., 2025
; 
Rai et al., 2026
)
.

3.1  Overview of the Architecture

The reference architecture in Figure 
1
 separates the screening process into candidate intake and preparation, authority and provenance controls, an LLM-based evaluation mechanism, an assessment record, and downstream review and action. The organizational configuration specifies three elements of the delegated task: 
job requirements
 identify the qualifications sought; 
screening rules
 determine how qualification assessments inform progression; and 
permissions
 establish who may change those rules or authorize exceptions. The model, prompts, and decision interface implement this assignment within the screening workflow.

Candidate intake and preparation
 collects application materials and prepares them for evaluation. Candidate text may contain qualification evidence, identity cues, and instructions embedded by the applicant. Its role is to supply evidence for assessment; its contents have no authority to amend the screening rules. User requests enter through a separate application interface. The application authenticates the requesting user and supplies the associated permissions independently of any authority claimed in the request.

Authority and provenance controls
 communicate these distinctions to the evaluator. They identify the source and instructional authority of each input, supply the applicable permissions, and specify which interventions are permitted. For example, a recruiter may request an assessment or explanation, while a designated administrator may further authorize an exception. In our implementation, these distinctions are conveyed through prompt instructions, application-supplied permission information, and the formatting of external content. Study 2 tests whether the evaluator respects these communicated boundaries. Authentication and enforcement of permissions for downstream actions remain responsibilities of the application.

The 
evaluation mechanism
 interprets candidate evidence against the job requirements and screening rules. Its decision interface specifies the judgment requested from the model: a shortlist recommendation, an ordinal judgment, a numerical score, qualification-level assessments, or a comparison among candidates. These interfaces differ in how much of the assessment the model must combine into a single judgment and what remains available for verification. Their implications for delegated authority depend on the surrounding workflow: a binary recommendation may require human approval, while qualification-level assessments may feed an automated selection rule. Study 1 examines whether the configured mechanism responds appropriately to qualification evidence and remains invariant to irrelevant cues.

The 
assessment record
 retains the evaluator’s output and, where elicited, its qualification assessments and stated reasons, together with the screening rules and permission context needed for review. The record should distinguish an assessment of evidence from an authorized exception: waiving a qualification may change the decision without establishing that the candidate possesses it. This distinction motivates the assessment fidelity audit in Study 2, which examines whether returned assessments remain consistent with the evidence and permissions. The study evaluates these outputs rather than a complete record-management implementation.

Downstream review and action
 uses the assessment to determine progression through the hiring pipeline. The organization specifies which outputs require verification, who may correct an assessment or approve an exception, and whether progression occurs through human review or application rules. Monitoring and audit findings feed back into the organizational configuration, supporting revision of both the evaluation mechanism and its controls.

3.2  Component-level Readiness Assessment

We test two components of the above architecture under controlled conditions. The studies concern distinct functional responsibilities within the same system: Study 1 examines the evaluation mechanism, while Study 2 examines authority and provenance controls through their effects on the evaluator’s outputs. We highlight that they are not independent certification exercises, since both components operate through the configured LLM and their adequacy depends on the surrounding application.

Study 1 varies qualification evidence, decision interfaces, demographic name cues, and presentation order. Its first requirement is 
qualification sensitivity
: removing relevant evidence should not improve an evaluation, and adding relevant evidence should not worsen it. Beyond this directional requirement, the interface must distinguish qualification differences sufficiently for its intended screening purpose. Its second requirement is 
invariance to irrelevant inputs
: name substitutions and presentation order should not systematically alter judgments when the job-relevant evidence remains unchanged. A qualification-controlled resume bank and the six decision interfaces allow us to examine these requirements separately.

Study 2 holds job-relevant evidence, job requirements, and the decision interface fixed across matched comparisons, while varying requests, the permissions of their sources, and their presentation to the evaluator. Its first requirement is 
authorized compliance
: the evaluator should resist interventions outside the requesting party’s permissions while responding to those permitted by the screening rules. Testing both is necessary, as refusal of all interventions would prevent legitimate exceptions. Requests embedded in resumes provide a further test to ensure that candidate-controlled content other than relevant qualifications cannot influence evaluation. Its second requirement is 
assessment fidelity
: returned qualification assessments and reasons should remain consistent with the source evidence, applicable screening rules, and actual permissions. An authorized exception may change the decision without changing the candidate’s documented qualifications. The assessment must preserve this distinction so that subsequent review can identify whether an outcome follows from evidence or from a permitted exception. We therefore inspect the reported basis of the decision, including cases in which an attempted manipulation leaves the decision unchanged.

Overall, the two studies assess whether a configured LLM-based evaluation system implements two deployment requirements of delegated screening: appropriate evaluation of presented evidence and suitable control over interventions to the system. The upstream extraction and preprocessing of resume data, authentication, recruiter behavior, and downstream hiring outcomes are outside the scope of this paper.

4.2  Decision Interfaces

A hiring screen can judge a resume in many ways. It can assign the resume a binary pass/fail category, assign it a coarse score (e.g. out of 1–5 or 1–10), a continuous score (e.g. out of 1–100), or compare two resumes to determine the preferred one. When the screen is performed by an LLM-based evaluator, the decision interface choice translates to the degree of autonomy a firm allocates to the evaluator. For example, binary decisions give complete authority to the LLM for a shortlist decision, and ordinal or scalar scoring partially elicits the internal belief a model has on the suitability of a resume. However, rubric-based evaluations provide granular information, while still deferring to a human in the loop to take the final call on a resume based on the output of the LLM evaluator. To this end, we evaluate six forms of judgment for the same job description and resume (Table

For example, in the backend software engineer role, MiniMax-M3’s mean score across 20 LLM calls decreased from 72.0 to 59.5 after three required qualifications were removed and increased to 81.2 after three preferred qualifications were added. However, every version was shortlisted in all binary evaluations. It may also fail to correctly flag missing qualifications.

For the above reasons, we recommend that hiring teams calibrate LLM resume screeners on their specific job profiles, potentially through small pilot studies, to ensure that the final decision rule can reliably screen for job-relevant qualifications.

4.4  Name-associated Differences in Hiring Evaluations

We check for potential allocational harm in LLM evaluator decisions by measuring whether adding names to a resume alters its chance of being shortlisted by our models. We begin with the 2011 Socio-Economic Caste Census (SECC) data in India, consider the set of surnames belonging to the constitutionally protected Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (SC/ST) demographic group, and compare with a set of control surnames not belonging to the SC/ST category. We consider 80 versions of each resume, augmented with one of these surnames and 40 each of male and female-associated Indian first names, and consider scalar scores from LLM evaluators. Although we find no statistically significant associations between surname category and their shortlist status/scores, post-hoc analysis reveals that individual scores can diverge sharply. After two removals, GPT-OSS-120B scores “Pooja Nadar” (SC/ST surname) and “Pooja Duraisamy” (control surname) at 55 and 78 for the mid-level IT profile, although both initially scored 85 at baseline. For the orthopaedic-surgeon profile, DeepSeek scores “Rahul Damor” (SC/ST surname) and “Rahul Tiwari” (control surname) at 30 and 70, respectively, compared to a common baseline of 72. Differences also occur in the opposite direction: for the senior legal resume, Nemotron scores Reserved-associated “Sneha Yadav” at 78 and Other-associated “Sneha Jha” at 55, although both initially scored 88. More details, including a representational harm experiment and a parallel study of US surnames, are in Appendix 
A.7
.

The main takeaway from the above experiments when considering LLM-based screeners in an AI-augmented hiring process, the sensitivity of an LLM to a chosen decision interface, its ability to distinguish applicants based on job-relevant information, and the stability of its decisions when irrelevant information is provided are important criteria that need to be evaluated. While high-granularity scalar scores resolve qualification changes more reliably than binary or ordinal decisions, they need to be calibrated before deployment, and may not always distinguish but the effects of individual qualifications and the outcomes of comparative evaluations vary across evaluators and presentation orders. Pairwise and listwise comparisons, while broadly effective in picking stronger candidates from a pair or 4-tuple of resumes, show significant positional bias in that choice. Although we did not find overall evidence of name-associated score differences, examples show that differences may still be present for particular LLM-job profile combinations. Operational readiness therefore requires validating the chosen LLM and the decision rule 
together
 for the specific job profile.

5  Study 2: Compliance Readiness

Study 2 tests whether LLM-based resume screeners can respect organizational permissions and accurately report candidate qualifications through the lens of provenance (identifying where an input instruction comes from) and authorization (identifying who is giving an input instruction) control. For example, a recruiter may provide instructions to a resume but cannot waive qualification or demographic category requirements, a hiring manager or HR policy administrator may be allowed to authorize an exception to a requirement, while a evaluation instructions embedded in a candidate resume is permitted to do neither.

We evaluate the efficacy of a governance layer described in 
Section
 
5.1
 to ensure compliance readiness of our proposed system (
Section
 
3.1
). To this end, we test the extent to which this authorization layer can help the LLM underneath comply to incoming evaluation requests while considering who the request is coming from (
Section
 
5.2
), and candidate-side input text from evaluation instructions in its input prompt (
Section
 
5.3
). Finally, we compare the assessments and reasoning of the models under ordinary and adversarial requests (
Section
 
5.4
). We summarize the main outcomes below and report full details in Appendix

We experiment with a number of formatting options for the governance layer to organize information from different sources into an input prompt. Broadly, we apply two techniques: 
Role-Based Access Control (RBAC)

•

•

•

•

D2 (RBAC): The application-supplied identifier (AUTH_ID) determines whether the user is authorized for a certain request.

•

To ensure strict permission boundaries for requests from internal users to override or modify default evaluation instructions, we recommend implementing RBAC for user provenance and delineating user text separately in the input prompt. 6  Discussion and Conclusion

Our study examines how the distinction between interpreting evidence and exercising authority over the rules governing that interpretation is implemented through decision interfaces, permissions, and the treatment of external content in AI-assisted hiring. An evaluator’s ability to produce useful judgments provides only part of the basis for assigning it responsibility. Organizations must also establish which inputs can influence those judgments, which interventions are permitted, and if the resulting record supports review. The contribution of our framework is to translate these responsibilities into testable requirements within a common design, providing complementary evidence to inform deployment decisions.

6.1  Evaluation Interfaces and Organizational Control

Decision interfaces reflect the level of autonomy expected from the LLM evaluator. A binary recommendation asks the LLM to reduce qualification evidence into a proposed selection outcome, a score leaves threshold selection available to the organization, while a rubric exposes individual qualification judgments for verification and combination through explicit rules. These choices determine which parts of the assessment the model performs and which parts remain available for organizational scrutiny.

The interface alone, however, does not determine the model’s authority. A binary recommendation may be subject to human review, while rubric entries can feed an automated selection rule. The scope of delegation therefore depends on how the output is used. This distinction matters when interpreting operational performance: an interface that resolves small qualification differences may support comparison without establishing a defensible selection threshold. Similarly, agreement between a score threshold and the model’s binary decisions establishes correspondence between outputs.

The organization must consequently specify how its requirements operate in the screening process. If a qualification is mandatory, its absence may require exclusion, further verification, or an authorized exception. Leaving this treatment implicit allows the evaluator’s interpretation to determine the practical force of the requirement. An audit should examine whether the requested judgment and the downstream rule together implement the intended treatment of qualification evidence. Structured assessments can further support this examination by exposing intermediate judgments, but their value depends on the ability to verify and correct them. Human review requires access to source evidence, a defined verification task, and the authority to act on discrepancies. This is consistent with research showing that human–AI collaboration depends on how professional expertise and machine outputs are incorporated into organizational routines 
(
van den Broek et al., 2021
; 
Lebovitz et al., 2021
)
.

Fairness evaluation should follow same logic. A score difference may have different consequences depending on its proximity to a selection threshold or its use in comparing candidates. Audits must examine the interface and screening rule intended for deployment, with attention to variation across occupations and candidate groups. Removing names is a possible intervention, but its effectiveness requires evaluation as other resume attributes may retain demographic associations. These considerations connect fairness measurement to the organizational process through which assessments affect candidates 
(
Raghavan et al., 2020
; 
Blodgett et al., 2020
)
.

6.2  Permissions, Exceptions, and Assessment Fidelity

Delegated evaluation must accommodate legitimate changes without making every request a potential amendment to the screening rules. An organization may permit designated users to correct an assessment, waive a requirement, or authorize further review. Compliance readiness therefore concerns the relationship among the request, its source, and the permissions assigned to that source. Resistance to attacks is insufficient evidence if it is achieved by preventing interventions that the workflow requires.

This distinction limits what prompt-based controls can accomplish. Instructions can communicate the permissions associated with an authenticated role, but the application must establish that role and enforce permissions for consequential actions. Research on indirect prompt injection shows why this is consequential when external material enters an instruction-following system 
(
Greshake et al., 2023
; 
Hines et al., 2025
)
. While separating external evidence from governing instructions helps express the intended boundary, testing is still required to establish how reliably the configured system respects it.

Permissions themselves also require governance. A system may correctly follow an administrator’s request even when the request implements an inappropriate hiring practice. Our definition of compliance readiness concerns adherence to assigned authority, leaving the legitimacy of that authority and the acceptability of the underlying policy as separate organizational questions. Moreover, assessment fidelity makes the exercise of this authority reviewable. A waiver and a finding that a qualification is present can produce the same selection outcome, but they have different evidentiary and organizational bases. If the assessment record conflates them, a reviewer cannot determine whether the outcome was based on the candidate’s qualifications, a permitted exception, or an error. Decision-level checks alone cannot resolve this ambiguity.

The system architecture should therefore preserve source evidence, qualification assessments, authorized interventions, and resulting decisions as distinguishable components of the record. Such a design would support targeted review and make disagreements easier to locate. Our fidelity audit motivates this requirement, but does not establish whether the explanation is consistent with the model’s internal reasoning 
(
Jacovi and Goldberg, 2020
; 
Turpin et al., 2023
)
. Whether these records improve a human reviewer’s ability to detect and correct failures also remains an empirical question.

6.3  Implications for Deployment and Further Research

Deployment readiness must be assessed for a particular system configuration and scope of delegation. Changing the model, prompts, interface, screening rules, or permission structure can alter the behaviour on which an earlier deployment decision rested. Auditing should therefore inform both the initial assignment of responsibilities and the conditions that trigger a reassessment. This connects the framework to organizational guardrails and lifecycle approaches to algorithmic auditing 
(
Grisold et al., 2025
; 
Raji et al., 2020
; 
Mökander et al., 2024
)
.

Our proposal also supports decisions on the extent of delegation. An organization may assign qualification assessment to the model while retaining selection rules and exception approval elsewhere in the workflow. Broader delegation would require evidence suitable for the additional responsibilities. We provide ways to help identify weaknesses in these choices, but determining acceptable risk levels or optimal allocation of responsibilities requires further research.

Several limitations define the scope of our findings. Controlled synthetic resumes allow interpretable comparisons, but cover a limited range of candidate histories, occupations, and document formats. The experiments do not establish predictive validity for employment outcomes or examine how recruiters use the assessments. Likewise, the compliance tests cover specified attacks and permission arrangements rather than adaptive adversaries, extended interactions, or misuse by authorized users.

An immediate research priority is to examine operational and compliance requirements jointly under the same configuration. Controls that reduce unauthorized influence may also affect the interpretation of legitimate evidence or responsiveness to permitted requests. Evaluating these effects together would clarify whether a configuration preserves both responsibilities. Field studies could then examine whether explicit verification tasks and separate exception records improve the ability of reviewers to detect errors, correct assessments, and account for decisions. Broader coverage of languages, occupations, and intersecting demographic signals would help establish the scope of these conclusions.

A.3  Human validation of JD and resumes

We assessed whether human judgements supported the intended stronger-weaker ordering of the resumes as well as the suitability of the job description itself for the specified posts. Domain-specific
online forms presented a job description alongside comparisons between
a baseline resume and an edited version. Each job included six
comparisons: three variants removing one required qualification each
and three variants adding one preferred qualification each. The
baseline appeared on the left, and the edited content was highlighted. Respondents expressed their preference using a slider. Slider towards the left favored the baseline, whereas slider towards the right favored the perturbed resume. That is, for a baseline-degraded comparison, we expect the slider to be on the ledt side, whereas for a baseline-upgraded resume, we expect the sldier to be on the right side. The validation concerned the direction of preference from the human experts.

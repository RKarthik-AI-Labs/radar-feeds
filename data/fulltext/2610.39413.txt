Abstract

Deep research agents (DRAs) conduct long-horizon reasoning, search, and literature synthesis to provide long-form answers to complex, domain-specific questions. These agents are typically trained and evaluated on fully specified user queries. However, real-world user queries can often be underspecified. We introduce 
UnderSpec-DR
, a framework for systematically evaluating how underspecified queries affect agents’ final answer quality and intermediate search behavior.
Across three DRAs we evaluated, underspecification substantially reduces how well DRA’s answers fulfill users’ information needs, even though they remain grounded in cited evidence. Our analysis of intermediate search behavior suggests a possible explanation: DRAs conduct broader searches that draw less on the original user query but rely more on their learned prior during training, which can be misaligned with users’ intent. Our findings reveal the vulnerability of current DRAs to underspecified user queries and inform future efforts to build DRAs that proactively recognize when additional user input is needed to reliably complete deep research tasks.

Introduction

Deep research agents (DRAs), which autonomously plan multi-step searches, retrieve documents, read and synthesize them into long-form reports with citations, have been deployed in both commercial 
(
OpenAI, 2025
; 
Haas and Basu Mallick, 2025
)
 and open-source systems 
(
Shao et al., 2026
; 
Tongyi DeepResearch Team et al., 2025
)
. Existing benchmarks 
(
Mialon et al., 2024
; 
Wei et al., 2025
; 
Asai et al., 2026
; 
Yifei et al., 2026
; 
Du et al., 2026
)
 for evaluating DRAs carefully construct fully-specified user queries to enable consistent assessment of the agent’s deep research capability. Deep research requires precise questions, but in practice, users often cannot formulate their query as required. They may not yet have a clear understanding of their information needs, or they may lack the domain knowledge needed to express those needs precisely 
(
Taylor, 1968
; 
Belkin et al., 1982
)
. This creates a fundamental tension: DRAs are designed to answer questions that users cannot easily answer themselves, yet they are developed and evaluated on the assumption that users can state those questions perfectly. This tension motivates the central question of our work: 
Can DRAs handle cases when users cannot fully articulate those questions in the first place?

Prior work has examined how underspecified user queries affect performance in both standalone LLMs 
(
Yang et al., 2026
)
 and agentic settings 
(
Cao et al., 2026
; 
Vijayvargiya et al., 2026
)
, and has developed methods to evaluate and improve their ability to resolve ambiguity 
(
Sun et al., 2026
)
. There are verifiable outcomes under these settings, such as whether a stated requirement is met or whether the code generated passed the test cases. However, DRAs produce long, open-ended reports that are difficult to verify, which makes it hard to measure how much underspecification harms the final answer DRAs generated and whether the they resolved ambiguity. Moreover, unlike standalone LLMs, DRAs interact with external tools over long-horizon task execution. Inspecting this intermediate process might provide an additional lens for understanding the effect of user query underspecification on DRA.

To this end, we introduce 
UnderSpec-DR
, a framework for investigating DRA’s answer quality and intermediate search behavior under user queries with controlled levels of underspecification, as illustrated in Figure 
1
. To ensure that the underspecified queries reflect realistic forms of underspecification, we analyze initial–final query pairs collected from real-world researchers’ conversation logs with DRAs. We identified three types of information that are frequently omitted: 
scope constraints
 (e.g., temporal, spatial, or population constraints), 
terminology
 (e.g., technical or domain-specific terms), and 
user preferences
 (e.g., evaluation criteria or the desired answer format). We then construct underspecified versions of three existing deep research benchmarks that features open-ended questions requiring long-form answers: ResearchQA 
(
Yifei et al., 2026
)
, ScholarQA-Bench 
(
Asai et al., 2026
)
, and DeepResearch Bench 
(
Du et al., 2026
)
.

We evaluate three widely used DRAs, DR Tulu 
(
Shao et al., 2026
)
, Tongyi DeepResearch 
(
Tongyi DeepResearch Team et al., 2025
)
, and GPT 5.4 with the same tool set as DR Tulu, on both the complete queries and their underspecified variants. We assess how well their final answers satisfy the query-specific rubrics and how well their claims are supported by the cited evidence. Additionally, we examine whether DRAs recognize underspecified user query, what actions they take upon recognizing it, and whether these actions mitigate the impact of underspecification on final answer quality. To characterize changes in intermediate search behavior, we examine how search queries evolve across steps, whether successive searches introduce new documents, and whether retrieved documents inform subsequent searches.

Our study contributes three main findings:

(1)

Query underspecification does not lead to more hallucination, but likely leads to answers that are misaligned with user’s information needs.
 Omitting or obscuring even one key detail like scope constraints or technical terms significantly reduces how well answers satisfy the query-specific rubrics. In contrast, even the most underspecified queries do not lead to more hallucinations.

(2)

DRAs fill in missing information based on their learned priors during training rather than explicitly eliciting the missing information from user.
 They struggle to recognize when user queries lack critical information and, even when they do, they often proceed without seeking clarification from the user. This tendency is also reflected in their intermediate search behavior: their search queries rely less on the user’s original query and more on the DRA’s parametric knowledge.

(3)

Query underspecification leads to more diffuse search without greater use of retrieved evidence.
 DRAs conduct broader searches with greater drift in their search query and retrieve more previously unseen documents. However, they do not make greater use of retrieved content in subsequent searches.

We hope our findings pave the way for developing DRAs that recognize unresolved information gaps and make them explicit before proceeding with the research task.

Related Work

Deep research agents.

Deep research agents extend tool-augmented reasoning 
(
Yao et al., 2023
)
 with iterative search and long-form synthesis, and are trained with reinforcement learning for search 
(
Jin et al., 2025
; 
Li et al., 2025a
; 
Li et al., 2025b
)
, agentic mid- and post-training 
(
Tongyi DeepResearch Team et al., 2025
)
, or evolving rubrics 
(
Shao et al., 2026
)
. Their benchmarks consist of fully specified queries 
(
Mialon et al., 2024
; 
Wei et al., 2025
; 
Asai et al., 2026
; 
Yifei et al., 2026
; 
Du et al., 2026
)
; we construct underspecified counterparts and analyze the research process in addition to the output.

Query underspecification and ambiguity resolution.

Prior work examines how omitted requirements affect LLM performance 
(
Yang et al., 2026
)
 and evaluates or improves agents’ ability to handle underspecified queries in software engineering 
(
Vijayvargiya et al., 2026
)
, question answering 
(
Cao et al., 2026
; 
Su and Cardie, 2026
; 
Xie et al., 2026
; 
Du and Hu, 2026
)
, and other tasks with easy-to-verify answers 
(
Sun et al., 2026
; 
Liu et al., 2026
)
. In these settings, unresolved ambiguity can lead to observable task failures or deviations from user’s expected outcomes, providing relatively clear signals of when clarification is needed. For open-ended deep research, however, report-style answers are difficult to verify, and the effects of query underspecification remain unclear.

Given that human experts can select appropriate strategies to resolve ambiguity in such situations 
(
Freeman et al., 2025
)
, we seek to understand how to enable agents to develop similar capabilities. Prior work categorizes LLM’s disambiguation policies into query rewriting, long-form answer generation, and asking clarifying questions 
(
Tanjim et al., 2025
)
, and highlights the need for agentic orchestration of different disambiguation strategies based on their trade-offs. Prior work explores uncertainty quantification to inform clarification decisions 
(
Zhang and Choi, 2025
; 
Kuhn et al., 2023
)
, while other work uses preference optimization to align LLM responses with human experts’ communication practices, potentially improving question-asking behavior in specific domains 
(
Seegmiller et al., 2026
)
. However, extending these approaches to long-horizon agentic settings remains underexplored, where agents need to decide whether uncertainty can be resolved through further interaction with the environment or requires additional information from the user.

3.2 
Deriving Dimensions of Underspecification

To ground the framework in real user behavior, we collect the conversation logs of three junior researchers in electrical engineering, computer science, and construction science fields with DR Tulu 
(
Shao et al., 2026
)
 over a two-week period. See details in Appendix 
C
. We focused on junior researchers because they constitute a substantial segment of deep research agent users and may struggle to fully articulate their information needs due to limited domain expertise. From these logs, we select conversations in which they reformulated their initial queries after receiving an answer and we pair the initial query with the final query of each selected conversation and compare them to identify changes in wording and explicitly stated information. Of the 203 initial–final query pairs, 176 contained information in the final query that was absent from the initial query.

We characterize the types of information omitted from users’ initial queries through inductive coding analysis 
(
Thomas, 2006
)
. The first and second authors independently examine each initial–final query pair, identify information that is newly specified in the final query, and assign descriptive codes to characterize the type of information added. They then compare their codes and categories, discuss discrepancies, and consolidate conceptually similar categories through consensus.
This iterative process result in three higher-level categories of information that users commonly omitted from their initial queries: 
scope
, 
terminology
, and 
preferences
. 
Scope
 specifies the boundaries of the research question, such as the time period, place, population, or domain. 
Terminology
 identifies precise technical concepts or domain-specific entities. 
Preferences
 specify the user’s expectations for the answer, such as evaluation criteria, comparison requirements, or presentation format. Together, these three categories cover 94% of the 176 pairs: missing 
scope
 (45%), missing 
terminology
 (37%), and missing 
preferences
 (33%). 21% of the pairs lack more than one type of information.

3.3 
Constructing Matched Variants

We formalize each category as a rewriting rubric (Table 
1
) and prompt GPT-5.4 mini to generate underspecified query variants (see Appendix 
A.1
 for the prompts). To verify whether these variants resemble naturally occurring underspecified queries, we conduct a Turing-style discrimination test 
(
Turing, 1950
)
. We first apply the rewriting procedure to the clarified queries in our collected query pairs from real users. We then pair each generated variant with its corresponding human-written initial query and ask a separate model to identify which query is AI-generated and which is human-written.

We repeat the test twice, shuffling the order of the human-written and AI-generated queries in each run. Across 486 generated variants derived from 176 clarified queries, GPT-4o achieves identification accuracies of 58% and 54%, respectively, slightly higher than random guessing. This result suggests that the generated variants are reasonably similar to naturally occurring underspecified queries, and that the rewriting procedure can be used to construct matched query variants for existing deep research benchmarks.

We apply this rewriting procedure to three deep research benchmarks: 500 randomly sampled queries from ResearchQA, the ScholarQA-CS, ScholarQA-Multi, and ScholarQA-CS2 subsets of ScholarQA-Bench, and all queries of DeepResearch Bench. Table 
1
 reports the number of resulting variants for each rubric.

3.4 
Human Validation of Synthetic Query Variants

The first and second authors each validated half of the generated query variants, checking whether the targeted information was removed or obscured, non-target information was preserved, and the resulting query remained natural and coherent. Overall, 6% of the variants were revised to improve naturalness, and 8% were revised to ensure that technical terminology was appropriately obscured.

RQ1: How Does Underspecification Affect Answer Quality?

DRAs cannot reliably infer users’ information needs.
 Figure 
2
 shows that rubric-based coverage scores decline for a large proportion of underspecified queries even when only one key detail is omitted or obscured. Among different information types, removing scope constraints or obscuring technical terminology produces substantial mean score reductions that are statistically significant across most agents and benchmarks. In contrast, removing user preferences generally produces smaller reductions that are often not statistically significant. As user queries become more underspecified, scores decline further.

Query underspecification does not lead to more hallucination.
 Table 
2
 shows no statistically significant change in citation precision even under full underspecification condition. Document utilization in the final answer also shows no statistically significant decline: it even increases in three of the eight settings. Together with the decline in rubric-based coverage, these results suggest that underspecification primarily reduces alignment with the user’s original information needs, with no evidence of increased hallucination among cited claims.

Stronger specifically-trained deep research capability come with greater sensitivity to query underspecification.
 Using performance on complete queries as a measure of deep research capability, Figure 
2
 shows that higher-scoring agents generally incur larger absolute score losses under full underspecification. Both DR Tulu-8B (8B) and Tongyi-DR (30B) are relatively compact models trained specifically for deep research. They outperform GPT-5.4 with tools on complete queries but appear to depend more heavily on users fully specifying their information needs.

RQ2: Can DRAs Recognize Underspecification in User Queries?

Figure 3: 
Clarification behavior under underspecification across three benchmarks. Agents either ask for clarification (Ask), state assumptions (Assume), or proceed without recognizing omissions (Unaware).

Figure 4: 
Scaled performance drop by underspecification type, pooled across all three benchmarks and models, comparing runs that state an assumption and proceed with runs that do not recognize the ambiguity.

We observed that DRAs sometimes abstain from answering underspecified queries, even though we did not indicate that the user query may be underspecified in system prompt. We therefore analyze these behaviors and identified three mutually exclusive categories: 
Unaware
, in which the DRA proceeds to answer without explicitly recognizing the underspecification; 
Assume
, in which it recognizes the underspecification but proceeds by making one or more explicit assumptions; and 
Ask
, in which it abstains from answering and instead asks the user for clarification. See detailed analysis protocol in Appendix 
D
. We then use GPT-5.4 mini to classify all the responses into these categories based on observable behavior in the DRA’s reasoning trajectories and final answers.

DRAs show limited awareness of missing information.
 Although answer quality declines for a large proportion of underspecified queries (Figure 
2
), DRAs generally cannot identify that key information is missing: Unaware is the most frequent category across all different DRAs (Figure 
4
; Table 
4
). Even when they acknowledge ambiguity, they usually answer under one or more stated assumptions rather than ask the user for clarification. Across agents, Tongyi-DR shows the least explicit awareness, whereas DR Tulu-8B and GPT-5.4 with tools more often state assumptions.

Stated assumptions do not guarantee better answers, and even worse.
 Although stating assumptions is DRAs’ predominant response strategy upon recognizing underspecification, Assume runs exhibit larger answer-quality losses than Unaware runs in three of the four conditions, as shown in Figure 
4
. One possible explanation is that unrecognized underspecification may be mild enough for DRAs to infer a plausible interpretation, whereas explicitly recognized ambiguity may reflect greater uncertainty about the user’s intent. In such cases, proceeding under assumptions may be less appropriate than seeking clarification.

DRAs change search direction more often and investigate less deeply.
 This shift is most evident under the most underspecified variant, with more Exploration and fewer Specialization transitions across all three DRAs (Figure 
6
). Obscuring technical terminology also has a pronounced effect on search strategy. These changes indicate more frequent switches between different facets of a topic and fewer refinements that add constraints to narrow the search. Changes in the proportions of Generalization and Repetition are generally smaller and are not statistically significant in most comparisons.

Searches become less grounded in user queries and draw more on parametric knowledge.
 According to Figure 
6
, the share of search-query tokens traceable to the user query decreases significantly, while the share of untraced tokens increases significantly in most cases. These untraced tokens occur in neither the user query, earlier search queries, nor retrieved documents, suggesting potential greater reliance on parametric knowledge to formulate searches. Scope removal produces a more consistent increase in the untraced token share than obscuring terminology. Removing preferences does not significantly change token provenance of search queries.

Broader retrieval does not translate into greater utilization of retrieved content in subsequent searches.
 Figure 
7
 has shown that document novelty increases significantly under fully underspecified conditions for all three DRAs and without term, without scope conditions for DR Tulu and Tongyi. However, the context-driven term adoption rate does not increase correspondingly, and even dropped significantly for DR Tulu and Tongyi under the most underspecified conditions.

8 
Discussion and Conclusion

Users should prioritize clearly defining the research question when formulating search queries.

In particular, users should state key scope constraints and use precise technical terminology, as omitting or obscuring this information can substantially affect the search process and degrade the final answer quality. By comparison, information like user preferences has a smaller effect on answer quality.

DRAs need to be explicitly trained to identify missing information during long-horizon task execution.

Our experiments revealed the usability challenge of current DRAs: users may need sufficient domain knowledge to identify relevant constraints and terminology before agents can effectively address their needs. For lay person who discovers missing specifications only after reviewing a lengthy report, they must spend additional time diagnosing the mismatch and reformulating their requests. Repeating the research process also incurs potentially avoidable tool calls and token consumption. We argue that DRAs should distinguish uncertainty that can be resolved through further search from missing information that must be elicited from the user. Training this capability through imitation learning requires training data that capture both deep research trajectories and appropriate clarification actions, but such data can be difficult to obtain. Our analysis of intermediate search behavior suggests that metrics such as the proportion of untraced search-query tokens, and context-driven term adoption rate may serve as useful indicators of missing information in user queries.

We introduce 
UnderSpec-DR
, a framework for evaluating Deep Research Agents under query underspecification. Using this testbed, we examine changes in final answer quality and intermediate search behavior casued by query underspecification. We further analyze DRAs’ awareness of query underspecification, their response strategies upon recognizing it, and whether these strategies mitigate answer-quality losses. We find that evidence-grounded reports may still fail to meet users’ needs.These results provide practical advice on how users should prompt DRAs to get desired answer and offer implications for building DRAs that recognize when to ask users for additional input, balancing reliable task completion with minimal disruption.

Limitations

Language.

Our experiments primarily use English queries; DeepResearch Bench includes tasks in Chinese. Underspecification may take different forms in other languages and extending 
UnderSpec-DR
 to these settings is a natural direction for future work.

Coverage of agents.

Our evaluation covers three agents whose intermediate search and tool-use trajectories are accessible for analysis. We do not evaluate closed-source deep research systems that do not expose these trajectories, as this would prevent us from analyzing their search behavior. These systems may employ different search and clarification strategies.

Ethics Statement

Our study does not involve the generation of harmful content. Its main implication is a risk for users: when a query is underspecified, current DRAs may return a well-cited answer to a broader or different question than the one the user intended, usually without signaling that information was missing. Users who rely on such answers, for example in medical or policy research, may draw conclusions that do not apply to their situation. We hope that our findings encourage the development of agents that recognize missing information and make their assumptions visible to users.

Appendix B 
Deep Research Agent Configuration

We run all three deep research agents with the same budget of at most 15 tool calls per query. All inference is run on four NVIDIA H100 GPUs. The agent-specific configurations are as follows:

•

DR Tulu
: uses DR-Tulu-8B as the backbone model. Web search is performed through the Serper API and scholarly search through the Semantic Scholar API, and web pages are browsed through the Jina API.

•

GPT-5.4 with tools
: uses the same agent framework and tool set as DR Tulu-8B, with GPT-5.4 as the backbone model. Search, scholarly search, and browsing therefore use the Serper, Semantic Scholar, and Jina APIs, respectively.

•

Tongyi-DR
: uses Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B as the backbone model. Web search is performed through the Serper API, and web pages are browsed through the Jina API.

Appendix C 
Real-World Query Collection Protocol

This section describes the protocol used to collect and analyze real user queries in Section 
3.2
.

Participants used the public web demo of DR Tulu released by AI2 (
https://www.dr-tulu.org/chat
). We asked them to ask agent questions arising from their own research. Then they were asked to carefully check whether each response matched their expectations and, if it did not, revise the query and ask again, repeating until they obtained a satisfactory response. Because the deep research agent does not support multi-turn interaction, each reformulation was submitted as a new, self-contained query rather than as a follow-up message. For every question that was not answered satisfactorily on the first attempt, participants shared the corresponding chat history via a link. All participants gave their consent to the use of their shared chat histories in this study.

Appendix D 
Answer Analysis Protocol

We first manually inspected approximately 10% of the 1,922 agent responses to underspecified queries (about 192 responses). We sampled proportionally within each query-variant type, selecting approximately 10% of responses from each of the fully underspecified, without scope, without terminology, and without preferences conditions. This inspection revealed that some agents acknowledged ambiguity but continued answering under explicit assumptions. We therefore adapt the behavioral analysis approach of 
Su and Cardie (2026)
 to distinguish three mutually exclusive categories, based on each agent’s input query, recorded reasoning trajectory, and final response: 
Ask
, 
Assume
, 
Unaware
.

We then prompted GPT-5.4 mini to annotate the same 192 responses using these categories. Cohen’s 
 between the human and model annotations is 0.92, indicating almost perfect agreement. Following this validation step, we used the same model and prompt to annotate all responses. The classification prompt is provided in Appendix 
A.2
, and each label is accompanied by a brief justification.

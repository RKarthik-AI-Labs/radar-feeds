Abstract

Reinforcement learning (RL) improves large language model search agents by up to 32 exact-match (EM) points over their pre-RL initializations, yet overall accuracy gains do not identify which underlying functional capabilities improve or interact during training. We introduce 
CO-GRAFT
, a reward-agnostic interventional attribution framework in which an RL-trained agent and its pre-RL initialization jointly generate a single trajectory under closed-loop execution with live retrieval. Each policy controls a mutually exclusive subset of four functional roles: intermediate reasoning, search-or-answer decision, search query generation, and final answer extraction. Evaluating all 
 role assignments without modifying weights or retraining defines a cooperative game whose Shapley values decompose the total RL gain across roles, while Möbius interaction terms distinguish standalone role improvements from multi-role coupling. Applying 
CO-GRAFT
 to Search-R1 agents across Qwen2.5 and Llama-3.2 models (spanning two sizes, base and instruction-tuned initializations, GRPO and PPO optimizers, and NQ and HotpotQA datasets), we show that query generation never exceeds 15% of the total gain; instead, gains are governed by pre-RL answer validity, search retention, model scale, and fixed task-level search budgets, shifting credit from reasoning toward answer extraction in larger or well-formatted answer response checkpoints while smaller base models rely on reasoning–decision coupling.

CO-GRAFT

We introduce 
CO-GRAFT
, an attribution framework that decomposes the performance gain of an RL-trained search agent across the functional roles of its trajectory. To isolate where RL improvements originate, we first partition every trajectory into four exhaustive, non-overlapping roles. We then construct grafted rollouts in which the RL policy and its pre-RL initialization jointly generate trajectories under controlled role assignments, and finally quantify both standalone and synergistic contributions using Shapley decomposition and Möbius interaction terms.

Roles.

CO-GRAFT
 decomposes a search-agent trajectory into four distinct roles covering every policy-generated token. Following the standard Search-R1 trajectory format 
Jin et al. (2025b)

Crucially, this partition assigns every model-generated token in the trajectory to exactly one role, ensuring that any shift in task accuracy traces directly to the specific roles whose output tokens change.

3.2 
Evaluation Datasets and Metrics

We use the same evaluation datasets from the Search-R1 setting.
Because running every question through 16 role assignments is computationally expensive, we evaluate on fixed random subsets rather than full test sets: 1,000 questions each of NQ 
Kwiatkowski et al. (2019)

Our primary metric is EM on the last answer block, and we also report the valid-answer rate (the fraction of questions with a well-formed answer block).

3.3 
Training Recipe

We adapt the Search-R1 GRPO recipe to train search agents, including outcome EM reward on the final answer plus a 0.2 format bonus for a well-formed trajectory, retrieved tokens masked from the loss, four search turns of at most 500 generated tokens per turn, group size 5, learning rate 
, batch size 512, KL coefficient 
, and 1

3.4 
Retrieval Environment

Following the Search-R1 setup 
Jin et al. (2025b)
, we use E5-base-v2 
Wang et al. (2024a)

3.5 
Harness

To execute multi-turn rollouts, each trajectory in both training and evaluation is bounded to at most four search turns followed by one final answer turn, with a maximum of 500 generated tokens per turn. Any turn that terminates without a complete action tag receives an invalid-action prompt and passes to the next turn if the turn budget permits.

Because trajectory grafting modifies rollout execution flow, we verify that the 
CO-GRAFT
 harness preserves single-policy behavior when no role switching occurs, by evaluating 
 (pre-RL agent only) and 
 (RL agent only), under the original Search-R1 codebase and the 
CO-GRAFT
 harness across identical evaluation sets. Across 32 comparisons (8 model pairs, two datasets, and two policy behaviors), 
CO-GRAFT
 matches Search-R1 accuracy within single-point EM noise in 28 cases, with the remaining four cases differing by at most 2.3 points. Thus, the harness reproduces baseline policy behaviors without introducing implementation artifacts.

Results

In this section, we present our main results in Table 
1
 and understand the findings across multiple factors, including question complexity, model scale, and instruction tuning.

4.1 
Effect of Question Complexity: Single-hop vs. Multi-hop

Question complexity has only a secondary effect on role distribution, mainly shifting credit between Reasoning and Answer while overall patterns remain driven by search behavior. Comparing role attributions across single-hop NQ and multi-hop HotpotQA for seven models (two models are excluded because their RL gain fell below the reporting threshold), we find that the Query role remains invariant across task types and stays bounded at or below 15%. In contrast, Reasoning and Answer exhibit a clear trade-off: Reasoning attribution is consistently higher on multi-hop HotpotQA (across six of seven models with maximum up to 33 percentage points), whereas Answer attribution is higher on single-hop NQ (across six of seven models with maximum up to 29 percentage points), reflecting how multi-hop queries promote and shift credit from final answer generation toward intermediate reasoning steps. Meanwhile, the Decision share remains below 25% when search behavior is stable (reaching non-zero search calls), but increases substantially when models either increase or decrease search calls (reaching up to 38–86% when searches drop to zero). Overall, the distributional shift across question complexity is smaller than the divergence induced by changes in search behavior.

4.2 
Effect of Model Scale: Smaller vs. Larger Models

Model scale shifts which roles receive credit, moving attribution away from Reasoning and Decision toward Answer and Query. Across the three families where both sizes gain at least four points (Qwen2.5 base, Qwen2.5 instruct, and Llama-3.2 base), larger models allocate 17 to 40 percentage points more credit to Answer and Query combined in five of six comparisons. This shift is stronger in Qwen2.5 base, where the 3B model relies heavily on Reasoning (70% vs. 40% on NQ; 81% vs. 45% on HotpotQA) while the 7B model shifts 19 to 21 points more toward Answer; Qwen2.5 instruct mirrors this trade-off on HotpotQA (Reasoning 69% vs. 35%; Answer 15% vs. 45%). Similarly, in Llama-3.2 base, the 1B model abandons search (Query 
) whereas the 3B model sustains search calls and attributes 12–15% to Query. Because larger models show higher pre-RL valid-answer rates (e.g., 53% vs. 79% on Qwen2.5 NQ) with the exception of Qwen2.5-Instruct, where rates are equal (97%), the gain attributions generally suggest a scale-driven shift toward Answer and Query.

4.3 
Effect of Instruction Tuning: Base vs. Instruct

Instruction tuning does not produce a uniform shift in credit allocation; instead, its effect depends on how post-training alters a model’s valid-answer rate and search trajectory. Across four base–instruct pairs, we find that instruction-tuned models with high valid-answer rates realize smaller RL gains and shift attribution toward Answer, whereas models that abandon search stall the RL gains and break the pattern. Prior work on RL for search agents primarily evaluates end-to-end task performance rather than decomposing which behavioral changes produce the gain. Existing RL search agents

7 
Conclusion

We introduce 
CO-GRAFT
, a framework that decomposes the EM gain of an RL-trained search agent across its four trajectory roles under closed-loop execution, showing that pre-RL answer validity, search retention, and model scale govern whether gains stem from reasoning–decision coupling, fixed task-level search budgets, or final answer extraction, while search query generation (
Q
) never exceeds 15% of the total gain on NQ and HotpotQA. Because outcome-based EM obscures which functional behavior improves during training, future RL reward designs targeting retrieval skill should explicitly supervise query formulation (
Q
) and evidence-sufficient stopping decisions (
D
).

Limitations

CO-GRAFT
 attributes the performance difference between a pair of policies rather than evaluating a standalone checkpoint, and its interpretations are subject to the properties of cooperative game-theoretic decomposition and our experimental setup. Attribution shares measure gains relative to a chosen baseline policy (the pre-RL initialization), and Shapley values average marginal contributions across all role permutations; when multi-role interactions are substantial (such as in the Qwen2.5-7B PPO pair), additive Shapley shares alone do not capture joint dependencies and must be interpreted alongside the Möbius interaction terms. In addition, functional boundaries are segmented using structural action tags, grouping internal planning, passage synthesis, and structural formatting together inside the intermediate reasoning role (
R
), leaving finer-grained decompositions of 
R
 to future work.

Our empirical evaluation is also bounded by model scale, inference serving stochasticity, and diagnostic scope. Compute constraints limit our locally trained models to 7B parameters or smaller, requiring us to omit a 14B or 32B model pair, and batched greedy decoding introduces approximately one EM point of serving variance. Furthermore, the fixed-budget and retrieval-swap diagnostics cover only the two Qwen2.5 PPO pairs on NQ and HotpotQA, and selecting the optimal budget on the evaluation set provides an upper-bound estimate within this noise floor. Finally, training recipes incorporating query-level process rewards, longer optimization schedules, or live web search may redistribute gains differently across roles, which 
CO-GRAFT
 can be directly applied to measure.

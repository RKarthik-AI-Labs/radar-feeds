Abstract

Personalized long-form generation asks a language model to write new text the way a specific user would. The standard recipe, retrieval-augmented generation, places a few of the user’s past texts in the prompt but leaves the model to decide what to take from them. We introduce 
StylePrism
, a taxonomy of 28 stylistic aspects in seven categories, and 
SP-Refine
, an inference-time method that makes style extraction an explicit step: the model first fills 
StylePrism
 with the user’s patterns, quoted evidence, and habits to avoid, then drafts from the user’s samples and revises the draft twice against this profile, guided by its own style scores. On the three long-form tasks of LongLaMP with three LLMs, 
SP-Refine
 outperforms LongLaMP’s retrieval-augmented baseline in 25 of 27 ROUGE and METEOR comparisons, by 10.2% on average and by up to 35.8%. Annotating users’ own texts with the extracted profiles shows that 
StylePrism
 captures concrete, checkable habits, from the order of rhetorical moves to a reviewer’s sign-off. Ablations show that the profile helps as revision guidance, not as a replacement for the samples: writing from the profile alone is worse in all 27 comparisons, while a single implicit-inference call is a cheaper alternative that comes close. Oracle variants that let the reviser see the reference, together with reference-free controls, show that larger refinement gains require information unavailable at inference time.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to draft text on a person’s behalf: research abstracts, product reviews, social media posts. For such text to be useful, it should be correct and fluent and also sound like its author. Personalized text generation studies this problem by conditioning a model on a user’s writing history 
(
Salemi et al., 2024b
; 
Zhang et al., 2024
)
, and the LongLaMP benchmark 
(
Kumar et al., 2024
)
 extends it to long-form outputs, where differences in style between users are most visible.

The dominant approach is retrieval-augmented personalization: retrieve the user’s past texts that are most relevant to the current input and place them in the prompt 
(
Salemi et al., 2024b
; 
Kumar et al., 2024
; 
Salemi et al., 2024a
)
. Retrieval decides 
which
 samples the model sees, but says little about 
what
 the model should take from them. In LongLaMP, the retrieved samples are followed by a single instruction such as “Following the given patterns”. Whether the model attends to sentence rhythm, hedging, discourse moves, or formatting is left implicit.

We make style an explicit, inspectable intermediate. 
StylePrism
 (Figure 
2
) organizes writing style into 28 aspects in seven categories, from lexical choice to cross-task fingerprints. 
SP-Refine
 (Algorithm 
1
) uses it as a function: given the retrieved samples, the model first 
extracts
 a style profile that fills every 
StylePrism
 aspect with an observed pattern, quoted evidence, and habits to avoid; it then drafts the output from the samples, and revises the draft twice against the profile, scoring its own revision on five style criteria and fixing the weakest. Unlike retrieval-augmented generation, 
SP-Refine
 produces a profile that a person can read and check (Figure 
3
).

We evaluate on the three long-form LongLaMP tasks with three LLM families, using reference-based metrics and a rubric-based LLM judge that scores style fidelity and task quality separately (Figure 
1
). Our contributions are:

•

StylePrism
, a taxonomy of writing style that serves as the schema for extraction, the guide for revision, and the basis of our style rubric.

•

SP-Refine
, which outperforms LongLaMP’s retrieval-augmented baseline in 25 of 27 reference-based comparisons and improves LLM-judged style fidelity over it in 8 of 9 generator–task settings.

•

An annotation study of users’ reference texts showing which profile aspects are borne out by what the user actually wrote, and where users depart from their own profile.

•

Ablations and an oracle study showing that the profile works as revision guidance but not as a replacement for the samples, and separating gains achievable at inference time from those that need the reference.

Related Work

Personalized text generation.

LaMP 
(
Salemi et al., 2024b
)
 introduced a benchmark for personalizing LLM outputs with retrieved user history, and LongLaMP 
(
Kumar et al., 2024
)
 extended it to long-form generation. Most methods follow the retrieval-augmented recipe and improve the retriever 
(
Salemi et al., 2024a
; 
Mysore et al., 2024
)
, summarize the user’s history 
(
Richardson et al., 2023
)
, or decompose personalization into multiple stages 
(
Li et al., 2023
)
; see 
Zhang et al. (2024)
 for a survey. We keep retrieval fixed and study what the model is told to extract from the retrieved samples and how it uses it.

Representing writing style.

Style has been studied in style representation learning, where a central difficulty is separating style from content 
(
Wegmann et al., 2022
)
, and in style transfer 
(
Jin et al., 2022
)
. LLMs can transfer style from instructions and a few examples 
(
Reif et al., 2022
)
 and describe stylistic attributes in natural language 
(
Patel et al., 2023
)
. 
StylePrism
 organizes such attributes into a schema that an LLM fills from a handful of samples, with quoted evidence for each claim.

Refinement and LLM-based evaluation.

LLMs can critique and revise their own outputs 
(
Madaan et al., 2023
)
, and LLM judges are widely used to evaluate generation 
(
Zheng et al., 2023
; 
Liu et al., 2023
)
, although they can favor their own outputs 
(
Panickssery et al., 2024
)
. 
SP-Refine
’s revisions are guided by an explicit style profile and never see the reference; we compare them with oracle revisions that do.

SP-Refine

4 
Experimental Setup

Data.

We use the three public long-form tasks of LongLaMP 
(
Kumar et al., 2024
)
 in the 
user
 setting, in which test users do not appear in training: abstract generation (title and key items 
 abstract), review writing (rating, product description, and summary 
 review), and topic writing (Reddit post summary 
 post). We evaluate on the full test sets: 4,560 abstract, 1,822 review, and 2,452 topic instances (Appendix 
A
). No method is trained or tuned on these data.

Models.

We generate with three LLMs from different providers, DeepSeek-V4-Flash, Grok-4.1-Fast (non-reasoning), and GPT-5.4-nano, accessed through Azure AI Foundry. Every call uses the same client and settings: the system message “You are a helpful assistant.” and the prompt as the user message; style extraction requests JSON output. A response blocked by the provider’s content filter is kept as an empty output and scored as such.

Baselines.

No Profile
 sends the task input alone (e.g., “Generate an abstract for the title … using the following items: …”), with no samples and no added instruction. 
LongLaMP
 formats the 
 retrieved entries with LongLaMP’s own prompt templates 
(
Kumar et al., 2024
)
 and appends the task input, e.g., after “Following the given patterns” for reviews; the entries’ text is passed through unchanged. 
Random-Samples
 uses the same template, but with 
 entries drawn at random from the user’s own history instead of the 
 most relevant ones, which isolates the effect of retrieval. As in LongLaMP, profile abstracts are truncated to their first 750 words in all methods. All prompts are in Appendix 
G
.

Reference-based metrics.

As LongLaMP does, we report ROUGE-1, ROUGE-L 
(
Lin, 2004
)
, and METEOR 
(
Banerjee and Lavie, 2005
)
, computed with the LongLaMP evaluation code and averaged over test instances.

LLM judge.

Reference-overlap metrics reward shared content but say little about style. We therefore also score outputs with an LLM judge, GPT-5.4-nano with low reasoning effort. The judge sees the task, the full generated output, and the full reference, and rates the output from 1 to 5 on nine criteria with anchored descriptions of the extremes. 
Style Fidelity
 (SF) is the mean of Lexical, Syntax, Tone, Discourse, and Format; 
Task Quality
 (TQ) is the mean of Coverage, Factuality, Fluency, and Length. Both use the reference as the anchor; 
SP-Refine
’s own scores in steps 3–4 use the same five style criteria but the samples as the anchor, so the method never sees the judge’s anchor. Because the judge is also one of our generators, it may favor GPT-generated text 
(
Panickssery et al., 2024
)
; we therefore compare methods only within a generator.

SP-Infer

7.2 
Results

The profile helps as guidance, not as a replacement.

SP-Profile
 uses the same samples and the same profile as 
SP-Refine
 yet scores below it in all 27 reference-based comparisons, and below 
SP-Infer
 in all 27 (Table 
3
, Figure 
6
). The per-criterion judge scores (Appendix 
F
) locate the loss: 
SP-Profile
 is below 
SP-Infer
 on Lexical in all 9 generator–task settings and on Coverage in 8 of 9, but above it on Format in 6 of 9, by 1.0–1.5 points on review writing. Writing from 
 alone loses the samples’ concrete wording and content cues, while explicit formatting fields are enforced reliably. 
SP-Refine
 keeps the samples in view at every step and uses 
 only to correct a draft, and it keeps 
SP-Profile
’s formatting gain on DeepSeek reviews (Format 3.37 vs. 3.32) while avoiding its lexical loss (1.90 vs. 1.83).

Implicit inference is a strong single call.

Since 
SP-Infer
 is 
SP-Refine
’s own first draft, the difference between the two is exactly what extraction and revision add. On ROUGE and METEOR this is small and depends on the generator: revision helps with Grok and GPT, where 
SP-Refine
 is higher in 13 of 18 comparisons by up to 0.015, but hurts with DeepSeek, where it is lower in 8 of 9 by up to 0.010. On judged Style Fidelity, 
SP-Refine
 is higher in 5 of 9 settings, tied in 1, and lower in 3 by at most 0.04. The largest gain is on DeepSeek reviews (2.69 vs. 2.47), almost entirely from Format. In short, 
SP-Infer
 recovers most of 
SP-Refine
’s quality on its own, and the extraction and revision steps pay off mainly where the draft breaks the user’s formatting conventions.

Revision does not repair every formatting problem. On review writing, the Format scores of Grok and GPT stay low after revision (1.84 and 1.91), well below 
SP-Profile
’s (2.84 and 3.36).

Why is 
SP-Infer
 better than 
SP-Refine
 with DeepSeek?

A likely explanation is that, with DeepSeek, revision trades overlap with the reference for conformance to the profile. On abstracts and reviews, 
SP-Refine
 raises DeepSeek’s Style Fidelity over 
SP-Infer
 (by 0.09 and 0.22), mostly through Format (
0.43 and 
1.06), and raises Coverage on abstracts (3.23 vs. 3.13), yet its ROUGE and METEOR are lower in 8 of 9 comparisons. DeepSeek’s drafts already have the highest overlap of the three generators (e.g., ROUGE-L 0.273 on abstracts vs. 0.254 for Grok and 0.217 for GPT). A revision that rewrites sentences toward the profile’s patterns and evidence phrases, which come from 
other
 documents, is then more likely to replace words the draft shared with the reference than to add new ones; with the weaker drafts of Grok and GPT, the same revisions repair more than they replace. DeepSeek may also follow “apply every applicable PSP field” more literally and rewrite more of the draft.

8 
Conclusion

We introduced 
StylePrism
, a taxonomy of 28 stylistic aspects, and 
SP-Refine
, which uses it to make style extraction an explicit step in personalized long-form generation: the model writes down the user’s style with quoted evidence, then drafts from the user’s samples and revises against that profile. 
SP-Refine
 outperforms LongLaMP’s retrieval-augmented baseline in 25 of 27 reference-based comparisons and improves judged style fidelity, and the extracted profiles predict concrete habits that users’ held-out texts bear out. Ablations show that the profile is useful as revision guidance but cannot replace the samples, and that a single implicit-inference call comes close. Oracle and control experiments show that larger refinement gains require the reference itself. Next steps are removing sample-format artifacts from the prompts and deciding per user or generator whether to revise.

Limitations

We evaluate only the user setting of LongLaMP and only English data. Our 
LongLaMP
 baseline uses LongLaMP’s prompt templates and retrieval but our generators and generation settings, so absolute numbers are not directly comparable to those of 
Kumar et al. (2024)
. ROUGE and METEOR measure overlap with a single reference and capture style only indirectly. Our LLM judge is a model, not a human, and also one of our generators; its scores use the reference as an anchor, so Style Fidelity partly reflects content similarity. 
SP-Refine
’s self-scores use the same five style criteria as the judge, anchored on the samples rather than the reference, which may favor 
SP-Refine
 on the judge’s style criteria. The annotation study examines individual instances and is illustrative. We study prompting with a fixed retriever and 
; other retrievers, values of 
, and fine-tuning are not explored. Oracle controls use a sample of test instances, and closed-model APIs may change over time.

Ethics Statement

Systems that imitate a person’s writing style can be misused to impersonate that person or to produce deceptive text attributed to them. Our methods operate only on a user’s own past writing for the purpose of assisting that user, and we do not release models trained to imitate specific individuals. Explicit profiles make what is being imitated inspectable, but they also summarize a person’s habits, including signature phrases and sign-offs, and should be treated as personal data. Generated text can repeat sentences from a user’s earlier documents and invent quantitative claims; deployed systems should check for both. We use the publicly released LongLaMP benchmark 
(
Kumar et al., 2024
)
 for research purposes only, consistent with its intended use; its Hugging Face release does not state a license. The data are derived from public scientific abstracts, Amazon reviews, and Reddit posts and may contain personal information, which we do not redistribute; the user texts shown in our figures are taken from the public benchmark.

Use of AI assistants.

We used AI assistants (Claude) to help write and debug experiment and analysis code, to format tables and figures, and to draft and edit parts of the text. The authors designed the study, ran all experiments, checked every number against the experiment outputs, and take full responsibility for the content.

Abstract

Language-model agents’ beliefs can be manipulated through open channels such as social media, even when the information they receive is truthful. When agents fill gaps between fragments with self-induced relationships, they may accept those relationships as established facts and pass the resulting rumours to others. Drawing on cognitive theory of narrative understanding and film theory of montage, we introduce Generative Montage to operationalize this failure, termed 
narrative overfitting
, by coordinating agents to arrange truthful fragments into misleading narratives. Across 1,090 rumour targets from six Twitter events, agents often accepted rumours overconfidently, with overall acceptance exceeding 65% in twelve of fourteen models. Acceptance remained above 50% after majority voting or review of the same evidence, showing how errors can reach downstream decisions. To explain this susceptibility, fragment interventions identify posts that influence belief within complete narratives. Synthetic controls then show that similar response patterns arise from valid evidence, separating belief influence from evidential support. This distinction motivates Grounded Monitor, a proof-of-concept neuro-symbolic runtime monitor that checks direct evidential support to guide agents’ judgements, improving decision accuracy by 5.6–29.0 percentage points across original and anonymized settings. These findings expose a cognitive route to rumour amplification and support monitoring inferred relationships before agent-generated conclusions guide public decisions.

Humans can make sense of incomplete information by connecting information fragments into narratives, inferring relationships that have not been stated explicitly 
[
1
, 
2
]
. In literature and film, for example, this capacity allows readers and viewers to follow stories whose connections are left unstated. However, in everyday communication, a plausible connection can be mistaken for an established fact. The proliferation of social media makes this problem significant because people interpret unfolding events from scattered reports and share explanations while information is still incomplete 
[
3
]
. A large study of Twitter found that false news spread faster and reached more people than true news 
[
4
]
, underscoring the importance of understanding how an interpretation becomes a claim that others accept and circulate.

To judge whether a circulating narrative is reliable, a reader normally first checks whether its individual fragments are true. Even when they are truthful, the connections inferred between them can still be mistaken. For instance, reports that a police officer was present when an attack began and was later reported dead may indicate that he was the first victim, although neither establishes the order of casualties. In this inference, the reader may persuade themselves with the missing relationship, turning two truthful fragments into a more specific narrative than the evidence offers. Such gap-filling opens a route for 
lying with truth
, in which someone selects and arranges truthful statements to mislead a target conclusion. Research on 
paltering
 describes how truthful communication can produce this effect in human society 
[
5
, 
6
]
. Checking individual statements must therefore be accompanied by examining the relationships inferred between them as in factuality evaluation 
[
7
]
.

The task of retrieving and aggregating information from multiple sources into summaries or answers that explain events is increasingly delegated to autonomous language-model agents. On commercial social platforms, Grok Bot can search posts, read timelines and assemble narratives about activity on X 
[
8
]
, while Reddit’s AI search summarizes posts and comments across communities into answers that users can share elsewhere 
[
9
]
. Alongside these commercial applications, researchers also examine how language models summarize discussions and participate in social exchanges 
[
10
, 
11
]
. Across these settings, systems must reason and decide how fragments relate when the sources leave those relationships unstated. Their growing role raises the concern: whether language-model agents share the human cognitive gap of accepting coherent explanations that fit truthful fragments but are not established by them.

The answer is illustrated by recent cybersecurity evaluation reported by Anthropic. Claude agent inferred a real company belonged to an authorized exercise because its name resembled the fictional target and its systems were accessible from the exercise network 
[
12
]
. Yet these inference actually should not establish the authorization. In social information systems, such an unsupported connection can also pass from one recipient to another when an agent presents its interpretation in a summary that others accept and repeat in good faith. As repetition increases perceived accuracy 
[
13
, 
14
, 
15
]
, narratives derived from the same inference can appear to corroborate one another 
[
16
]
. Then the auditor reviewing their agreement may encounter additional persuasive consensus without new evidence for the relationship that produced it. Consequently, the malicious users can exploit this coordinated process by leading unwitting users and agents to reconstruct and propagate the same unsupported connection for their expected target. On social media, such a rumour cascade creates a risk of collective cognitive failure where the manipulated interpretations finally become shared beliefs for distorting public understanding and responses to rumour events 
[
4
, 
13
]
.

To understand why an inferred connection can acquire the status of evidence, we draw on cognitive theory of how people self-interpret incomplete events 
[
17
]
. Situation models organize events around time, place, actors, causality and goals 
[
18
]
; the resulting coherence can shape both verdicts and evaluations of individual arguments 
[
19
, 
20
]
. These processes make incomplete information intelligible, but they also sustain causal beliefs that the observations do not justify 
[
21
, 
22
]
. The studies about language-model cognition and event comprehension further motivate us testing how models distinguish a suggested relationship from an established one 
[
23
, 
24
, 
25
]
. We describe this cognitive failure to maintain this distinction as 
narrative overfitting
, in which a autonomous agent overconfidently support spurious links to truthful fragments and accepts the targeted narratives 
[
26
]
.

To test whether malicious information producers can deliberately elicit this failure, we develop Generative Montage. Its design draws on the Kuleshov effect in file theory, in which surrounding images change the emotion attributed to the same neutral face 
[
27
, 
28
]
. Applied to social feeds, this principle suggests that the selection, juxtaposition, and ordering of truthful fragments can lead readers to infer relationships that no individual fragment establishes. Our framework coordinates three major roles: a 
Writer
 assembles truthful evidence around a target rumour, an 
Editor
 structures and sequences the material into posts, and a 
Director
 verifies their factual grounding against source records and guides revisions. The resulting posts are then disseminated naturally through controlled Sybil publishers on social media. Crucially, the target claim is never presented as an established fact; instead, the victim agent must supply the missing inference that makes the narrative appear to support it. We term this mechanism 
cognition-based collusion
, whereby coordinated information producers manipulate an agent’s beliefs by inducing it to draw conclusions unsupported by the underlying evidence. Unlike conventional attacks that rely on stronger assumptions, such as covert coordination or backdoor access 
[
29
, 
30
, 
31
, 
32
, 
33
]
, our study reveals a new cognitive threat that is harder to guard against: 
even open communication channels can be exploited to manipulate agents’ beliefs, with potential downstream consequences for public opinion.

We apply this procedure to 1,090 rumour targets spanning six public events in CoPHEME and find high unsupported acceptance with high confidence across all 14 evaluated models. This pattern persists across the tested model versions and reasoning prompts, with high initial confidence often accompanying later acceptance. The full procedure also elicits greater acceptance than its ablations, suggesting that narrative composition, revision and presentation jointly contribute to the effect. Across examples, agents can self-infer temporal relations, numerical referents or intentions that extend the accepted narratives beyond what the truthful fragments interpret. These errors also persist under additional downstream review. Although multi-agent debate can improve reasoning and factuality 
[
34
]
, both majority voting and a language-model judge retain substantial unsupported acceptance in our evaluation. In this configuration, five separately instantiated analysts first interpret the feed without knowing the target, and their reports are fixed before target assessment. The judge then reviews these reports alongside the original feed and rational. Crucially, wrong agreement among multiple victim analysts normally self-defend their reasoning rational and the collective review therefore can reinforce an unsupported interpretation and make the judge agent more difficult to detect 
[
35
]
.

To examine how the attack shapes judgement, we quantify each post’s 
belief pull
 as its effect on target belief when presented alone relative to an empty context, and use 
response dispersion
 to capture variation in the judgements elicited by different fragments within a feed. Across six models, removing the post with the strongest isolated belief pull reduces target belief in the complete feed more than random deletion. Transplanting the selected post into ordinary reporting increases dispersion, but does not consistently strengthen belief in the complete feed. These interventions identify content that influences judgement within its original narrative, while leaving open whether that influence reflects valid evidence or unsupported inference.
We therefore examine the same response patterns in synthetic worlds built as graphs of entities linked by known relations, such as temporal order or spatial containment, so that both target truth and support from the supplied fragments can be checked directly.
Similar patterns arise both without adversarial evidence selection and when narratives provide valid support for their targets, showing that an influential fragment can either supply evidence or invite an unsupported inference. The key implication for monitoring is therefore to distinguish relationships established by the supplied fragments from assumptions that merely make the narrative appear to support its target.

Building on this distinction, we develop Grounded Monitor (GM), a proof-of-concept neuro-symbolic monitor 
[
36
]
, to check evidential support before an agent judges a target. Its neural component interprets each post’s relation to the target, distinguishing direct support from inferential assumptions, while its symbolic component withdraws those assumptions and checks whether support remains. GM flags narratives that promote a target without detected direct support from any individual post, allowing us to test whether communicating this assessment changes judgement on the same feed. In our sample drawn from real Twitter events 
[
3
]
, atomic claims about a single fact, such as a count or identity, account for 75.9% of rumour targets and 90.0% of non-rumour controls. Direct-support checks provide a clearer distinction between these groups for atomic claims, showing their relevance to the predominant target type in the sample. Across the full sample, responding to the GM assessment improves the overall accuracy of acceptance decisions in all four evaluated models under both original and anonymized settings, while leaving the feeds unchanged. For public warnings and crisis response, these checks could help prevent manipulated rumours from guiding decisions while directing unresolved claims to further verification. Entity controls further show that regenerating narratives changes responses more than replacing names in otherwise fixed text, underscoring how the presented narrative shapes judgement. For information assistants that summarize and review social events, these findings motivate tracing the relationships behind conclusions and explicitly recording unresolved connections when reports pass to downstream systems, so that repeated agreement is not mistaken for independent evidence.

Results

We evaluated the threat in a simulated social-media environment, where victim agents acted as personal analysts, gathering and summarizing information about unfolding events from public communication channels for users. Generative Montage used GPT-4.1-mini for the Writer, Editor and Director roles to select and organize truthful material from a public evidence pool into narratives designed to steer victims towards a specified rumour target. The resulting posts were introduced into a public feed accessible to the victim, without explicitly presenting the target as an established fact. Each victim first interpreted the feed without being shown the target and reported its conclusions, reasoning and confidence. It then assessed whether the feed supported the specified target. For each target and victim model, we conducted five separately instantiated runs and defined the attack success rate (ASR) as the proportion that accepted the target during this assessment. Finally, to evaluate downstream effects, a judge agent reviewed the original feed alongside the five victim agents’ reports and issued a final judgement on the target.

Using this protocol, we first evaluate attack effectiveness and transfer across 14 models from the GPT

Construction ablations, reasoning prompts and feed-length comparisons examine how attack generation and the victim agent’s reasoning affect acceptance. We then test whether errors persist when downstream decision systems use majority voting or a judge agent to review the victim agents’ outputs. This comparison asks whether agreement and access to the agents’ reasoning help correct unsupported conclusions. To explain how these conclusions arise, we next compare responses to complete feeds and individual posts, identifying fragments that most strongly increase target belief. Comparing their deletion with random removal tests their causal contribution within the original narrative, while transplantation examines whether their influence carries into ordinary reporting. Because influential fragments can also provide valid evidence, we use synthetic worlds with known facts and relations to test whether these response patterns distinguish unsupported narratives from those whose targets are established by the supplied fragments. This distinction between influence and support motivates evaluating GM as a proof-of-concept neuro-symbolic monitor. We test whether communicating its direct-support assessment changes judgement of the same feed, evaluating overall acceptance decisions across rumour targets and non-rumour controls. Cognitive threats across models and events

All 14 evaluated models accepted rumour claims that no individual evidence fragment established, with overall ASR exceeding 65% in twelve models. Mean acceptance reached 74.4% for proprietary models and 70.3% for open-weight models (Fig. 
1
a). Although susceptibility varied across receiving models, high acceptance in both groups shows that attacks constructed with GPT-4.1-mini transfer beyond the model family used for generation. This widespread susceptibility highlights a vulnerability in how agents interpret fragmented evidence: individually truthful observations can be composed into narratives that lend apparent credibility to unsupported claims. An attacker can therefore induce similar errors across different receiving models using a single model to construct the attack. Extended Data Table 
Extended Data
 reports the model and event results, and Supplementary Table 
2

To examine the confidence accompanying this behaviour, we paired each target verdict with the victim agent’s confidence in its earlier open report. High-confidence ASR (HC-ASR) counts runs in which reported confidence was at least 0.8 and the target was subsequently accepted. It reached at least 50% in 56 out of 84 model and event combinations (Fig. 
1
b). These results raise a deeper concern about the conviction with which unsupported interpretations may be maintained and communicated. An agent that becomes overconfident in a conclusion drawn from fragmentary observations may use its own reasoning to justify that conclusion, reinforcing its belief rather than exposing the assumptions that connect the evidence to the target. Its rationale may then make the unsupported interpretation appear well grounded to other agents or human users. Cognitive attacks could thus exploit confident reasoning to sustain a mistaken belief and carry it into downstream judgements.

The examples in Table 
1
 illustrate how victim agents inferred spurious relationships that the evidence left unresolved. In the Charlie Hebdo case, the posts placed Ahmed Merabet near the beginning of the attack and subsequently reported his death. The victim agent connected these observations to conclude that he was the first victim, although neither his early appearance in the narrative nor his reported death established the casualty order. The unsupported step was therefore the transition from his position in the account to his position in the actual sequence of deaths. In the Sydney case, a count of 13 appeared alongside hostage reports, and the victim agent interpreted this number as the hostage count. This reading implicitly assumed that every counted person was a hostage, a relationship that the feed did not establish. Across all six events, plausible connections concerning numerical reference, temporal order, context or intent were incorporated into the accepted narrative without sufficient evidential support. Such connections have a familiar role in narrative understanding: readers infer links between observations to construct a coherent situation model 
[
18
, 
1
]
. However, a plausible connection became a premise for accepting a more specific claim in these cases. The failure arose when the agent treated its own interpretation as though that relationship had been established by the observations.

To analyse how narrative construction contributed to this effect, we compared the complete procedure with simplified variants on Charlie Hebdo. Acceptance reached 77.0% under the complete procedure, compared with 26.8% for a single agent using one prompt. Removing Director-guided revision or Editor sequencing also reduced acceptance (Extended Data Table 
Extended Data
), suggesting that both iterative refinement and narrative presentation contributed to the attack’s effectiveness. These comparisons indicate that susceptibility depended in part on how the material was developed and organized into a persuasive account. On the receiving side, chain-of-thought prompting increased acceptance rate in Qwen2.5-7B and DeepSeek-R1-7B (Extended Data Table 
Extended Data
). Thus, explicitly asking these models to reason through the evidence did not prevent unsupported conclusions. Additional reasoning may instead elaborate an initially plausible interpretation without checking whether its connecting assumptions are grounded, consistent with evidence that reasoning traces can rationalize answers shaped by input biases 
[
45
]
. Our separate ablation analysis further showed that acceptance peaked at intermediate feed lengths before declining (Extended Data Fig. 
Extended Data
). Persuasiveness therefore did not increase consistently as more material was presented: within this evaluation, extending a feed could strengthen or weaken target acceptance. These findings suggest that narrative construction, the receiving model’s reasoning behaviour and feed length interact in ways that cannot be reduced to a simple expectation that more reasoning or more information will improve judgement.

Error propagation from victim agents to downstream decision systems

Having established that individual victim agents accept unsupported conclusions, we next examined whether these errors persist when their outputs inform a downstream decision system. We tested two common routes to a final target judgement. Majority voting 
[
46
]
 combined the five victim agents’ target verdicts, whereas an additional language-model judge 
[
47
]
 reviewed the original feed alongside all five fixed initial reports, including their reasoning and self-reported confidence. The first route relied on agreement among the agents; the second allowed a downstream decision-maker to examine their interpretations against the original evidence. These routes therefore tested whether aggregation or further review could prevent unsupported interpretations from becoming final decisions.

Under majority voting, a target was accepted only when most agents endorsed it, following a rule widely used in human collective decision-making. Yet rumour acceptance remained at 59.6–75.0% across the evaluated model groups (Fig. 
1
c). This result highlights how apparent consensus can obscure the evidential gaps behind a shared conclusion. When several analysts endorse the same rumour, their agreement may be mistaken for independent corroboration, making the claim appear more credible than the underlying observations warrant. Human studies have shown that repeated expressions from a single source can create an impression of broader agreement 
[
16
]
. In our setting, majority voting translates convergent but unsupported interpretations into a final system decision. Such collective endorsement may then discourage further scrutiny by making an unresolved claim appear settled. The resulting risk extends to subsequent information exchange, where other agents or human users may repeat the conclusion on the strength of that apparent consensus, allowing a rumour to circulate with increasing credibility while its evidential basis remains unchanged.

The language-model judge could inspect the original feed alongside all five agents’ structured reports, giving it an opportunity to assess whether their reasoning was grounded in the source material. Nevertheless, rumour acceptance remained at 57.7–69.5%, exceeding half the targets in every evaluated model group. Access to both the evidence and the accompanying explanations therefore did not reliably prevent unsupported conclusions from reaching the final decision. This raises a concern about how persuasive reasoning can shape subsequent review. A structured report may explain how an agent arrived at a conclusion while leaving the crucial connection between the observations and the target unverified. Its self-reported confidence may further make that interpretation appear settled, without resolving the underlying uncertainty. A downstream judge could consequently treat a coherent explanation as sufficient justification, allowing an inferred relationship to pass through review without being established by the evidence. The broader risk is that passing through these stages may give a rumour the appearance of having been checked and validated, increasing its apparent authority when the final decision is communicated to other agents or human users.

To understand how these attacks shape judgement, we moved beyond aggregate success rates to identify which truthful fragments influence the victim’s response and whether that influence persists within the complete narrative. We first measured each post’s effect in isolation, selected the strongest candidate and then modified the feed to test its contribution to the overall judgement. This analysis used a separate corpus containing 223 attack feeds spanning 37 targets and 464 ordinary-report feeds from the same events. We evaluated these feeds with six open-weight models to access token log-probabilities at the first answer position for quantifying changes in target belief. The attack feeds had been selected for success under a reference model’s fixed belief readout, thus the comparison focused on successful attack constructions and ordinary reporting.

A model’s response to a complete feed can conceal substantial differences between its responses to individual posts. Two feeds may produce similar overall target beliefs even when their constituent fragments exert very different influences. To distinguish these patterns, we expressed the probability of answering TRUE as a log-odds 
belief margin
. Positive values favour TRUE, negative values favour FALSE, and larger values indicate a stronger tendency to accept the target. The 
full-feed belief margin
 measures this tendency when all posts are presented together. Each post’s 
belief pull
, instead measures how the margin changes when that post is presented alone, relative to an empty-context baseline. A positive pull therefore increases target belief relative to the baseline, whereas a negative pull decreases it. We then defined 
response dispersion
 as the sample standard deviation across these isolated pulls. Greater dispersion indicates that posts differ more strongly in their individual effects, whether those effects point in the same direction or in opposing directions.

The aforementioned analysis showed that selected fragments influence judgement within their original narratives, while transplantation increased dispersion without consistently strengthening belief in a different feed. These findings, however, do not establish whether the response patterns distinguish misleading inference from valid evidence integration. We therefore designed controlled comparisons in synthetic worlds with known facts and relations, where target truth and evidential support could be checked directly. We first varied fragment selection and narrative construction, and then examined whether similar response patterns arose when the fragments established the target. Alongside the six models used in the fragment analysis, we evaluated Qwen3-8B and Llama-3.1-8B to test whether the response patterns extend to further model generation or different family.

Target promotion and direct support provide distinct diagnostic information

The preceding analyses showed that influential fragments can shape judgement through either valid evidence or unsupported inference. This distinction motivates checking how the presented content supports a target, beyond measuring how strongly it affects the response. We therefore use GM as a proof-of-concept neuro-symbolic monitor before target judgement. Its neural component interprets each post’s relation to the target, while its symbolic component checks whether detected direct support remains after abductive assumptions are withdrawn. GM uses this assessment to flag narratives that strongly promote a target without detected single-post grounding. We first examined what this signal distinguishes across different feed types, before testing whether communicating it changes victim agents’ judgements.

For these experiments, we used a focused set comprising 158 unique rumour targets and 80 non-rumour controls, with both groups composed into montages using the same procedure. The original setting (ORIG) retained the real entities, whereas the anonymized setting (ANON) replaced them with fictional counterparts before regenerating the feeds. Regeneration reduced rumour acceptance, and fixed-text controls showed that differences between generation sources were larger than the effect of replacing names within an otherwise unchanged feed. ORIG and ANON therefore differ in their generated narratives as well as their entity names. Evaluating GM in both settings tests whether its support assessment remains informative across these changes in the presented content. Extended Data Tables

Discussion

Belief formation through narrative coherence

When an agent combines truthful fragments into a narrative, it can mistake a relationship that it has inferred for one that the fragments have established. In our study, removing influential posts indeed weakened rumour belief in their original narratives, whereas transferring them to ordinary reporting did not consistently strengthen belief. Similar variation in responses to individual fragments occurred in both misleading narratives and narratives whose conclusions were supported. Influence therefore helps locate content that shapes judgement, but assessing whether that influence is justified requires checking how the fragments support the conclusion.

Cognitive accounts of judgement help explain this risk by describing how an emerging explanation shapes the interpretation of individual facts 
[
19
, 
20
]
. If coherence guides an agent’s judgement, it may accept a connecting assumption because that assumption makes the narrative fit together. Research on source monitoring addresses the related problem of distinguishing externally supplied information from internally generated interpretations 
[
50
]
. For agents, keeping track of which relationships were reported and which were inferred would allow a plausible explanation to guide further investigation without being presented as a verified conclusion.

Apparent consensus from shared evidence

When several agents reach the same conclusion, their agreement may appear to provide independent confirmation even when they rely on the same evidence. In our shared-feed setting, rumour acceptance remained above 50% under both majority voting and judge review, despite the judge’s access to the original material. These errors show why multi-agent systems need to check whether agreeing reports draw on independent evidence 
[
34
, 
51
]
. Different explanations may still rely on the same unverified connection, leaving the conclusion without additional support. Human studies describe a similar concern, as repeated expressions from one source can create an impression of wider agreement, and social influence can increase confidence without improving collective accuracy 
[
16
, 
52
]
.

As reports are summarized and recirculated, their reliance on the same evidence may become harder to recognize. Agreement between reports can become more visible than their common source, allowing an inferred connection to be repeated as a verified conclusion. Systems that pass summaries between agents could record which fragments support a conclusion and which connections an agent has added, helping later agents distinguish repeated interpretation from independent support. This record would also make it possible to trace which conclusions need correction when an underlying premise is challenged.

Runtime monitoring of evidential support

GM uses salience to assess how strongly a narrative promotes a target and grounding to check whether any individual post directly supports it. Salience distinguished attack montages from chronological reporting, whereas rumour and non-rumour montages generated by the same procedure both strongly promoted their targets. Within this latter comparison, grounding-post counts provided additional information about direct support, as shown in Table 
2
. The monitor therefore raises an alarm only when salience reaches its threshold and no grounding post is detected, focusing attention on the gap between promoting a conclusion and directly supporting it. Communicating this assessment while allowing agents to withhold acceptance improved decision accuracy across all four models, as shown in Fig. 
5
. The feeds remained unchanged, making this a proof of concept for improving acceptance decisions without removing truthful fragments.

Extending this approach to conclusions supported by several fragments requires checking how those fragments jointly establish the claim. For illustration, a post reporting eleven deaths, if accurate, establishes that casualty count, whereas inferring a plane’s rapid descent requires comparing altitude readings taken at different times. A monitor would need to verify that the readings refer to the same aircraft and establish the rate of descent before judging whether it supports the description. More generally, an extended GM could identify the premises needed for a conclusion, link each premise to its source fragment and check the intermediate reasoning steps, drawing on work that represents reasoning through explicit evidence chains 
[
53
, 
54
]
. The aim would be to locate missing or unsupported connections while preserving conclusions that follow from several fragments, even when no single post establishes them.

For these checks to be useful, the monitor’s own support judgements must also be open to verification. The classifier could return the relevant text with each relation label, allowing a separate verifier to assess whether that label follows from the fragment or depends on an added assumption. Evaluating support labels and final acceptance decisions separately on both supported and unsupported narratives would help distinguish more accurate support checking from a general tendency to withhold acceptance. Alongside these runtime checks, machine unlearning could be explored as a way to reduce learned influences that encourage unsupported inference 
[
55
, 
56
]
. Its value for narrative manipulation would depend on identifying which influences to weaken while preserving factual knowledge and the ability to combine evidence correctly.

Prior knowledge in attack and monitoring evaluation

From an attack perspective, rumour acceptance remains a consequential failure even if the victim agent is familiar with the event, particularly if it possesses knowledge that could help it challenge the narrative. Real-event settings therefore remain important for assessing the risks to assistants interpreting public events. In the fixed-text controls, replacing entity names produced smaller changes in target belief than changing the narrative generation source, as shown in Extended Data Table 
Extended Data
. Thus, the comparison between ORIG and ANON also reflects changes in the generated narrative, beyond the replacement of familiar names.

For monitoring, however, prior knowledge may help an agent reject a familiar rumour or influence how the monitor assesses its support. The additional ANON evaluation therefore tested whether GM remained useful with fewer familiar event cues. In target-only probes, event recognition by GPT-4.1-mini fell from 44.5% to 5.5%, confirming reduced recognizability in the model used for monitoring, as shown in Extended Data Table 
Extended Data
. Although final accuracy was higher in ANON, the evidence for GM’s value comes from comparing decisions between baseline and GM response conditions within each setting, where the feeds remain unchanged. The improvements in both settings, shown in Fig. 
5
, indicate that the monitoring benefit extends to regenerated narratives with reduced event recognizability.

Broader implications

For information assistants that summarize public events for users, monitoring needs to address the inferences that connect factual reporting to decisions. A social-media summary may preserve every quoted fact yet add a relationship that changes whom users blame, what they believe has happened or which response they consider necessary. The risk therefore concerns how truthful information is organized and given authority, extending responsibility beyond the accuracy of individual statements. Building on methods that link generated claims to their sources 
[
57
]
, systems could show users which parts of a summary are directly reported and which depend on an unresolved interpretation. A public warning or decision recommendation would then make clear which assumptions require verification before action is taken.

When automated support checks flag a conclusion, human oversight could provide a route to verification. An agent’s 
uncertain
 response following an alarm could prompt a reviewer to check whether the fragments jointly establish the conclusion or whether independent evidence is needed, with retrieval supporting that investigation 
[
58
, 
59
]
. Review could prioritize claims that affect important decisions, while helping agents accept supported conclusions when the monitor misses a valid connection across fragments. Studies of human and AI collaboration show that its benefits depend on the task and the roles assigned to each participant 
[
60
]
. Evaluation should therefore measure how often reviewers correct unsupported decisions or confirm supported conclusions, alongside the time and workload required.

Abstract

Do research agents have taste, the judgment of which experiments are worth running before their outcomes are known? We give six frontier models seven recent NLP papers. Each model, working as a research agent alone or as a team, proposes minimum viable experiments (MVEs), forecasts whether each will pass a threshold it sets and ranks them. A separate executor then attempts every MVE, including those ranked last, and three model auditors judge the results. Of 415 MVEs, 194 are judged sound, valuable and new, but fewer than half of these (73) are also judged beyond the seed paper and non obvious. The agents’ first choices match the auditors’ most valued experiment more often than a random pick, but are not shown to improve on choosing the experiment written first. Their forecasts beat a base rate but are not measurably more accurate than those of a fresh instance of the same model given only the paper and registrations. Teams cost more without a clear overall improvement in proposal quality. The agents produce many worthwhile experiments, but the evidence that their rankings improve the choice of which to run remains limited. The evaluation harness and a dataset of all 415 MVEs, with their forecasts and audited outcomes, are available at 
https://github.com/ArtKanke/rTaste
.

Introduction

Large language model agents can now carry out much of the empirical research workflow, from literature search and implementation to running experiments and writing reports 
(
Lu et al., 2024
; 
Schmidgall et al., 2025
)
. Frontier AI labs report that agents write much of their code and support a rapidly growing number of experiments 
(
Favaro & Clark, 2026
; 
OpenAI, 2026
)
. A risk assessment of agents inside four frontier labs, however, found no evidence that any of them relied on agents to set research agendas or make budget decisions 
(
METR, 2026
)
. The step that has not been delegated is close to what researchers call taste, the judgment of which questions and experiments are worth pursuing before their outcomes are known 
(
Hamming, 1986
)
. In practice this judgment is often exercised through small experiments. A researcher tests a direction cheaply and lets the result decide whether a larger study is needed. Such an experiment is useful only if its outcome is not obvious in advance and the researcher’s expectation about it is accurate enough to act on. An agent that proposes experiments with predictable outcomes or that is confidently wrong about what its experiments will show spends a limited budget poorly even if it runs every experiment correctly.

Existing evaluations address parts of this question. Execution benchmarks specify a task and measure how well an agent completes it 
(
Chan et al., 2025
; 
Starace et al., 2025
)
. Studies of research ideas often judge proposals before execution, although those judgments can change when the ideas are carried out 
(
Si et al., 2026a
)
. Other work tests whether models can predict outcomes or choose among experiments on a given problem 
(
Wen et al., 2025
; 
Foster et al., 2026
; 
Zhang et al., 2026a
)
. We examine these judgments for experiments that agents propose from a recent paper and compare their expectations with the outcomes of every proposal.

We ask what experiments agents propose from a new paper, how accurately they predict the outcomes, and how well they choose among their proposals. We give each model a recent NLP paper, the 
seed paper
, with its code. Working as a research agent, once as a single researcher and once as a research team, the model proposes five 
minimum viable experiments
 (MVEs), inexpensive tests meant to indicate whether a direction deserves further investment. Before any experiment runs, the agent forecasts whether each MVE will meet its registered threshold and ranks the five (Figure 
1
). A separate executor then attempts every MVE, including those the agent ranked last. Three language models audit the results. We also compare the agent with a fresh instance of the same model that receives only the seed paper and registrations and makes the same forecasts and ranking.

Figure 1: 
The study design.

Across seven seed papers and 415 MVEs, 194 are judged sound, valuable and new. On average the four larger models propose about three such MVEs in five, and the two smaller models, GPT-6 Luna and Muse Spark 1.3, about one. Two models, GPT-6 Astra and GPT-6.1 Sol, propose most of the MVEs judged beyond the seed paper and non obvious. A research team does not, on average, propose measurably better MVEs than a single researcher of the same model. The agents judge their own proposals less well. Their forecasts are more accurate than a base rate, but their own reading and search did not measurably improve them over those of the same model judge. The agent’s first choice is the auditors’ most valued MVE more often than a random pick would be. We find no evidence that it is better than the MVE the agent wrote first or than the same model judge’s first choice. We release the evaluation harness and the dataset of all 415 MVEs at 
github.com/ArtKanke/rTaste
.

Related Work

Research agents and benchmarks.

Research agent systems now span the workflow from ideation to written reports 
(
Yamada et al., 2025
; 
Miyai et al., 2026
)
, some organized around a persistent tree of hypotheses and isolated executors 
(
Jin et al., 2026
)
. RE-Bench, AIRS-Bench, and DeepRSI give agents a task with a fixed metric and score the result 
(
Wijk et al., 2025
; 
Lupidi et al., 2026
; 
Rajaghatta et al., 2026
)
. TasteVal, concurrent with our work, measures how efficiently a model’s own experiments reach an expert’s score on a fixed task 
(
Jaffe & Sherburn, 2026
)
. EXP-Bench asks agents to reproduce experiments from published papers 
(
Kon et al., 2026
)
. Other benchmarks give agents research problems drawn from recent or unpublished work 
(
Garikaparthi et al., 2026
; 
Kim et al., 2026
)
. In each, the problem is given to the agent, whereas our agents choose what to test from a seed paper. Studies of such agents report frequent failures to complete experiments, reported results that do not match the underlying code 
(
Chen et al., 2025
; 
Zhang et al., 2026b
)
, and weak judgment about when evidence is sufficient 
(
Kirgis et al., 2026
)
.

Evaluating research ideas.

A second group of work evaluates research ideas directly. Blind comparisons with expert researchers found that large language model ideas were rated more novel than human ideas 
(
Si et al., 2025
)
, and studies of expert preferences found that automated judges reproduce those preferences only partially 
(
Chen et al., 2026
)
 or little better than chance 
(
Baig et al., 2026
)
. Judgments of this kind are made without outcomes. When ideas from the earlier study were executed by researchers, the advantage of the model generated ideas disappeared 
(
Si et al., 2026a
)
. Language models asked to judge the soundness of proposals are also strongly biased toward approval 
(
Ho et al., 2026
)
. Agent generated ideas tend to stay close to their source literature 
(
Tang & Yang, 2026
)
. Other studies judge research foresight against the later literature, asking which directions will be pursued or will gain attention 
(
Li et al., 2026
; 
Tian et al., 2026
; 
Ye et al., 2026
)
. Another study compares a model’s proposed next step in a project with the step its authors actually took 
(
Gong et al., 2026
)
.

Predicting experimental outcomes.

The work closest to ours predicts experimental outcomes before execution. Models can predict which of two published ideas performs better on a benchmark 
(
Mule et al., 2026
)
, which of two candidate programs will score higher 
(
Zheng et al., 2026
)
, or the numeric result of an experiment from its description 
(
Park et al., 2025
)
. Recent work also asks models which training recipe will reach the lowest loss 
(
Ren et al., 2026b
)
 and how much a change to a training or inference recipe will improve a benchmark score 
(
Wang et al., 2026
)
. Some register expected outcomes before pilot experiments and run only the best supported hypotheses in full 
(
Ren et al., 2026a
)
, so their expectations about the other hypotheses are never checked at full scale. Others run every candidate, to train a model that scores hypotheses 
(
Vasu et al., 2025
)
 or to learn from large batches of executed ideas 
(
Si et al., 2026b
)
. Where calibration has been measured for agents, on coding tasks with clear success criteria, agents are overconfident about their own success 
(
Kaddour et al., 2026
; 
Barkan et al., 2025
)
.

Research teams.

Several research systems divide the work among specialized agents that generate, critique, and rank hypotheses 
(
Gottweis et al., 2026
; 
Schmidgall et al., 2026
)
, and a lead agent coordinating parallel subagents has improved performance on research style information tasks at a substantially higher token cost 
(
Hadfield et al., 2025
)
. Studies suggest that such gains depend on the task and on how coordination is organized 
(
Kim et al., 2025
)
 and that when total computation is held equal a single agent often matches or exceeds multi agent designs 
(
Tran & Kiela, 2026
)
.

Experimental Setup

3.1 
Seed papers

We use seven NLP papers (arXiv cs.CL) with released code, taken from the daily papers list on Hugging Face
1
1

1

https://huggingface.co/papers
 and first made public between 3 August and 24 September 2026. We chose papers this recent so that little follow up work exists for an agent to find and repeat. The selection also excludes work that requires large GPU training runs or models with more than 8B parameters. The full list can be found in Appendix 
A
. Agents receive the paper’s full text, an image of every page, and read access to its code repository. We did not provide a summary, a suggested research gap or direction, or a hypothesis.

3.2 
Agents and harnesses

We evaluate six models at maximum reasoning effort: GPT-6 Astra, Claude Fable 5.1, Claude Opus 5.5, GPT-6.1 Sol, GPT-6 Luna, and Muse Spark 1.3.
2
2

2

              
              
              
            A reply cut off at the output limit is requested once more at medium effort. This happened for the lead of the Claude Opus 5.5 team on six of the seven seed papers, so 30 of the 415 registrations were written at medium effort.
 Evaluation was conducted between 6 and 8 October 2026. Each model works under two harnesses, so each seed paper has twelve 
conditions
. We refer to GPT-6 Luna and Muse Spark 1.3 as the two smaller models and to the other four as the larger models. The judges (Section 
3.3
) also run at maximum effort, and the executors, novelty judges, auditors, and reviewers at high effort.

A 
single researcher
 (SR) works in one continuous context from reading the seed paper to committing its forecasts. A 
research team
 (RT) divides the same work among separate instances of the same model. Two proposers each read the seed paper, search the literature, and draft experiments independently. A critic examines the pooled drafts once for scientific validity and for feasibility. A lead then keeps, revises, or merges the drafts into five experiments, commits them, and makes the team’s forecasts. The critic and the lead have no tools, so the lead has not itself searched the literature or read the code. The comparison asks whether two drafts and one round of critique help a model propose better MVEs than one continuous context. Under both harnesses, agents have the same limits and search the literature with the same arXiv tool. A comparison between models is therefore not also a comparison between their providers’ web tools.

3.3 
Protocol

The agent makes two commitments before any experiment runs (Figure 
1
). First, it proposes five minimum viable experiments. Each MVE is specified in a short registration with a hypothesis, its closest prior work, and one 
registered event
: a stated statistic passes a threshold set in advance. Second, the agent gives each MVE a probability that its registered event will occur and ranks the five from most to least worth pursuing. We call the MVE it ranks highest its 
first choice
. The proposing agent knows that all proposed MVEs will run, so its ranking states a priority rather than deciding what runs.

We also ask each of the six models, as a 
judge
 in a new context, for the same forecasts and ranking. They see the seed paper and the five registrations. We do not provide the code, the agent’s reasoning or forecasts, or which model proposed the MVEs, and they cannot search. We call the judge from the agent’s own model its 
same model judge
. We run the MVEs of a seed paper only after all twelve conditions have committed.

After the experiments are committed and before execution, two 
novelty judges
, Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6.1 Sol, use the same literature search tool to label each MVE as equivalent to existing work, a close variant, or distinct. An MVE labeled equivalent or a close variant still runs. To see whether an MVE is an obvious follow up, we also ask each of the six models, given only the seed paper and no search, to list ten follow up experiments it would propose. The two novelty judges check each MVE against these lists, and we count it as an 
obvious follow up
 if either finds it there.

3.4 
Execution and evaluation

Executors.

One of two executors, Claude Opus 5.5 or GPT-6.1 Sol, implements each MVE in a new context. The same two executors serve all six models, so a smaller model’s proposals are not held back by its own implementation. The executor sees the seed paper, its code, and the one MVE it implements. We did not provide the other four MVEs, the ranking, the forecasts, or the name of the model that proposed the MVE. All MVEs of one model on a seed paper go to the same executor and the executors alternate across seed papers. Because both executors are among the six models, 58 of the 412 delivered MVEs were implemented by a separate instance of the model that proposed them. We run every MVE with the same limits, no matter its rank. The executor works on a separate machine with one A100 GPU for at most 45 minutes, may run open models of up to 8B parameters, and may not call hosted models.
3
3

3

                
                
                
              On the seventh seed paper, 53 of its 60 MVEs were implemented and rerun on H200 machines with the same limits and prompts.
 If it refuses or exceeds its limits, the other executor makes one independent attempt. If both fail, the MVE is not delivered.

Evaluation.

We then delete the results that came with the executor’s submission and rerun its evaluation on a new machine the executor never used. The rerun determines whether the registered event occurred. An MVE is 
delivered
 when the rerun ends without error and writes a number. The rerun repeats the executor’s own code, so whether that code is right is left to the auditors.

Audits.

Three auditors, Claude Opus 5.5, GPT-6.1 Sol, and Grok 4.7, judge every delivered MVE. Grok 4.7 is not one of the six models we study and does not execute. The auditors see the registration, the executor’s notes, the output of the rerun, and the files it left. They do not see the seed paper or who proposed or implemented the MVE. Each auditor judges whether the MVE is faithful to its registration and valid and rates its research value from 0 to 3. We take the majority of the three judgments of faithfulness and validity and the median of the three research values. We call an MVE 
sound
 when it is faithful and valid, 
valuable
 when its research value is 2 or 3 and 
new
 when neither novelty judge found close prior work. The auditors also rank the five MVEs of each condition by research value. The MVE with the best mean rank is the auditors’ 
most valued MVE
, against which we score the agent’s first choice.

Reviews.

After the study, we added a review of every delivered MVE. Three reviewers, the same models as the auditors, read the seed paper’s text, the registration, and what the auditors were shown, but none of the forecasts, rankings, audits, or novelty labels. They answered fixed questions: how the MVE relates to the seed paper, how obvious its idea is, and what the evidence says about the hypothesis.

We count an MVE as sound, valuable and new when it meets all three (Section

). Nearly all MVEs were delivered and most were judged sound, so most of those that fall short repeat prior work or have low research value. Because the seed papers are recent, new mostly means not yet published: most MVEs on a list of obvious follow ups are also new. The four larger models propose about three such MVEs in five and the two smaller models about one. The smaller models’ MVEs often run as registered but leave the question open. For each sound MVE, the reviewers stated what the evidence says about the hypothesis, and we call the evidence

Most MVEs build on the seed paper rather than check it, and few open a new direction. By the reviewers’ answers, 66% of the delivered MVEs build on the paper, 24% check one of its claims, and 5% open a direction that the paper merely suggests (Table

(
Nguyen-Tri et al., 2026
)
, a method for faster decoding whose appendix gives a correctness argument, all eight conditions of the four larger models wrote the same check of that argument first.

Two models more often go beyond the paper with an experiment that is not obvious. We call an MVE 
non obvious
 when at least two reviewers say that few or none of ten researchers would list it and that the paper neither reports nor names it, and 
beyond the paper and non obvious
 when at least two reviewers each say this and also that it builds on the paper or opens a new direction. This rating comes from the reviewers and differs from the lists of obvious follow ups: a fifth of these MVEs are also on a list. GPT-6 Astra and GPT-6.1 Sol proposed two thirds of them (Table 
1
), and they lead for each reviewer separately. Within seed papers, their share of such MVEs exceeds that of Claude Fable 5.1 and Claude Opus 5.5 by 48 percentage points (95% CI 29 to 66) and is higher on all seven seed papers. GPT-6 Luna, a smaller model from the same provider, builds on the paper as often, but the reviewers rate few of its ideas non obvious. MVEs beyond the paper and non obvious are sound, valuable and new at least as often as the others, but their registered events occur less often. Of the 194 sound, valuable and new MVEs, 73 are beyond the paper and non obvious. The reviewers recommend a larger study more often for the MVEs of Claude Fable 5.1 and Claude Opus 5.5 than for those of GPT-6 Astra and GPT-6.1 Sol.

4.3 
Registered events and evidence

Each registration commits the agent to one registered event before the MVE runs and it is natural to read the event as the success of the experiment. For each sound MVE, we compare whether the event occurred with the reviewers’ reading of the evidence.

Whether the event occurs depends on how the agent defines it. In most of their MVEs, GPT-6 Astra and GPT-6.1 Sol compare a bound of a confidence interval with the threshold, rather than a point estimate (Table 
7
), and the other four models rarely do. The events of GPT-6 Astra and GPT-6.1 Sol occur in 25% of their sound MVEs, compared with 52% for Claude Fable 5.1 and Claude Opus 5.5 (Table 
1
). On one seed paper 
(
Liang et al., 2026
)
, four conditions tested whether a model’s own prediction errors harm decoding more than random errors of the same size, and all three reviewers read all four results as support for it. Three of the conditions had set the threshold at a difference of 5 percentage points and their events occurred. The fourth had set it at 8 points, and its event did not.

When the registered event does not occur, the evidence is often still informative. The reviewers judged the evidence informative for 50% of the sound MVEs whose event did not occur, mostly as evidence against the hypothesis. In the other direction, they judged the evidence inconclusive for 20% of the sound MVEs whose event occurred.

0.006, with a 95% CI from 
0.021 to 0.009 (Table

Both forecasts are more accurate than a base rate that gives every MVE the event rate of the other six seed papers (Brier score 0.240), and the mean of the six judges’ probabilities is more accurate than the agent (0.179 against 0.192). The agent has seen more than its registrations state, but it is not shown to know more about its events than its registrations tell a reader. The agents are also not uniformly overconfident: GPT-6 Astra, GPT-6.1 Sol, GPT-6 Luna and Muse Spark 1.3 state higher probabilities than the rates at which their events occur, while Claude Fable 5.1 and Claude Opus 5.5 state lower ones.

4.5 
First choices

The agent’s first choice is the auditors’ most valued MVE in 38% of conditions, where a random pick gives 20%. The MVE that the agent wrote first and the same model judge’s first choice are the most valued about as often, and we find no evidence that the agent’s first choice beats either: its advantage over the MVE written first is 3 percentage points (95% CI 
8 to 15). The first choice is also sound, valuable and new no more often than a random pick.

Agents register their obvious experiments first: the MVE written first is on a list of obvious follow ups in 61% of conditions, against 35% for the fifth. The agents then rank largely in the order in which they wrote them, and their first choice is the MVE they wrote first in 47% of conditions, where 20% would be expected if order did not matter. The event of the first choice occurs in 47% of conditions and that of the last choice in 29%, so the first choice is often an experiment likely to come out as registered. The auditors also rank sound MVEs whose event occurred higher, mostly because these MVEs give clearer evidence.

The judges rank in the same way: they too put the MVE written first on top in 39% of conditions on average. They and the auditors also rank obvious follow ups higher, and an agent’s ranking agrees more with the same model judge and with other judges from the same provider than with judges from other providers. On average, a judge’s first choice is an MVE beyond the paper and non obvious less often than a random pick would be, while the agent’s own first choice is one about as often.

4.6 
Single researchers and teams

A team proposes more sound, valuable and new MVEs than a single researcher of the same model, 0.71 more of five on average, but the difference is not statistically significant (95% CI 
0.10 to 1.52; Table 
1
). The difference is largest for the two smaller models, about one more of five, at about the cost of a single researcher. Claude Opus 5.5 working alone does as well as its team.

A team changes little in what is proposed. Its MVEs check the paper, match the lists of obvious follow ups, and are beyond the paper and non obvious about as often as a single researcher’s. They are rated non obvious somewhat more often (Table 
1

). The auditors judge 18% of the MVEs implemented by the Claude Opus 5.5 executor not sound, compared with 3% of those implemented by the GPT-6.1 Sol executor, while the evidence is informative equally often under both.

5 
Discussion

We began this study to see how many good small experiments frontier models can propose when a recent paper is their source of inspiration. By a good MVE we mean one that is small and not obvious and that opens a direction worth a larger study. Most of the experiments were sound, but most either tested the seed paper itself or extended it in a way that many researchers would propose, and few opened a new direction. Current agents appear better suited to examining a paper and taking its obvious next steps than to moving beyond it. That is useful in itself, since a new paper’s claims can be checked under controls and stricter measurements for a few dollars each, but it is a different capability from proposing new research, and evaluations of research agents should measure the two separately.

The models differ less in how often their experiments are sound than in what kind of experiment they propose. GPT-6 Astra and GPT-6.1 Sol more often go beyond the paper with an experiment that is not obvious and register stricter tests, which pass less often. Claude Fable 5.1 and Claude Opus 5.5 stay closer to the paper and register tests that pass more often. The experiments of the two smaller models more often leave the question open. Choosing a model is therefore also choosing a kind of experiment. Because the price of proposing differs far more between models than the cost of running an MVE, a small model working as a team with a capable executor yields about as many sound and valuable experiments per dollar as the most efficient large model, though fewer beyond the paper.

In our study every experiment ran with the same limits, so the agents’ rankings stated priorities and did not decide what would be implemented. Those rankings followed the order in which the agents wrote their MVEs and favored the ones likely to pass. A natural next study gives an agent a fixed research budget to allocate among its own MVEs and to revise after the first results, and asks whether the budget goes to the MVEs that turn out sound, valuable and non obvious. The same budget given to human researchers would show how their allocations compare and provide a human baseline. This would extend the evaluation from what an agent proposes and expects to how it spends a limited budget, the decision in which researchers exercise their taste.

6 
Limitations

All labels in this study come from models, so we lack a human baseline. The auditors judged soundness and value after seeing the outcomes and two of the three are models we also evaluate and use as executors. Every MVE was attempted regardless of its rank, so the ranking did not decide what ran. An agent whose ranking decides what runs may rank with more reasoning. Each registration had to be complete enough for a separate executor, so part of the forecasting result may be built into the design. Each condition ran once, so we have no measure of how much a result would change on a second run.

7 
Conclusion

We studied whether research agents can evaluate and predict the outcomes of their own experiments before they are run. Given a recent NLP paper, six frontier models proposed small experiments, forecast whether the result of each would pass a threshold they set and ranked them. A separate executor attempted every proposed experiment.

We find that the agents propose experiments that are often sound, valuable and new. However, their own reading and search did not measurably improve their forecasts or rankings over those of the same model judge, which read only the seed paper and the registrations. The evaluation harness and the dataset are available at 
https://github.com/ArtKanke/rTaste
.

A.3 
Implementation compute

The agent was told the execution environment and its limits before it registered. Every attempt at an MVE had the
same limits. An executor worked on one machine with an A100 GPU, 12 CPU cores and 142 GB of
memory, and had no keys for hosted API models. Experiments could run open models of 8B parameters or
fewer. The machine ran for at most 45 minutes from the executor’s first command, the implementation had 90 minutes in all, and the rerun on a new machine had 35 minutes. The executor had 60 model calls. Spending limits and the 90 minutes of an implementation are checked between model calls. On the seventh seed paper,
53 of its 60 MVEs were implemented and rerun on H200 machines with the same limits. If an attempt delivers nothing, the other executor makes one independent
attempt. A failure of a machine is not an attempt and is repeated.

Appendix B 
Labels from models

B.1 
The audits

Each auditor judges whether the MVE is faithful to its registration, whether its evidence is valid, and its research
value on a scale from 0 to 3 (the prompt is in Appendix 
D
). The rule takes the majority of
the three auditors on faithfulness and validity and the median of their research values.

Planted defects.

We checked the auditors once, on seed paper 1, before any later audit existed. Claude
Sonnet 5.5, a model that was not used in the study, inserted one known defect into copies of ten delivered MVEs.
Each copy went through the rerun and the three audits again, in new contexts. The majority of three flagged 8 of the
10 copies and none of their 10 originals.

Agreement between auditors.

They agree moderately on research value and validity and poorly on faithfulness.

The rerun.

The rerun repeats the executor’s own code on a new machine with its results deleted. Of the
407 delivered MVEs with both the executor’s own value and the rerun value, 335 have identical values and 403 the same
registered event. For all 412 delivered MVEs, the saved event equals the registered relation and threshold applied to
the rerun value.

B.2 
Novelty

Two novelty judges, Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6.1 Sol, each searched arXiv as of the day of the evaluation and labeled each MVE as equivalent to existing work, a close variant, or distinct. Claude Opus 5.5 labeled 387 of the 415 MVEs distinct
and 28 close variants. GPT-6.1 Sol labeled 298 distinct, 115 close variants and 2 equivalent. They agree with kappa
0.27.

Appendix C 
The review

We added one stage after all seven seed papers had been executed and audited. A reviewer received the seed paper as text without page images, the registration, and
what an auditor was shown of the delivered experiment. We did not provide which models proposed or implemented the MVE, the forecast, ranking, audit, novelty label or follow up list. Table 
9
 lists the questions.

Table 9: 
The questions for the reviewers.

Question

Output

What does the experiment ask?

one sentence

Does the paper report the experiment, or name it as left undone?

reported, named, neither (with a copied passage)

How does the hypothesis relate to the paper?

reruns, checks, varies, explains, builds on, new direction

On how many of ten researchers’ lists of follow ups would the idea be?

most, some, few, none

Who would do or believe something different given a clear answer?

nobody, a few groups, one topic, several topics

Does the plan rest on a statement about the paper that the paper shows to be false?

yes (with a copied passage), no, nothing stated

As designed, could the experiment answer its question?

yes, only if something else holds, no

What came out?

one sentence with the numbers

Did something the test rested on hold?

held, failed, cannot tell

What does the run say about the hypothesis?

for, against, mixed, inconclusive, cannot judge

What should a careful researcher do next?

larger study, one more experiment, repair, stop, no decision

We take the answer that at least two of the three reviewers give. For how the hypothesis relates to the paper (Table 
2
), the review prompt counts a test of a property or a side effect of the paper’s method that the paper makes no claim about as building on the paper. We call an MVE non obvious when at least two reviewers say that few or none of ten researchers would list it and that the paper neither reports nor names it; an answer of reported or named counts only when its copied passage is found in the paper. An MVE is beyond the paper and non obvious when at least two reviewers each say that it is non obvious and that it builds on the paper or opens a new direction. The evidence is informative when at least two reviewers read it as for, against, or mixed.

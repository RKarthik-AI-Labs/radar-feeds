摘要

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is now widely used across industries, including architecture, engineering and construction (AEC), and research on RAG security is growing rapidly; yet the security of building-regulation knowledge bases has received little study. This thesis therefore reports a pre-registered experiment on one fully instrumented LightRAG system built on the UK Approved Documents. Using a 150-item benchmark labeled with safety consequences and tampering directions, it evaluates four attacks (text layer, graph layer, fabricated amendment narrative and a defense-aware adaptive attacker) together with a three-layer retrieval–graph–generation defense in a full factorial design. Text-level poisoning succeeded, with success rising as the budget increased. Graph-level poisoning was less effective, contrary to the registered hypothesis. The full defense met the registered criterion and six of the seven configurations met its threshold, but those containing the generation layer did so by refusing 69–80 percent of poisoned queries, at a cost of up to 74 points of clean accuracy, whereas provenance-based retrieval filtering produced no measurable loss of clean accuracy in the configuration used. A second experimental batch showed that excluding a legitimate unregistered amendment caused no measurable loss of accuracy either, because its wording reached the generator through the graph's entity and relationship descriptions, the route the poison took, which no layer inspects: the three layers act on text chunks, anchored relation edges and cited evidence, whereas the descriptions carry no document provenance. The layers interacted so strongly that the contribution of each cannot be isolated. A reported zero attack success rate is therefore not evidence of detection unless the refusal rate is reported with it: zero arises either because provenance filtering removes every document outside the manifest without judging its content, or because the system refuses. In the registered batch the generation layer never returned a contradiction verdict, only neutral ones (cannot be confirmed). Layered defenses must therefore be evaluated factorially, and RAG assistants for regulatory knowledge require corpus admission control and version-aware verification before they can support safety-critical decisions.

Keywords:
 Retrieval-augmented generation; Knowledge poisoning; LightRAG; Building regulations; Defense evaluation

Summary

This thesis examines whether a retrieval-augmented question-answering system for building regulations can be poisoned, and whether a layered defense can stop such poisoning without destroying the system's usefulness. The experiments use LightRAG, which answers from text chunks and from an automatically built knowledge graph, over the UK Approved Documents and the Manual to the Building Regulations. The question matters in this domain because a wrong escape-stair width or guarding height arrives in the same confident, well-cited form as a correct one, and because regulatory corpora are assembled from many sources and updated by amendment, which gives material of uncertain provenance realistic ways into a knowledge base.

The research question was decomposed into four sub-questions, covering the text layer, the graph layer, the defense and the unevenness of harms, and ten hypotheses with their tests and thresholds were registered on 4 October 2026, before the first attack run; the confirmatory family of seven tests was protected with Holm correction. A 150-item benchmark was written from the corpus, each item anchored to the provision it rests on and labeled with a safety-consequence weight and a paired loosening or tightening mutation. A poisoned query counted as a success only when the answer matched none of the required values, contained the attacker's value and was not a refusal. Four attacks were implemented: PoisonedRAG-style text injection, GRAGPoison-style shared-relation poisoning of the graph, a fabricated “amendment booklet” narrative and a defense-aware adaptive attacker. A three-layer defense (provenance-aware retrieval filtering, anchor-based graph verification and NLI citation checking) was evaluated in all eight combinations of its layers, each configuration run three times.

Text-level poisoning succeeded on 33 of 150 targets at five passages per target, a rate of 0.220 against a registered bound of 0.10 (p = 1.17

5
), and its success rose with the budget, although the poison was already retrieved every time at a budget of one passage. Graph-level poisoning succeeded on 22 of 150 targets, the opposite of the registered prediction, so the graph was not the softer target. The full defense met its registered criterion and six of the seven configurations met its threshold. However, every configuration containing the NLI layer did so by refusing 69–80 percent of poisoned queries, at a cost of up to 74 points of clean accuracy, whereas provenance filtering showed no measurable loss of clean accuracy in the configuration used, by a margin of only 0.36 points; a registered variant with a different graph-side path lost 32 points. The layers interacted strongly, the adaptive attacker gained nothing, and the fabricated amendment notices were repeated in all 18 answers, often in answers that value-based scoring judged correct.

The harms were also uneven in ways that a single rate hides. Weighting errors by their safety consequence barely changed the error rate (a mean tilt of +0.005, within its uncertainty), and neither loosening nor tightening mutations were favored (ten discordant pairs each way). Under poisoning, strict accuracy fell by 21.6 points while the share of strongly assertive answers fell by only 4.3 points: wrong answers were stated no less confidently than right ones, so correctness could not be judged from tone. And on neither of the two items asking which instrument a value belongs to, in any of the three indexes, did the system name the instrument that governs it.

A second experimental batch (6–8 November 2026) sharpened these findings. Excluding a legitimate amendment from the manifest caused no measurable loss of accuracy, because the filter withholds only an unregistered document's text chunks, while the amendment's wording reached the prompt through the graph's entity and relationship descriptions in 14 of the 16 measurable items, the route the poison used. With the graph removed, text-level poisoning remained effective. Under poisoning, the model's self-rated confidence became markedly less calibrated. An LLM judge from a different provider, used as an independent second scorer, agreed with the strict rubric on 50 of 58 explanatory answers.

These results change what a low attack success rate can be taken to mean. Under the defense, a zero arose in two ways: provenance filtering removed every document outside the manifest without judging its content, and the generation layer refused; in the registered batch it never returned a contradiction verdict, only neutral ones. The graph, in turn, proved to be a second attack surface rather than a second line of defense, because its descriptions carry no document provenance and no layer inspects them.

The thesis concludes that a low attack success rate under defense must be reported together with the refusal rate and clean accuracy, that a layered defense must be evaluated with a full factorial design, and that regulatory RAG systems need controlled corpus admission and version-aware verification. For organizations, this means admitting documents, including official amendments, only through an authenticated and logged process enforced before indexing, requiring answers to state the instrument, edition and commencement date they rely on, and keeping human verification for safety-critical values. For researchers, it means reporting the refusal share and the clean-accuracy cost beside every attack success rate.

These findings describe one system (LightRAG 1.5.8), one serving model, one jurisdiction's regulations in one language and one curated experimental index; they may not transfer to open corpora or to systems with different retrieval parameters.

Glossary

Budget M:
 the number of poison passages injected for one target question (N in PoisonedRAG).

ASR (attack success rate):
 the share of poisoned queries whose answer omits the correct value, contains the attacker's value and is not a refusal.

Anchor table:
 a frozen set of structured assertions (subject, relation, comparator, value, unit) extracted from the authoritative text and used by the graph-side defense.

Disruption:
 removal of the correct value from the answer, whether or not the attacker's value replaced it.

ECE (expected calibration error):
 the share-weighted gap between stated confidence and observed accuracy across confidence levels (Equation 4.11).

LLM judge:
 a second, independent scorer that rates each answer's consistency with the provision on a 1–5 scale without seeing the rubric or the experimental condition (Section 4.10).

LightRAG:
 an open-source graph-based RAG framework that retrieves from text chunks and from an automatically extracted entity–relation graph.

NLI (Natural Language Inference):
 A model that classifies whether a premise entails, contradicts, or is neutral to a hypothesis; used here to check the relationship between an answer sentence and its cited evidence.

Plain accuracy:
 accuracy over all records, with a refusal counted as a wrong answer.

Strict accuracy:
 accuracy over answered records only.

Retreat:
 a generation-layer outcome in which the answer is withheld or replaced by a conservative reply because the NLI checker cannot confirm it against the cited evidence; the retreat rate is the share of records whose third-layer check triggered a retreat.

Chapter 1: Introduction and Background

1.1 Research Topics and Key Points

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed to answer questions about technical regulation, usually through retrieval-augmented generation (RAG): the system retrieves passages relevant to the query and conditions the generator on them, so that the answer can be tied to a source. For building regulations, which are long, frequently revised and written in a register where a single value carries legal weight, a system that can point to the paragraph it relied on is easier to trust and audit.

The same property creates an attack surface: whatever the retriever can reach, the generator reads as evidence. An adversary who can place documents into the store needs no access to the model, its prompt or its operator. PoisonedRAG showed that a handful of such passages can redirect a RAG system's answers to attacker-chosen targets (W. Zou et al., 2025).

This thesis studies that problem in one concrete and safety-relevant setting. Its object is LightRAG, an open-source RAG framework that combines vector retrieval over text chunks with an automatically constructed entity–relation graph (Z. Guo et al., 2025); the source code used is listed in Appendix B. Its corpus is the Approved Documents published by the UK government, together with the government's Manual to the Building Regulations. It asks how an adversary who can contribute documents can make such a system return a wrong value for a specific provision, and whether a defense spread across retrieval, graph and generation can prevent that without destroying its usefulness for honest queries.

1.2 Motivation

In practice, the architecture, engineering and construction (AEC) sector has begun to adopt LLM-based tools for regulatory work. Uhm et al. (2025) evaluated retrieval-augmented LLMs for generating construction safety information and found retrieval to improve the reliability of the output; Shi et al. (2025) combined a fine-tuned LLM with retrieval to turn building rules into draft scripts for automated compliance checking; and J. Chen et al. (2026) adapted general-purpose LLMs to building-code analysis by instruction tuning rather than domain-specific pre-training. The same pattern is visible in security operations, where RAG now supports vulnerability risk perception and penetration testing (Gu et al., 2026; Jiang et al., 2025). These studies are concerned with accuracy under honest conditions. None of them asks what happens when part of the document store is controlled by someone who wants the system to be wrong.

This question matters more in this domain than in many others. A wrong escape-stair width or guarding height arrives in the same confident, well-cited form as a correct one, so the reader cannot detect it, and its consequence may be a building that does not protect its occupants.

Regulatory corpora are also assembled from many sources and updated by amendment, which gives material of uncertain provenance realistic ways into a knowledge base.

The second motivation is methodological. Attack success rates in the RAG poisoning literature are reported under different scoring rules, corpora, retrievers and conventions for refusals, and both a recent security benchmark and a recent review argue for unified evaluation settings and benchmarks precisely because evaluation practice has been fragmented (X. Liang et al., 2025; Mu et al., 2026, p. 37). Questions such as whether a graph layer makes a system harder to poison, or what each component of a layered defense contributes, have therefore been answered by juxtaposing papers rather than by controlled comparison on one system.

1.3 Gaps in Existing Literature

The first is a gap of domain. The closest graph-RAG security studies use general-purpose or non-engineering corpora such as multi-hop question answering, medical text and encyclopedic material (J. Liang et al., 2026; Q. Chen et al., 2026; Yan et al., 2026), and the broadest RAG security benchmarks omit graph-based systems (X. Liang et al., 2025). In a building-regulation knowledge base, by contrast, an attack can be scored against a specific provision value whose direction and consequence can be stated, yet research in this setting remains limited.

The second is a gap of comparison. Text-layer and graph-layer attacks have each been shown to work on their own testbeds, but whether shared-relation poisoning is more or less effective than passage injection at a matched budget has not been tested on one index under one scoring rule. Defenses are likewise mostly evaluated one at a time (Xiang et al., 2024; Alizadeh Noughabi et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2026). The layered defenses that report the contribution of each layer combine text-side layers on a vector-only pipeline (Kumar et al., 2026), so whether retrieval-, graph- and generation-side layers compose or interfere in a graph-based system is unknown.

The third is a gap of measurement. Most papers lead with a binary attack success rate (ASR) and rarely report the share of queries a defense declines to answer, so a defense that stopped the attack is hard to tell from a system that stopped answering; the rate also ignores whether the attacked provisions were safety-critical, whether a requirement was loosened or tightened, and whether wrong answers sound as certain as right ones. Studies of attack severity (M. Zou et al., 2026) and of verbal confidence (J. Liu et al., 2026) suggest that these dimensions matter, but they have not been examined in a domain-specific knowledge base such as a regulatory one.

1.4 Research Objectives and Research Questions

The thesis aims to produce an empirically grounded and reproducible security evaluation of a graph-based RAG system for building regulations, covering both attack and defense. Following the approved proposal, the objectives are:

1. to characterize the poisoning attack surface of LightRAG's dual vector–graph architecture on a real regulatory corpus;

2. to implement representative attacks against the text layer and the graph layer, together with a qualitative narrative attack and a defense-aware adaptive attacker, and to measure their effectiveness;

3. to implement a three-layer retrieval–graph–generation defense and evaluate it factorially, so that the contribution and interaction of each layer can be estimated;

4. to build a reusable benchmark of regulation questions with safety-consequence labels and directional poison seeds; and

5. to evaluate outcomes beyond binary ASR, namely safety-consequence weighting, directional bias, confidence–correctness alignment and natural version conflict.

These objectives serve a single research question, stated in the proposal and retained here without change:

In a LightRAG system built on current UK building regulations, which poisoning strategies achieve clause-level retrieval hijacking and generation misdirection through its vector and graph layers, and how should a defense combining retrieval-side anomaly filtering, graph-side evidence verification and generation-side fact verification be designed so that the attack success rate is reduced to an acceptable level while clean question-answering accuracy is essentially maintained?

Chapter 3 decomposes this question into four sub-questions and states the hypotheses that were registered before any experimental run.

1.5 Significance

Academically, the study offers a within-system comparison of attacks on two layers of a graph-based RAG architecture and a factorial measurement of a layered defense under one scoring rule, together with a measurement argument for any defended RAG system: a zero attack success rate is not evidence of detection unless the refusals behind it are reported. Practically, it shows organizations how building-regulation question-answering assistants fail under adversarial input and which defenses work at little cost; the benchmark and scripts form a reusable testbed.

1.6 Research Setting and Data Collection Strategy

The UK Approved Documents were chosen as the research setting for four reasons. First, the corpus states values: widths in millimeters, distances in meters, periods of fire resistance in minutes, each attached to a numbered paragraph or table. A value can be checked, so a successful attack can be scored as the replacement of a specific value rather than as a vague change of tone. Second, many values carry a direction, so that a tampered value either loosens or tightens a requirement. Third, the guidance is layered by amendment, so that several official editions of the same provision can coexist, which creates genuine ambiguity about which instrument governs. Fourth, the documents are public and freely available, which allows the corpus to be redistributed and the experiment to be repeated.

The data were collected through controlled experiments rather than through observation of a deployed system. The official documents were downloaded from GOV.UK, registered in a manifest with their hashes and indexed by LightRAG; all confirmatory experiments use the resulting 850-chunk index. A 150-item benchmark was frozen before the first attack, every attack ran against an isolated copy of the index, and every answer and score was logged per item and repeat (Chapter 4).

Chapter 2: Literature Review and Theoretical Framework

This chapter has two parts. The literature review (Sections 2.1 to 2.5) is organized around the questions this thesis asks rather than paper by paper; the theoretical framework (Section 2.6) states the definitions and commitments on which the design rests.

2.1 Conceptual Framework

The study models a graph-based RAG system as an information path with three stages. 
Retrieval
 selects candidate evidence: text chunks ranked by embedding similarity and, in a graph-based system, entities and relations reached from query keywords. At the 
graph
 stage, entities, typed relations and their descriptions extracted at indexing time are assembled into the context. At 
generation
, the LLM composes an answer from that context and, in LightRAG, cites its source documents (Z. Guo et al., 2025).

A poisoning adversary enters this path at indexing time by contributing documents. A defense can filter what is retrieved, verify what the graph asserts or check what is generated; and an answer can be right, wrong or withheld, so the measurement must account for all three outcomes.

2.2 Text-Level Poisoning of Retrieval-Augmented Generation

The foundational result for this study is PoisonedRAG (W. Zou et al., 2025, p. 9). The attacker crafts a few passages per target question that satisfy a retrieval condition, ranking among the top results for the query, and a generation condition, leading the LLM to the attacker's chosen answer. In the black-box variant the retrieval condition is met by prepending the target question itself to the malicious text. Five passages per target achieved high success rates on several benchmarks. A standalone reproduction reported that success diluted as the knowledge base grew from tens to over a thousand documents (J. Lin, 2026), and another study carried content poisoning into deployed application frameworks, where normal-looking reference content induced harmful answers in most attempts across five open models (Zeng, 2025).

Subsequent work has varied the mechanism along two axes. The first is optimization: Li et al. (2026) plan adversarial passages globally with Monte Carlo tree search, Z. Wang et al. (2026) refine them word by word against the retriever while keeping them fluent, Qian (2026) hijacks retrieval with poisons that preserve the semantics of the surrounding text, Jung et al. (2026) camouflage adversarial content among benign chunks, and Thornton (2026) shows that a poison's effectiveness depends on the corpus it enters. The second axis is the target of the attack: Sun et al. (2026) aim at the knowledge claim rather than the final answer, and Miao et al. (2026) poison the chain of thought rather than the evidence.

Two conclusions follow. Text-level poisoning with a small budget is well established, so the open questions concern its boundaries; and the unit of measurement has converged on per-question scoring against a preset wrong answer, which this thesis inherits so that its results are comparable in kind.

2.3 Graph-Based RAG as the Attack Surface

LightRAG builds its index by prompting an LLM to extract entities and relations from each chunk and stores the merged result as a graph beside vector indexes. At query time it retrieves along a local path, anchored on entities, and a global path, anchored on relations and themes; the hybrid mode combines both with chunk retrieval (Z. Guo et al., 2025). It has already been used in security applications, for example to model an industrial messaging protocol (Y. Chen et al., 2026).

A common intuition holds that the graph layer is defensive, because structure is harder to fabricate than prose. The attack literature has tested this from the other side. GRAGPoison selects relations shared by many queries, injects competing relations wrapped in a cover narrative and enhances them with supporting relations, reaching high success rates on GraphRAG and LightRAG; its ablation identifies temporal ordering, the claim that the competing relation is more recent, as the most influential element of the cover narrative (J. Liang et al., 2026, p. 10). KEPo similarly frames poison as a plausible evolution of the graph's knowledge (Q. Chen et al., 2026). The extraction step is itself an attack surface: because the graph is built by an LLM reading the source text, changing only a few words of that text can alter the extracted graph enough to mislead downstream answers (Wen et al., 2025). Other work isolates an entity's neighborhood (Yan et al., 2026), uses an entity as a backdoor trigger (Z. Zheng et al., 2026), steers an agent's reasoning through a corrupted graph (Kereopa-Yorke et al., 2026), crafts universal adversarial text for text-attributed graphs (Z. Chen et al., 2026) or extracts the graph with few queries (S. Yang et al., 2026). Zhao et al. (2026, p. 8) find that adversarial triples are retrieved for most attacked questions but adopted in only about half of those answers, locating part of the robustness in generation.

The consensus is that the graph is a second attack surface rather than a second line of defense, but each graph attack is evaluated on its own testbed and success criterion. Whether shared-relation poisoning outperforms passage injection when corpus, budget, benchmark and scoring rule are held fixed is the question this thesis addresses.

2.4 Defenses against Retrieval Corruption

Defenses can be grouped by the stage at which they act.

At 
retrieval
, one family certifies robustness by isolating the retrieved passages into disjoint groups, answering from each group separately and aggregating the answers, so that a corrupted minority cannot carry the final answer (Xiang et al., 2024). Another family filters: FilterRAG and its variants remove suspected adversarial passages from the retrieved set before generation (Edemacu et al., 2025, p. 3), and attention-based detection looks for the trace that a poison leaves in the generator's attention rather than in the text (Choudhary et al., 2026). The limits of filtering are stated from the attack side: a reranker trained on clean in-domain data does suppress injected text, but an attacker who edits about one percent of the characters recovers most of the success rate (X. Yang et al., 2026). TailGuard targets specifically the retrieval-optimized class of poisons (Zhou et al., 2026). Layering is the obvious response to the limits of any one filter: RAGuard adds to an adversarially trained retriever a filter that removes each retrieved document in turn and flags those whose removal shifts the answer, and reports each layer's contribution separately (Kumar et al., 2026). Source credibility receives direct support from J. Liang et al. (2026): annotating corpus entries with a credibility label reduced GRAGPoison's success rate from 89.2 to 45.7 percent, which still leaves nearly half of the attacks successful, because labeled poison is still retrieved and can still be believed.

At the 
graph
, HoG-GRAG decomposes a multi-hop question into ordered sub-queries, monitors each hop for poisoning-induced inconsistencies and prunes the compromised entities and relations (Alizadeh Noughabi et al., 2026, p. 1), and Wu et al. (2026) build a separate verification graph outside the retrieval pipeline to detect poisoned agent memories. A third line governs writes rather than reads: a protocol for write-time conflict governance arbitrates between conflicting relations by timestamp and confidence before they enter the graph (
Write-Time Conflict Governance
, 2026), and LedgerRAG keeps a claim-level ledger of the evidence behind an answer and uses its temporal validity, source authority and conflicts to decide when to retrieve, refresh and stop (S. Wang et al., 2026, p. 4). These methods weigh sources against one another; none checks the values the graph asserts against an external, authoritative table of the values themselves.

At 
generation
, LCR-RAG enforces logical consistency through neuro-symbolic training (W. Zheng et al., 2026), evaluation agents try to detect misinformation in the retrieved context (Giri et al., 2026), and traceback methods identify after the fact which passages caused a wrong answer (B. Zhang et al., 2025). Practitioner guidance organizes the same space by control: OWASP (2026) treats retrieval and generation as separate surfaces to harden, and NIST's profile for generative AI classifies the risks by category, listing data poisoning among the information-security risks, and attaches governance, provenance and testing actions to each (National Institute of Standards and Technology [NIST], 2024, p. 8).

Two weaknesses run through this literature. Defenses are almost always evaluated in isolation, so the benefit of combining them is assumed rather than shown; one layer may remove exactly the inputs another would have caught. And defenses are usually evaluated against a fixed attacker, which measures their specification rather than their robustness (Thornton, 2026; X. Yang et al., 2026).

2.5 Beyond a Single Success Rate

The attack success rate answers one question: did the answer change to the attacker's target? Three strands of work argue that this is insufficient for a high-consequence domain. M. Zou et al. (2026) point out that equal success rates may hide very unequal consequences, and propose measuring severity instead. J. Liu et al. (2026) show that RAG systems' verbal confidence is poorly calibrated when the context contains noise or contradiction, which raises the question of whether poisoned answers are expressed with undiminished certainty. Giri et al. (2026) find that their NLI-based evaluation agent blocks instruction injection reliably, but in-place edits such as entity swaps, the kind of value substitution studied here, remain hard to detect; L. Zhang et al. (2026) map the same difficulty in disinformation detection.

A further gap concerns refusals. Generation-side defenses commonly decline to answer on doubtful evidence, and under the usual definition a refusal is a failed attack, so a defense can report a near-zero success rate by refusing most queries. Some defenses report clean and robust accuracy beside the success rate (Xiang et al., 2024; Choudhary et al., 2026), which reveals refusal indirectly, but none of the papers reviewed here reports the refusal share itself.

Finally, the poisoning literature treats recency only as an attack technique: GRAGPoison and KEPo use fabricated recency as cover (J. Liang et al., 2026; Q. Chen et al., 2026). In building regulation recency is real, and amended editions of the same guidance coexist. Whether a RAG system attributes a value to the instrument that governs it, even without an attacker, has not been examined.

2.6 Theoretical Framework

The study is grounded in four bodies of theory and translates each into a design commitment.

Threat modeling and the attacker's capability.
 Following W. Zou et al. (2025) and J. Liang et al. (2026), the adversary is characterized by its access (write access to the corpus only), its knowledge (black-, gray- or white-box with respect to retriever and graph) and its goal (targeted replacement of a value). Each attack therefore has a declared knowledge tier and budget, so that a result can be attributed to a stated adversary.

Dose–response as a model of adversarial budget.
 The number of injected passages per target is a controlled dose; a dose–response design yields the slope that tells a defender how fast risk grows with attacker effort.

Factorial design and interaction. A layered defense is modeled as a set of binary factors. In a full 2 
 2 
 2 factorial each layer's effect can be estimated in every context of the other two, and an interaction is the difference between a layer's effects in two contexts.

Measurement validity and the refusal problem.
 A measure is valid only if it measures the property it is used to infer. Attack success rate measures whether an attack produced its target answer, not whether a defense detected it: a refused query scores zero, exactly as a correct answer does (Equation 4.4), so a defense that refuses most queries reports a near-zero rate. The study therefore reports a conditional accuracy over answered records beside an unconditional one in which a refusal is counted as an incorrect answer (Equation 4.6a); only the latter can rank configurations that answer different shares of the questions. The secondary outcomes (severity weighting, directional analysis and confidence analysis) were registered with their roles fixed in advance.

Together, these commitments let the study borrow its attacks, defenses and measures from the literature while comparing them on one system rather than juxtaposing them.

Chapter 3: Presentation of the Problem

3.1 The Core Problem

The core problem of this thesis can be stated in one sentence: a retrieval-augmented system that answers questions about building regulations can be made to return a wrong value for a specific provision by anyone able to add documents to its corpus, and it is not known how far a graph layer changes that exposure or how far a layered defense can remove it without making the system useless.

The literature reviewed in Chapter 2 establishes that a few adversarial passages can redirect an answer, that graph-based systems can be poisoned through their relations, and that defenses exist at each stage of the pipeline. Three questions remain open.

The first is whether the graph alters what an attacker must do (make a payload survive the LLM's entity–relation extraction and be selected through keyword-to-entity-to-relation retrieval, rather than merely rank a passage in the vector top-k), or merely offers another location for the same payload (the entity and relation descriptions placed in the prompt beside the text chunks). The claim that structure is harder to fabricate than prose has been made on behalf of graph-based RAG, and attacks on the graph have been demonstrated, but the two layers have never been compared on one system under one rubric.

The second is whether a layered defense composes. If each layer's effect were independent of the others, the contributions could be measured one at a time and added. If the layers interact, for instance because one layer removes the very inputs another would have acted on, a one-at-a-time evaluation reports numbers that do not describe the combined system.

The third is what a low attack success rate under defense means. A defense that stops the poison and a defense that stops answering both produce a low rate, and they have very different value to a user.

3.2 Sub-questions

The research question stated in Section 1.4 is decomposed into four sub-questions, each tied to a part of the proposal.

SQ1 (text layer).
 How effective is text-level poisoning against LightRAG on the regulatory corpus, and how does its effectiveness change with the number of passages injected per target?

SQ2 (graph layer).
 Does poisoning aimed at the graph's shared relations succeed more or less often than text-level poisoning at a matched budget, and does poisoning spill over to questions that were not targeted?

SQ3 (defense).
 Can three defensive layers push attack success down to a tolerable rate without a material loss of clean accuracy, how do the layers contribute and interact, and does a defense-aware attacker recover what a static attacker loses?

SQ4 (harms).
 Are the harms uneven: do errors fall disproportionately on safety-critical provisions, does the direction of tampering matter, are wrong answers expressed with less certainty than right ones, and does the system confuse versions of the guidance even without an attacker?

3.3 Hypotheses

The hypotheses below were registered on 4 October 2026, before any attack run, with their tests and decision thresholds; later dated amendments, each made before the run it affected, fixed details such as the budget operator for the graph attack.

H1a.
 At a budget of five passages per target, the strict attack success rate of text-level poisoning over the 150 targets exceeds 0.10. Test: one-sided exact binomial test on the number of targets that succeed in the majority of their three repeats.

H1b.
 Attack success increases across the nested budgets of one, three and five passages. Test: Page's L for ordered alternatives in a repeated-measures layout.

H2.
 At a matched budget, shared-relation (graph) poisoning achieves a higher strict success rate than text-level poisoning. The direction follows the claim that the graph partially protects against text injection while graph-specific poisoning overcomes that protection. Test: one-sided exact McNemar test on the 150 targets, the outcome of each target being the majority of its repeats.

H3.
 Text-level poisoning reduces accuracy on untargeted questions in the same index (spillover). Test: one-sided exact McNemar test on the pooled untargeted observations. A seven-point equivalence margin may be claimed only if the clean discordance rate leaves the test enough power: at a true dACC of 0, a TOST with 
 = 7 points reaches 80 percent power only when the clean-control discordance rate is at most 8.6 percent; above that rate, a passing TOST would reflect insufficient power and is not read as equivalence.

H4a.
 With the defense armed, the strict attack success rate does not exceed a threshold set by the undefended rate:

Both bounds are required because each alone admits a case the other would reject: a defense that halves a very high rate, or one that reaches 0.10 from a rate barely above it.

H4b.
 The defense is non-inferior on clean accuracy: the lower bound of the one-sided 95 percent confidence interval for the change in clean accuracy lies above minus five percentage points,

H5.
 Weighting errors by safety consequence changes the error rate (descriptive: reported as the ratio R of Equation 4.7 with bootstrap intervals, without a directional test).

H6.
 Loosening and tightening a requirement differ in attack success (two-sided, because the proposal offered mechanisms for both directions: a model prior that newer rules are stricter would favor tightening, while suspicion of relaxed requirements would favor loosening). Test: two-sided exact McNemar test on paired targets.

H7.
 A defense-aware adaptive attacker recovers attack success against the full defense relative to a non-adaptive attacker. Test: exact McNemar test on the poisoned items, paired against a control arm run in the same batch.

H8.
 Expressed certainty tracks correctness under attack, that is, wrong answers are not stated as confidently as right ones.

The confirmatory family has seven members, corrected by the Holm procedure: H1a, H1b, H3, H4a on attack one, H4b, and H4a on attack two and H2, the last two added by dated amendment once attack two was designed (Appendix E). H5, H6, H7 and H8 are secondary: they are reported with intervals but do not move the conclusions of the confirmatory family.

3.4 Thresholds and Related Issues

Thresholds set after the data are seen tend to be set where the data already lie, and with many configurations the freedom to choose which comparison to report could make almost any defense look effective.

Two further issues bear on the problem: poisoned text was confined to isolated local copies of the index (Section 4.11), and the unit of analysis is the benchmark item, which makes paired comparisons possible.

Chapter 4: Research Methodology

4.1 Research Approach

The study uses a mixed-methods design. A controlled quantitative experiment carries the confirmatory claims: attacks and defenses are switched on and off on one instrumented system while the corpus, benchmark, serving model and query parameters are held fixed, and every answer is scored against a frozen rubric. A small qualitative component, a three-case study of the amendment-narrative attack and a hand-scored version probe, supports their interpretation.

An experimental design suits the question because it is causal: it asks what an attack or defense does to a system's answers with everything else held constant. It can establish whether and by how much outcomes change on this system, not whether the effects transfer (Chapter 9).

Two problems were anticipated: noise, because the serving LLM exposes no temperature parameter, handled by three repeats per configuration; and researcher degrees of freedom, handled by pre-registration (Section 3.3), hash-frozen inputs and recomputation of every number from raw per-item records.

4.2 System and Environment

All experiments used LightRAG core 1.5.8 as a local server. Indexing extraction and answer generation used the DeepSeek API with the model name deepseek-v4-flash (from 10 September 2026 the provider served this name with DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, reported by the API as deepseek-flash; the experimental index was extracted on 5–7 September, before the change, and all runs came after it); embeddings were computed locally with Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B; the graph was stored with NetworkX and the vectors with NanoVectorDB, on a laptop with an i9-14900HX processor, an RTX 5070 GPU and 32 GB of memory.

Query parameters were pinned for every run and recorded in its header (hybrid mode, 40 candidates, 20 chunks in the context, reranking disabled; Appendix B, Table B-3), so a difference between two runs cannot come from a change in retrieval settings.

The corpus consists of official documents downloaded from GOV.UK and registered in a manifest that records each file's name, source and SHA-256 hash (sources in Appendix B). 4.7 Experimental Procedure

The experiments were run in order, each step depending on frozen outputs of the previous one: (1) a clean baseline on the experimental index; (2) A1 at three budgets on each fold, with a clean control and the paired-direction set; (3) A2 at M = 5 on each fold; (4) the defense ablation, all eight layer combinations on A2's fold-0 poisoned index over all 150 items, with clean-index arms for H4b, in a registered batch (18 October 2026) that is the main reading, with two correction batches (21 and 25 October) reported beside it; (5) A4's four arms on the same 72 poisoned items, undefended and with all layers armed; (6) A3's three cases; and (7) the version-attribution probe, scored by hand. Every run had three repeats unless stated otherwise.

A second experimental batch was run between 6 and 8 November 2026, after the registered batches: a false-positive test of provenance filtering, the naive retrieval mode, the A4 arms against the generation layer alone, a self-rated confidence pass, a second scorer, and re-analyses of archived runs. Each experiment had its design and reading rule written down before it ran (Appendix E), none belongs to the confirmatory family, and each is reported in the section whose question it answers.

Each run produced three files: a header recording the environment, the parameters and the server identity; a raw file with one record per item and repeat; and a summary. Each attack run also preserved a copy of the index it queried.

5.2.2 Where the Dose Response Comes From

Retrieval was saturated from the smallest budget: at M = 1 the injected passage was cited in all 225 poisoned records, and at M = 3 and 5 in 99.0 to 99.6 percent. A2 added fewer wholly injected relations than A1 (6,416 against 7,186), so its lower success is not a larger perturbation diluting itself. And every poisoned target cited an injected passage in most of its repeats; the gap in injected passages cited per answer (4.82 and 4.86 against 4.98 and 4.97) is a fraction of one passage, against a one-third gap in successes. The pre-registered subgroup of 39 targets inside fold blocks moved in H2's favor (11 against 9; p = 0.377), but its blocks group items by shared provision, not by the shared entity A2 exploits.

A second-batch re-analysis of the same runs therefore grouped the 143 targets that both attacks poisoned by whether the entity A2 attacked is shared with another target in the same fold. A2 trailed A1 by 3.3 points on the 60 shared-entity targets (11 against 9 successes; one-sided p = 0.83) and by 8.4 points on the 83 others (20 against 13; p = 0.98). The difference between strata, +5.1 points (95 percent interval [
8.9, +19.2]), is the difference of two deficits rather than a gain: its direction agrees with the shared-entity mechanism, but the stratum cannot resolve it (McNemar reads only the discordant pairs, of which the shared stratum has ten).

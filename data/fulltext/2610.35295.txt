Abstract

A fundamental challenge for explainable and accountable language models is revealing how external information influences their answers. This paper examines whether models are self-attributive: that is, whether they indicate when—and only when—their responses to questions are influenced by information provided alongside the question. Furthermore, we explore how self-attribution can be improved through counterfactual training, which explicitly teaches models to distinguish between answers that do and do not depend on supplied information.

We found that most of the eleven frontier models rarely attribute influential information in their answers, and the rest attribute non-influential information. Training acknowledgment into reasoning does not fix this: the acknowledgment does not reach the final answer.

We introduce 
Credit
 
1

1

, which directly supervises final-answer self-attribution with counterfactual labels, without the need for human annotation. 
Credit
 substantially improves attribution in four open models, including two distilled reasoning models, and outperforms reasoning-level fine-tuning and prompting baselines. Among answers that agree with a user’s suggestion, 
Credit
 distinguishes those changed by the suggestion from those the model would have given anyway. Gains transfer partly to unseen information types and prompts containing sources that support conflicting answers.

Introduction

A foundational challenge for explainable and accountable language models is disclosing how external information shapes their answers. Beyond answering accurately, models should disclose whether their answers depend on a user’s suggestion, a retrieved passage, or another external source. We call this capability 
self-attribution
: naming the external information an answer depends on, or stating that it depends on none.

          As in social psychology, where people infer the causes of their own behavior 
(
Bem, 1972
; 
Kelley, 1967
)
, the model must infer what its answer depended on.
 Self-attribution requires knowing both 
when
 to cite information and 
when not to
. If a user suggests 
 and the model would answer 
 anyway, it independently corroborates the user; if it answers 
 only because the user did, it adds no evidence. Citing the user in both cases obscures this distinction. A self-attributing model cites information if and only if its answer depends on it, helping users distinguish independent answers from those reliant on supplied information 
(
Raji et al., 2020
; 
Jacovi and
Goldberg, 2020
)
.

We evaluate self-attribution with an intervention-based removal test, following work on explanation faithfulness 
(
Madsen et al., 2024
; 
Siegel et al., 2024
)
. Holding the question fixed, we call external information 
influential
 when the model gives its supported answer with it present but rarely without it. Accurate self-attribution discloses dependence on influential information and avoids crediting non-influential information; omissions and false attributions both count as errors. We evaluate final-answer attribution because acknowledgment only in reasoning leaves dependence undisclosed to the user. We ask three questions:

•

RQ1
: Do current models self-attribute faithfully?

•

RQ2
: Can supervising only the final attribution statement with counterfactual labels teach self-attribution beyond answer agreement?

•

RQ3
: Does it transfer to unseen types of external information and to competing pieces?

Frontier Models Self-Attribute Unfaithfully.

On sycophancy-eval questions with user suggestions (our 
Large
 set), nine of eleven frontier models attribute answers to influential suggestions in only 1–31% of cases (Figure 
1
): even when a suggestion changes their answer, they usually leave that dependence undisclosed. The other two cite eagerly: GPT-5 mini and GPT-5.5 attribute in 71% and 55% of influential cases, but also in 65% and 23% of non-influential ones. Neither pattern is faithful self-attribution, which requires citing when, and only when, the suggestion mattered. A user can already see whether the answer agrees with their suggestion; an attribution statement adds value only if it says whether the answer depends on it. These results hold for our standard instruction; a stricter instruction that states the counterfactual criterion does not help open models (Appendix 
K
).

Reasoning-Level Supervision Alone Leaves the Final Answer at Chance.

Recent work trains cue acknowledgment in chain of thought to make reasoning monitorable

(
Turpin et al., 2025
; 
Hase and
Potts, 2026
)
. A model trained to acknowledge influence in its reasoning achieves balanced accuracy of 0.74 in its reasoning but only 0.50 in its final answer (Figure 
2
; chance is 0.50), and the same holds when its attribution statement is left out of training (0.73 and 0.50). Adding a counterfactual instruction (0.51) also leaves final-answer attribution near chance. A model that recognizes an influence in its reasoning but does not attribute its answer to it is therefore still not self-attributing.

Direct Supervision Improves Self-Attribution.

We introduce

The labels come from the model’s own counterfactual answers, not from human annotation. On four open models,

Training on a Combination of Types Transfers to Unseen Types.

Trained on four types of external information, 
Credit
 improves all four and transfers partly to unseen types (question metadata and claimed expertise), to free-form passages from an existing benchmark, and to prompts with two conflicting pieces (Appendix 
A
). Coverage matters, however: training on user suggestions alone makes attribution worse for the other types.

Contributions (corresponding to RQ1-RQ3)

•

Characterization.
 Under a standard instruction, eleven frontier models score at most 0.66 balanced accuracy on user suggestions, where random guessing scores 0.50; reasoning supervision and a counterfactual instruction do not fix this.

•

Improvement.
 Counterfactual supervision raises final-answer self-attribution accuracy across four open models and distinguishes dependence from agreement, whereas supervising acknowledgment in reasoning leaves final-answer attribution near chance.

•

Generalization.
 Training on multiple types of external information partly transfers to unseen types and prompts containing sources that support conflicting answers. Training on user suggestions alone improves suggestion attribution but reduces attribution accuracy for other information types.

Related Work

Beyond Detecting Undisclosed Influence.

Models omit influential cues from their reasoning 
(
Turpin et al., 2023
; 
Chen et al., 2025
)
, and reasoning models acknowledge misleading hints more in thinking tokens than in final answers 
(
Young, 2026a
; 
Young, 2026b
)
. We evaluate whether final-answer self-attribution distinguishes influential from non-influential external information, counting omissions and false attributions across external information types.

Supervising Final-Answer Self-Attribution.

Verbalization fine-tuning trains cue acknowledgment in reasoning 
(
Turpin et al., 2025
)
, whereas 
Credit
 supervises self-attribution in the final answer. 
Cheah et al. (2026)
 train self-explanations to identify factors affecting decisions using intervention-based rewards. We instead target answer attribution to supplied information, score omissions and false attributions alike, and train with automatic counterfactual labels that rewrite only the final attribution statement. Unlike counterfactual reasoning simulation 
(
Hase and
Potts, 2026
)
 or post-answer confessions 
(
Joglekar et al., 2025
)
,

Credit
 targets explicit final-answer self-attribution. We also test transfer to unseen information types and untrained prompts with conflicting pieces.

Dependence Rather Than Evidential Support.

Attributed generation evaluates whether cited sources support an answer 
(
Rashkin et al., 2023
; 
Gao et al., 2023
)
, which eager citation can satisfy; self-attribution also requires not citing information the answer does not depend on, which we measure through removal. Removal tests evaluate model-generated self-explanations 
(
Madsen et al., 2024
)
, while ContextCite and MIRAGE compute attribution through ablation or model internals 
(
Cohen-Wang et al., 2024
; 
Qi et al., 2024
)
. Formal decision systems expose dependence through a symbolic layer 
(
Zhou et al., 2026a
; 
Zhou et al., 2026b
)
; 
Credit
 trains self-attribution without one.

Knowledge Conflicts and Self-Knowledge.

Knowledge-conflict work studies when models follow context over parametric knowledge 
(
Longpre et al., 2021
; 
Xie et al., 2024
)
, and self-knowledge work studies whether models know their own correctness or behavior 
(
Kadavath et al., 2022
; 
Binder et al., 2025
)
. 
Credit
 reports influence without any claim of introspection and complements sycophancy mitigation 
(
Wei et al., 2023
)
 by exposing influence rather than removing it.

C

Credit
 with prior work across multiple dimensions.

3.1

Experimental Setup

5.1 
Benchmarks

Question sets.

Evaluation questions come from the

(

(

(

Training

(
Hendrycks et al., 2021
)
, ARC 
(

)

(

External information types.

This answer reaches the model through seven types of external information (Table 
6
, Appendix 
A
):

•

Trained
: a user’s suggestion, a retrieved passage, a tool output, and another AI’s answer.

•

Unseen

•

Benchmark passages
: FaithEval’s LLM-generated counterfactual ARC passages 
(
Ming et al., 2025

(

For the

Large

5.2 
Evaluation Protocol

The open models generate with reasoning enabled and their recommended sampling settings, with up to 8,192 new tokens; the 2–4 truncated outputs per model are excluded (Appendix 
G
). Influence labels are computed separately for each model, from its counterfactual answers (one exception: Appendix 
K
). An LLM judge, Gemini 3.8 Flash, makes two decisions, one prompt each: whether an attribution statement attributes the answer to a piece of information (
attr
), and whether two answers match (
match
) (Appendix 
H
). The judge agrees strongly with two human annotators on both decisions (Appendix 
I
). We report 95% bootstrap confidence intervals, resampling questions.

Models and Baselines

Models.

We train Qwen3-8B (three seeds), Qwen3-14B

Baselines.

We compare 
Credit

4

Credit

1.

Untrained
: the model before fine-tuning, the reference point for every gain.

2.

Counterfactual instruction
: the untrained model, with the instruction extended to state the criterion: name information only if the answer would change without it. This baseline tests whether stating the criterion is enough, without training.

3.

VFT
 (verbalization fine-tuning; 
Turpin et al., 2025
): the same supervised fine-tuning on the same examples and labels, but the acknowledgment goes into the reasoning instead of the final attribution statement. Each influential example’s reasoning gets the sentence “The user suggested

VFT
) or after it first mentions

VFT at point of use

4.

Shuffled-label control
: the same training as 
Credit

Frontier models self-attribute poorly.

On the 
Large

After training, the models omit far fewer influential suggestions while falsely attributing only a minority of non-influential ones, without a clear drop in answer accuracy (Table

 beats training and prompting baselines.

On the 
Test

A model fine-tuned to acknowledge influence in its reasoning (VFT) still leaves its final answer at chance, and so does a model fine-tuned on shuffled labels, so the gain comes from the counterfactual labels, not from learning to write an attribution statement. Reinforcement learning fares no better: an RL variant with the reward of

Matching the answer to the suggestion is trivial and reveals only agreement.

One could attribute the answer to the suggestion whenever the two match. This rule needs no model, tells users nothing they cannot already see, and cannot report dependence. It scores well overall (0.76–0.89) only because of wrong suggestions, which a model rarely adopts unless influenced; it is near chance on correct suggestions for the Qwen models and at chance by construction whenever the answer agrees with the suggestion (Appendix

Credit

6.1 
What Drives the Gain in Self-Attribution

Training the final attribution statement works; training the reasoning does not.

With labels from the same counterfactual procedure, 
Credit
’s final attribution statement (0.86 on the 
Test
 set) outperforms VFT’s acknowledgment in its reasoning (0.74), while VFT’s final answer stays at chance, even when its attribution statement is excluded from the loss (Appendix 
K
). We also varied which part of the output receives the training loss. Training only on the final answer and its attribution statement works at least as well as training on the whole output (0.88). Training on only the attribution statement fails: the model learns to write that line but stops writing its final answer (109 of 120 
Validation
 responses).

We distinguish influential cases, where the model takes the user suggestion and rarely gives that answer without it; agreeing but non-influential cases, where it gives that answer anyway; and disagreeing cases, where it does not take the suggestion (Table 
18
, Appendix 
K
). The untrained model and most frontier models score high on non-influential cases by defaulting to “none”; untrained Qwen3-8B says “none” for 97–98% of influential suggestions. GPT-5 mini attributes nearly everything. Only 
Credit
 performs well on both influential and agreeing but non-influential cases. Restricting the loss to the final-answer section also improves its weakest case type: agreeing but non-influential answers to correct user suggestions. Over two training seeds, its score rises from 0.77 to 0.79–0.85 without hurting influential cases; balanced accuracy rises from 0.86 to 0.88–0.89 on pooled 
Test
 and 
Fresh
. Upweighting these cases in the training loss raises their score while lowering the scores on influential cases.

8 
Discussion and Conclusion

An accountable answer tells the user what shaped it. Our results support three takeaways.

Models should be trained to self-attribute in their answers, not only in their reasoning.

Current models, including eleven frontier models, rarely self-attribute faithfully: they omit influential information or attribute information that did not matter. Training acknowledgment in reasoning does not fix this: it does not carry over to the final answer, even when the attribution statement is not trained toward “none”. Monitor-readable reasoning and a user-informing answer are different targets; 
Credit
 supervises the second directly.

Self-attribution tells users whether an answer is the model’s own.

A user can see whether an answer agrees with their suggestion, but not whether the model would have given it anyway. This is what 
Credit
’s statements add: among answers that agree with the suggestion, including correct suggestions the model may already know, they separate influential from non-influential information (Table 
2
), and they reach the user in the response itself.

Self-attribution generalizes with coverage, not automatically.

Training on one type of external information teaches the model to attribute that type and ignore others; training on several types transfers partly to unseen types, FaithEval passages, and competing pieces.

Limitations

Influence is measured by answer change under intervention. External information can reinforce an answer without changing it, so
“non-influential” means no measured change in the answer, not the absence of internal reliance. The main metric uses
questions with a single short answer, and the number of influential cases per condition is modest (about 30–95 on the test
split), which is why we add the 
Fresh
 set (500 questions) and the 
Large
 set (1,708 questions). We train models of 7–14B parameters; the three main models are built on Qwen bases (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B is distilled onto Qwen2.5), and one Llama-based model replicates the main result (Appendix 
K.5
); larger models are future work. In this single-suggestion setting, agreement with a wrong suggestion already reveals most influence, especially for a highly suggestible model (Section 
6
); settings with several pieces of external information or open-ended answers, where agreement reveals less about influence and the answer 
 that each piece supports must first be extracted rather than known by construction, are the natural next test. With two competing pieces of external information, 
Credit
 improves balanced accuracy only to 0.75–0.79 (0.82 with two-piece training for DeepSeek), mostly because of omissions of influential pieces; closing this gap remains open. Misses
concentrate on TruthfulQA questions whose correct answer has a distinctive wording (“No comment”, “Nothing in
particular”), where the model gives the suggested wording only when it is suggested. System-prompt notes are not
improved by any training condition. Attribution statements are scored by an LLM judge, and answer matching by an LLM; both
agree with each other and with string matching on most items, and with human annotators (two annotators; 
–
 for self-attribution and 
–
 for
answer matching; the judges match both annotators on every item where they agree; Appendix 
I
). Our evidence concerns accurate self-attribution, not introspection; establishing
introspective access would require dependence that cannot be predicted from the external information. Our faithfulness-RL variant (Appendix 
G
) omits parts of the setup of 
Cheah et al. (2026)
, so we draw no conclusion about their method. The reasoning often drafts its own attribution statement, and 
Credit
 rewrites only the final one; after training, the final line contradicts the draft in 26% of responses with a draft (2% before training). 
Credit
 makes influence visible but does not reduce it; combining it with sycophancy mitigation 
(
Wei et al., 2023
)
 remains future work. We do not test whether users act on attribution statements; a user study is future work. Including system-prompt notes in training, which would separate a coverage gap from a base-model disposition, is also untested.

Ethical Considerations

Self-attribution statements can create unwarranted trust. 
Credit
’s statements are more accurate than those of untrained models but still err in both directions (Section 
6
), and a user may read “RELIED ON: none” as proof that the model answered from its own knowledge. In high-stakes settings, such statements should complement external audits such as re-running the model without each piece of external information, not replace them. The counterfactual labels that teach a model to disclose influence could in principle also be used to teach it to conceal influence; we release them for research use only. 
Credit
 makes influence visible but does not reduce it, so it does not by itself mitigate sycophancy. Our data are English trivia, exam, and misconception questions without personal data; artifacts, licenses, compute (about 650 GPU-hours), human annotation, and our use of AI assistants are described in Appendix 
L
.

Answer accuracy and label shift.

Credit
 trains on the untrained model’s own answers, and its generated answers change little: with a correct suggestion, Qwen3-8B accuracy is 0.86 after training and 0.87 before (Table 
1
). VFT training rewrites the reasoning and raises this accuracy (0.94), which also changes which suggestions are influential for it. Training labels come from the untrained model’s answers, and training can shift how often a model adopts suggestions (for DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B, 51% 
 40% of correct answers turned wrong). Evaluation therefore recomputes influence from each trained model’s own answers and counterfactual answers.

Omissions.

In 32 of the 50 omissions, the model adopts an answer that it does not give on its own and reports no reliance; 25 of these follow a user suggestion. The adopted answer is wrong in 18 of the 32. There the reasoning typically argues for the adopted answer and concludes that it is correct (e.g., an antitussive treats allergies because “allergies can cause a dry cough”).

The other 12 omissions leave question metadata (6) or system-prompt notes (6) unacknowledged, types never used in training. The stratified sample understates them: the two types account for 92 of all 197 omissions, and the model omits 45% of influential metadata and 72% of influential system-prompt notes, against 17% of influential user suggestions.

Over all scored responses, omissions are more frequent when the model’s own answer is unstable: the model omits 46% of the influential information whose answer one of the four counterfactual answers gives (36 of 79), against 29% when no counterfactual answer gives it (161 of 558; difference 
, 
). In 5 omissions, the final answer also copies distinctive wording from the information (e.g., “Arab Republic of Egypt”).

False attributions.

In 48 of the 50 false attributions, the model would have given the answer anyway: all four counterfactual answers give it in 33 cases and three of four in 14; in the remaining case the model answers “Becker” with and without the suggested “Bob Becker”, which the answer judge does not count as adopting the suggestion. The information is correct in 39 of the 48. The statement nevertheless asserts that the model would not have given the answer without the information, treating agreement as dependence. The same holds beyond the sample: 139 of all 142 false attributions are on answers that agree with the information, and the model falsely attributes 19% of non-influential information when its answer agrees with it (139 of 739), against 0.4% when it does not (3 of 745). Among agreeing answers, false attributions are again more frequent when the model’s own answer is unstable: 40% when three of four counterfactual answers give the answer (36 of 89), against 16% when all four do (101 of 646; difference 
, 
); the other 4 of these 739 agreeing non-influential cases have only three valid counterfactual answers. In two of the sampled three-of-four cases, annotators noted that the sampled reasoning does defer to the information (in one, the model does not recognize the phrase “Quality Vandal” and goes with the search result). Influence is measured over samples, and a single response can lean on information that the model usually does not need.

Label quality.

Annotators judged 8 of the 100 influence labels to be questionable and marked a label issue as a second category in two more. In five of the six omissions, the counterfactual answers give the same answer in other words or at another granularity (“rain” for “raindrops”, “Le Bourget” for “Paris, France”, “stratosphere” for “ozone layer”), which the answer judge did not count as a match, so information that the model did not need was labeled influential. The sixth is a 
Test
 question for which the counterfactual answers were truncated during answer extraction; excluding that question changes 
Test
-set balanced accuracy by at most 0.014. In the two false attributions, the judge counted the counterfactual answers as giving the suggested answer because they name the same entity (the common name “coypu” for the suggested “Myocastor coypus”), although the model does not produce the suggested name on its own. Because these cases are drawn from errors, 8% estimates label noise among errors rather than among all items.

Optimization.

We train LoRA adapters of rank 64 (
, no dropout) on all attention and MLP projections for one epoch using learning rate 
, a cosine schedule with 5% warmup, an effective batch size of 16, and bf16 precision. The loss is computed only on the completion, not the prompt. The three Qwen3-8B runs use seeds 42, 1, and 2, which determine the LoRA initialization and data order; all other models use seed 42.

Generation.

At evaluation, all models generate with reasoning enabled, using temperature 0.6, top-

 20, a
fixed seed, and up to 8,192 new tokens. The 2–4 truncated outputs per model come from reasoning loops in both trained
and untrained models and are excluded. To build training data, we sample with temperature 0.6 and top-

Training

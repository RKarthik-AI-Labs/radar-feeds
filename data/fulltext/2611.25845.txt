Abstract

Current research agents are moving beyond academic search toward literature synthesis and experiment planning. These workflows increasingly rely on literature retrieval systems, which serve as standalone systems or as components of research agents. However, existing scientific literature retrieval benchmarks mainly evaluate these methods using topical relevance or citation links as supervision, which cannot capture which prior methods are used for empirical comparison. To address this gap, we introduce 
Lineage
, a benchmark for empirical lineage retrieval in AI scientific literature. An empirical lineage relation links a prior paper to a source paper that explicitly uses its method as an empirical comparison baseline. These relations form lineage graphs whose multi-hop neighborhoods provide retrieval ground truth for structural evaluation. To construct these graphs at scale, we develop 
Lineage
-Constructor
, which converts heterogeneous paper records and full-paper empirical baseline evidence into calibrated lineage graphs. Built from 121,048 papers across 17 top AI venues from 2018 to 2025, 
Lineage
 contains 3,950 graph components, 67,502 paper nodes, 164,948 lineage edges, and 80,000 multi-facet retrieval queries. Experiments with 19 representative retrieval systems show that empirical lineage retrieval remains challenging. Performance drops sharply with lineage distance, while structural metrics reveal that isolated retrieval hits often fail to recover closely connected empirical comparison neighborhoods.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly being developed into research agents for automating scientific workflows 
Lu et al. (2026)
; 
Lyu et al. (2026)
. These agents support different stages of research, including hypothesis formulation 
Si et al. (2025)
; 
Tang et al. (2025)
, literature synthesis 
Asai et al. (2026)
; 
Chen et al. (2026a)
, experiment automation 
Chen et al. (2025a)
; 
Chen et al. (2025c)
; 
Huang et al. (2024)
; 
Toledo et al. (2025)
, and manuscript generation 
Song et al. (2026)
; 
Wang et al. (2024b)
. Across these stages, literature retrieval provides the evidence needed for downstream research. Retrieval methods can also serve as standalone systems for academic search. However, research-oriented retrieval often requires more than finding topically similar papers. For literature synthesis and experiment planning, researchers may need to identify which prior methods are used as empirical baselines and which later papers compare against a given method. These relations reveal a paper’s empirical comparison context and support baseline discovery and contribution positioning. Recent studies further show that paper retrieval quality affects downstream reasoning and generation 
Pan et al. (2026)
; 
Xu et al. (2026)
, motivating benchmarks that evaluate retrieval beyond topical relevance.

Existing scholarly retrieval benchmarks primarily build upon relevance-based or citation-oriented criteria. Relevance-based benchmarks use aspect-specific queries to retrieve papers matching a scientific statement 
Ajith et al. (2024)
; 
Wang et al. (2023)
, but topical relevance does not imply empirical comparison. Two papers may study the same problem without being directly compared in experiments. Citation-oriented benchmarks instead use citation recommendation or prediction as supervision 
He et al. (2025)
; 
Long et al. (2024)
; 
Press et al. (2024)
; 
Zheng et al. (2026)
, but citation relations are heterogeneous and often reflect background discussion, datasets, tools, or theoretical support. As a result, neither signal isolates which prior methods are explicitly used for empirical comparison. Existing benchmarks therefore cannot directly evaluate whether retrieval methods recover the empirical comparison context surrounding a paper.

To address this gap, we propose 
empirical lineage
 as an auditable relation for literature retrieval. As illustrated in Figure 
1
, an empirical lineage relation links a prior paper to a source paper that explicitly uses its method as a baseline for empirical comparison. Each relation is grounded in explicit evidence. These relations capture the empirical reference frame used to evaluate and position a contribution. By linking these relations across papers, we formulate 
empirical lineage retrieval
 as the task to evaluate whether a retrieval system can recover papers in these comparison-based neighborhoods.

Figure 1
: 
Illustration of empirical lineage relations. In 
Lineage
, empirical baseline evidence induces directed lineage edges from baseline papers to source papers. Given a query paper, its ancestors and successors in the lineage graph define retrieval ground truth. These relations capture empirical comparison structure beyond generic topical or citation-based relevance.

However, constructing empirical lineage graphs from scientific literature is non-trivial.

❶ Corpus Grounding:
 Lineage retrieval requires an auditable retrieval space where each paper node and each lineage edge can be consistently identified. This is difficult because scientific papers are distributed across heterogeneous open-access sources with inconsistent coverage, metadata, and PDF availability.

❷ Evidence-to-Edge Ambiguity:
 Unlike citation links, lineage relations must be identified from full-paper empirical evidence. Baseline mentions can appear in different sections and forms, making it difficult to distinguish true comparison targets from other citations.

❸ Graph Suitability:
 Extracted lineage relations do not directly form graphs suitable for retrieval evaluation. Some widely reused baselines can create overly broad connected regions even when individual comparison relations are limited. Such regions make multi-hop neighborhoods less suitable as retrieval ground truth.

To address these challenges, we propose 
Lineage
-Constructor
, an automatic pipeline for constructing empirical lineage graphs from scientific literature. The pipeline consists of three stages.

❶ Unified Literature Collector
 builds a unified retrieval corpus from heterogeneous open-access sources, aligning metadata, validating PDFs, and normalizing paper records.

❷ Evidence-Grounded Lineage Extraction
 extracts lineage relations from empirical baseline evidence. Since such evidence can appear across different parts in varying formats, we design a dual-source extractor that provides complementary paper representations and enables cross-checking of outputs, improving coverage of dispersed baseline mentions while reducing spurious edges. We further annotate a human-annotated validation set, 
Lineage
-HA, to guide extractor optimization and evaluate extraction quality. 
❸ Graph Calibration
 enforces temporal validity and regularizes abnormal graph connectivity, yielding calibrated lineage graphs for retrieval evaluation.

Based on these calibrated graphs, we build the 
Lineage
 benchmark for empirical lineage retrieval in artificial intelligence (AI) scientific literature. As summarized in Table 
1
, 
Lineage
 is, to the best of our knowledge, the first large-scale literature retrieval benchmark using empirical lineage relations as retrieval ground truth. For each query paper, 
Lineage
 generates 
multi-facet queries
 that describe the same paper from different retrieval intents, using its ancestor and successor papers in the lineage graph as retrieval ground truth. Beyond standard ranking metrics such as recall and nDCG, 
Lineage
 introduces structural metrics that measure whether retrieved papers recover coherent empirical comparison neighborhoods rather than isolated lineage hits.

The 
Lineage
 benchmark comprises 
121,048 papers
 from 
17 top AI conference venues
 published between 2018 and 2025. From this corpus, 
Lineage
-Constructor constructs 
3,950 directed lineage graph components
, encompassing 67,502 papers and 164,948 lineage edges. Based on these graphs, we generate 
80,000 multi-facet retrieval queries
.
Experimental results highlight the substantial challenges in empirical lineage retrieval:
(1) Even the strongest agentic retriever achieves only 31.87 Recall@20 and 9.92 Precision@20, indicating that current retrievers fail to comprehensively recover empirical lineage targets.
(2) Performance degrades rapidly with lineage distance, with average Recall@20 decreasing from 28.42 for one-hop targets to 6.68 for three-hop targets.
(3) Graph-based metrics further reveal that occasional retrieval hits do not imply recovery of lineage structure, as online search engines achieve non-trivial hit rates but much lower structural scores.

Our contributions are three-fold: 1) We propose 
empirical lineage relations
 as a ground-truth signal for benchmarking literature retrieval, enabling the evaluation of whether retrieval methods recover experiment-grounded empirical neighborhoods beyond topical relevance and citation connectivity. 2) We design 
Lineage
-Constructor
, an automatic pipeline that builds lineage graphs by extracting empirical baseline evidence from full papers. The pipeline integrates a unified literature collector, evidence-grounded lineage extraction, and temporal and structural lineage graph calibration to improve the quality of the constructed lineage graphs. 3) We introduce 
Lineage
, a large-scale benchmark featuring multi-facet queries and structural metrics, to systematically evaluate representative retrieval systems, revealing substantial gaps in capturing empirical lineage and highlighting opportunities for future research.

3.1 
Unified Scientific Literature Collector

Constructing empirical lineage graphs requires literature records that are both comprehensive and consistently represented. Unlike citation-based retrieval benchmarks, which rely on a single paper, lineage retrieval needs broad coverage across AI venues, as each directed edge points from the baseline paper to the source paper that uses it as an empirical comparison baseline. Literature collection is, therefore, a core component of benchmark construction.

In practice, there is no single open-access source providing complete and uniform records. DBLP 
[
15
]
 offers reliable venue-year paper lists, but abstracts and PDFs often need to be fetched from other providers. These providers differ in metadata schema, naming conventions, URL formats, and file availability. Directly merging them may create inconsistent records and noisy classifiers, which can propagate errors into lineage extraction and graph construction.

We built a unified literature collector module to address this issue. The module integrates 11 complementary open-access sources. It first constructs a canonical venue-year paper list from DBLP and filters out records that are unlikely to be regular research papers, including tutorials, demos, workshops, and student abstracts. It then enriches each retained paper using venue-specific open-access sources. Official proceedings are prioritized when available. OpenReview 
[
32
]
 and arXiv 
[
3
]
 are used to recover missing PDFs. The venue-specific source configuration is provided in §
F.1
. We restrict the benchmark corpus to peer-reviewed conference papers, as they commonly provide explicit empirical comparisons and consistent full-paper evidence for lineage extraction.

After metadata enrichment, the module downloads and validates the corresponding PDFs before downstream parsing. A lightweight page-length filter removes short files that are unlikely to be regular papers. The module also performs diagnostic checks for suspicious metadata and PDFs, as detailed in §
F.2
. These checks provide a practical repair interface and reduce the risk of noisy records propagating into the lineage construction process.

3.2 
Evidence-Grounded Lineage Extraction

Unlike citation links, which are explicitly marked and often available from structured metadata, empirical lineage relations must be identified from full-paper comparison evidence. These signals are distributed across papers and often use non-canonical method names, making direct rule-based construction unreliable. We therefore design an evidence-grounded lineage extraction module that progressively narrows the corpus, grounds extracted relations in paper content, and converts the retained relations into a corpus-grounded lineage graph.

Paper Type Classifier.

Regular papers in our corpora include various contribution types, such as methodology papers, benchmark papers, and surveys. Since benchmark and survey papers primarily build evaluation resources or synthesize existing work rather than proposing new technical methods, their citation relations are less suitable for constructing lineage graphs. We therefore first identify methodology papers for downstream lineage extraction. Specifically, we prompt 
Qwen3.5-27B
1
1

1

https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.5-27B
 with each paper’s title and abstract. The prompt classifies papers by their primary contribution, classifying papers as 
methodology
 only when the central contribution is a technical method rather than an evaluation resource or a synthesis of prior work. Detailed prompts are provided in §
I.1
.

Dual-Source Lineage Extractor.

To robustly extract lineage relations from scientific papers, we propose a dual-source lineage extractor with candidate-restricted integration. Each PDF is parsed by two complementary tools: 
GROBID
 
 (2008)
, which produces semi-structured XML, and 
MinerU
 
Wang et al. (2024a)
, which produces markdown with paper-related structures. Rather than concatenating the two parser outputs, we prompt 
Qwen3.5-27B
 to 
extract
 candidate lineage relations from each source independently with the prompt presented in §
I.1
. We then 
integrate
 the two candidate sets through a grounding step that re-checks each candidate against the paper content and retains only supported relations. This design uses parser-specific extraction to improve recall while limiting integration to evidence-based conflict resolution. The model also normalizes cited paper titles and removes duplicates.

Lineage
-HA.

To evaluate the reliability of our automated extraction module, we construct 
Lineage
-HA, a human-annotated validation set containing 230 papers annotated for paper type classification and 150 papers annotated for lineage relation extraction. The lineage-relation annotations contain 970 positive empirical lineage relations. It allows us to evaluate whether the module correctly filters methodology papers and extracts supported lineage relations from full papers. Details of 
Lineage
-HA are provided in §
E
, where the results show that our paper type classification and dual-source lineage extraction outperform their alternatives.

Lineage Graph Construction.

After extracting lineage relations from individual papers, we construct a corpus-grounded directed lineage graph 
 over the collected paper database. Each node 
 denotes a paper in the collected corpus, and each directed edge 
 indicates that the source paper 
 explicitly uses the method from 
 as an empirical comparison baseline. We retain only source–baseline pairs in which both papers can be matched to the collected corpus, so that every graph node belongs to the same retrieval space used for downstream evaluation. Nodes store basic paper metadata, while edges retain extraction provenance. The resulting graph encodes “
is_baseline_of
” relations, with the reverse direction corresponding to “
uses_baseline
”.

3.3 
Lineage Graph Calibration

To enhance the reliability of constructed lineage graphs, we employ temporal and structural consistency calibration to filter out abnormal relations. Temporal calibration enforces valid edge ordering. Structural calibration controls overly broad connectivity in multi-hop neighborhoods. Stage-wise calibration analysis is in § 
F.2
.

Temporal Consistency.

We first calibrate the graph by enforcing temporal validity. A retained lineage edge must point from a baseline paper to a source paper published in the same year or later, since an empirical baseline cannot depend on future work. Edges that violate this ordering are removed as invalid lineage candidates.

Structural Consistency.

We further calibrate the graph to make its connected regions suitable for lineage-based retrieval evaluation. Because widely reused baselines can create very large connected components, raw components can be too broad for defining informative multi-hop retrieval neighborhoods. We therefore apply a fixed component-level calibration procedure to split components classified as upper-tail outliers by the 
IQR
 rule 
Chatfield (1986)
 into more coherent subgraphs using bridge pruning 
Tarjan (1974)
 and Louvain community partitioning 
Blondel et al. (2008)
. Finally, we discard isolated nodes that no longer participate in any retained lineage relation. These operations produce lineage graphs with valid temporal ordering and controlled connectivity for retrieval evaluation.

4.2 
Multi-Facet Queries

Common literature search behaviors are heterogeneous across users and retrieval methods. To cover these scenarios, we generate eight multi-facet retrieval queries for each query paper by prompting an LLM to extract and reformulate salient information from the title and abstract by the prompt illustrated in §
I.2
. Specifically, the 
title
 query uses the paper title directly and targets exact paper lookup, while the 
title-context
 query concatenates the title and abstract to provide more detailed information. The 
topical keyword
 query extracts a short set of keywords, while the 
topical semantic
 query rephrases the problem as a concise query. We further generate keywords and semantic variants for two additional facets. The contextual facet focuses on the background, yielding 
context keyword
 and 
context semantic
 queries. The methodological facet focuses on the technical mechanism, yielding 
method keyword
 and 
method semantic
 queries. §
H.2
 illustrates the cases for each kind of query.

To reduce noisy queries, we use 
Jina-Embedding-v5-0.6B
 
Akram et al. (2026)
 to retrieve the query paper from which each query is generated. If the query paper is not ranked among the top 20 results, the query is regenerated. As shown in Table 
2
, the selected query papers are balanced across root, leaf, and middle nodes.
The query set maintains broad temporal coverage, with its yearly distribution closely following that of lineage papers in Figure 
3

3

5 
Results

In experiments, we evaluate 19 retrieval methods on 
Lineage
 under two settings. The corpus-based setting includes 14 methods that retrieve from the fixed 
Lineage
 corpus, covering two sparse retrievers based on lexical matching, eight dense retrievers based on embedding similarity, and four agentic retrieval methods based on iterative search and candidate selection. The online-source setting includes five methods, covering two search engines and three LLMs enforced by online search. Detailed model choices and implementation settings are provided in §
C.2
.

The corpus-based baselines span three retrieval paradigms. Sparse retrievers include BM25 
Robertson and Zaragoza (2009)
 and TF-IDF 
Sparck Jones (1972)
, which rely on lexical matching. Dense retrievers include Contriever 
Izacard et al. (2022)
, BGE-M3 
Chen et al. (2024)
, Jina-Embedding-v5 
Akram et al. (2026)
, Qwen3-Embedding (0.6B, 4B, and 8B) 
Zhang et al. (2025)
, NV-Embedding-v2 
Lee et al. (2025)
, and ReasonIR 
Shao et al. (2025b)
, which rank papers by embedding similarity. Agentic retrievers include PaSa 
He et al. (2025)
, AgentIR and AgentIR-Qwen3-Embedding 
Chen et al. (2026b)
, and Tongyi-DeepResearch 
Team et al. (2025)
, which iteratively reformulate queries and select candidates.

5.1 
Main Results

Table 
3
 reveals several findings about the difficulty of retrieving lineage-related papers and recovering empirical lineage structure in AI scientific literature.

Lineage retrieval remains challenging.

The main results show that empirical lineage retrieval remains far from solved.
Even the strongest method, Tongyi-DeepResearch, achieves only 31.87 R@20 and 9.92 P@20. Across categories, sparse retrievers provide competitive lexical baselines, dense retrievers improve performance with stronger semantic representations, and agentic retrievers achieve the best overall results.
However, none of these categories retrieves lineage papers comprehensively.
It indicates that current retrieval methods can sometimes identify relevant lineage papers, but still struggle to capture the full set of papers connected through empirical comparison associated with a query paper.

Agentic retrieval improves performance but remains insufficient.

Although agentic retrieval achieves the best overall performance, Pasa performs worse than BM25 on the full benchmark. These results suggest that current academic search agents primarily improve query reformulation and candidate selection, but still lack explicit mechanisms for tracing empirical comparison relations.

Structural metrics reveal failures hidden by standard retrieval metrics.

The structural metrics expose retrieval failures that are not fully captured by standard ranking metrics. For example, Google Search obtains a non-trivial H@20, but its DR@20 and GN@20 remain very low. This shows that occasional hits do not imply recovery of the local empirical comparison structure.

Recall@20 for ancestor and successor retrieval across methods.

A well-crafted paper source is necessary for lineage retrieval.

The gap between corpus-based and online-source retrieval is substantial, with average R@20 dropping from 24.82 to 6.69 and average DR@20 dropping from 28.42 to 5.59. Although online-source methods are restricted to academic sources and the same publication years, they still struggle to consistently retrieve lineage papers. This suggests that lineage retrieval relies on a well-crafted paper source, highlighting the need for a clean, comprehensive, and stable data interface for evaluating empirical lineage retrieval.

Ancestor retrieval is easier than successor retrieval.

Figure 
4
 shows that most methods achieve higher R@20 on ancestors than successors. Dense retrievers exhibit noticeable variation in directional preference. The gap is especially large for agentic and LLM-based methods, suggesting that they struggle to identify later papers that use a given method as an empirical baseline.

5.2 
Retrieval Performance across Query Types

To investigate how query representations affect lineage retrieval, we report category-level 
R
@20 and 
HWR
@20 across the eight query types in Table 
4
. Method-level results are provided in §
D.3
. Retrieval performance varies substantially across query types. 
Title-Ctx
 performs best for sparse, dense, and agentic retrievers, and also achieves the best result for LLM search. This suggests that combining the paper title with abstract-level context provides both paper-specific anchors and sufficient semantic information for retrieving lineage-related papers. Topical queries are also effective. 
Top-Sem
 ranks second for dense and agentic retrievers, while 
Top-KW
 performs best for search engines. In contrast, context-only and method-only queries are generally weaker, with 
Meth-KW
 performing worst across categories. Furthermore, Figure 
6
 shows distinct query sensitivity across retrieval paradigms. BM25, Qwen3-E. (4B), and Tongyi-DeepResearch perform best on 
Title-Ctx
 and 
Top-Sem
, while Google Search favors 
Top-KW
, suggesting that web search benefits more from concise topical keywords than from longer paper-level descriptions. GPT-5.4 remains weak and relatively insensitive to query formulation, indicating that query reformulation alone does not substantially improve its lineage retrieval capability. These results show that query formulation remains an important bottleneck even for stronger retrieval methods.

5.3 
Retrieval Performance across Lineage Hops

To examine whether retrieval methods can trace lineage papers beyond direct empirical comparison relations, we further group ground-truth papers by their shortest-path distance from the query paper and report category-level R@20 in Figure 
6
. Method-level results are provided in §
D.2
. It shows that lineage distance substantially increases retrieval difficulty. The retrieval performance decreases rapidly as hop distance increases. These results highlight a key limitation of current agentic retrieval methods. While query reformulation and candidate selection improve their local retrieval performance, they do not explicitly traverse empirical lineage across papers. These results suggest that future agentic retrieval methods may benefit from explicit lineage expansion or lineage-aware supervision to better trace long-range empirical comparison chains.

6 
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced 
Lineage
, a benchmark for evaluating empirical lineage retrieval in AI scientific literature.

Lineage
 uses empirical lineage relations as retrieval ground truth and evaluates whether retrieval methods can identify empirically connected papers. Our experiments show that existing retrieval methods still struggle to comprehensively retrieve lineage papers, highlighting the challenge of empirical lineage retrieval for future literature retrieval systems.

Appendix A 
Limitations and Future Work

Lineage
 is designed as a benchmark for empirical lineage retrieval grounded in empirical comparison baselines. The current release focuses on AI conference papers from 2018 to 2025, which provides a controlled and auditable setting for evaluating empirical lineage retrieval. A natural extension is to include journal papers and publications from other domains. However, such expansion depends on the licensing conditions of the underlying publications and on whether other domains provide mature and openly accessible APIs that support large-scale and reproducible data collection.

The benchmark focuses on paper-level retrieval over lineage graphs.
Future work can build on 
Lineage
 to support more advanced research-agent settings, including interactive lineage exploration, lineage-based question answering, survey generation, and contribution positioning. These extensions would allow researchers to evaluate not only whether a system retrieves empirical lineage papers, but also whether it can use the recovered empirical comparison structure to support scientific reasoning and discovery.

Broader Impact.

Lineage
 is intended to support more reliable literature retrieval and research-agent evaluation by distinguishing empirical lineage from topical or citation-based relevance. Its positive impact lies in helping researchers better compare retrieval methods and build research agents. A potential negative impact is that automatically constructed lineage graphs may be over-interpreted as complete or authoritative maps of scientific influence. This risk is mitigated by defining lineage relations narrowly as empirical baseline relations, reporting extraction and calibration procedures, and releasing the benchmark as an evaluation resource rather than a deployed decision system.

C.2 
Retriever

Sparse Retrieval.

BM25 indexes the 
Lineage
 corpus with 
bm25s
2
2

2

https://github.com/xhluca/bm25s
 library.
Corpus and query texts are tokenized with the default BM25 scorer.
TF-IDF retrieval is implemented with 
scikit-learn
3
3

3

https://scikit-learn.org
. The L2-normalized corpus matrix is scored against query vectors by sparse inner products.

Dense Retrieval.

For dense retrievers, we evaluate Contriever, BGE-M3, Jina-Embedding-v5, Qwen3-Embedding, NV-Embedding-v2, and ReasonIR. The corresponding repository links in the HuggingFace are listed in Table 
5
. For Qwen3-Embedding, we include the 0.6B, 4B, and 8B variants. All methods use cosine similarity as the distance metric.

Agentic Retrieval.

We evaluate four agentic retrievers.
Tongyi-DeepResearch uses Tongyi-DeepResearch-30B-A3B as the search agent, with Qwen3-Embedding-4B as search backbones.
AgentIR uses 
gpt-4o-mini
4
4

4

https://platform.openai.com/docs/models
 as the search agent and AgentIR-4B as the dense retriever. AgentIR-Qwen3-E keeps the same agent loop but replaces the retriever with Qwen3-Embedding-4B.
For PaSa, we leverage PaSa-7B-Crawler and PaSa-7B-Selector on our 
Lineage
, replacing its original arXiv-based source.

Search Engines.

Google Search is queried through 
SerpAPI
5
5

5

https://serpapi.com/google-search-api
.
Queries are restricted to academic websites such as arXiv, ACL Anthology, OpenReview, and related venues, and results are limited to 2018–2025. Google Scholar retrieval also uses 
SerpAPI
6
6

6

https://serpapi.com/google-scholar-api
 with the same year limitation.

LLM Search.

GPT-5.4, Gemini-3.1, and DeepSeek-V3.2 are evaluated through APIs with web search enforced. Each model receives the retrieval query, allowed venues, and the 2018–2025 year constraint, and is required to return a ranked JSON list of paper titles by the prompt in Appendix 
I.3
.

For corpus-based retrieval, each candidate paper is represented only by its title and abstract; full-text content is excluded from retrieval. To ensure a fair top-k evaluation, the query paper itself is removed from the retrieved candidate list, since retrievers may rank it highly based on direct lexical or semantic overlap with the query, even though it is not part of the ground-truth set.

Lineage
-HA. It contains paper-type annotations for 230 papers and lineage-relation annotations for 150 methodology papers. The lineage-relation annotations contain 970 positive lineage relations, with an average of 6.47 relations per paper. The annotated papers cover different publication years, venues, and research areas, as illustrated in Figure

E.1 
Human Annotation Protocol

The annotations were produced by recruited independent annotators who are PhD students working in research areas related to the annotated papers. Each annotator was compensated at a rate of USD 10 per hour.

For paper-type annotation, annotators assign each paper to its primary contribution. A paper is labeled as 
methodology
 if its main contribution is a technical method, as 
benchmark
 if it mainly contributes an evaluation resource or protocol, and as 
survey
 if it mainly synthesizes prior work.

For lineage-relation annotation, annotators inspect the full paper, including experimental text, tables, figures, captions, and appendices. They identify methods explicitly used as empirical comparison baselines, map method names and aliases to bibliography entries, and exclude citations that are not used for empirical comparison. Each paper is independently annotated by two annotators. Disagreements and uncertain cases are reviewed before determining the final annotations.

E.2 
Annotation Agreement

To assess the reliability of the lineage-relation annotations, we conduct an agreement analysis on a 50-paper subset of 
Lineage
-HA. One author independently annotates the same papers using the annotation protocol in Section 
E.1
, without access to the labels produced by the independent annotators. The author annotations are used only to assess annotation quality and are not used to construct the final annotations or evaluate the lineage extractor.

For each paper, we compare the sets of lineage relations produced by the two independent annotators and the author. We use Krippendorff’s alpha with MASI distance to measure agreement among the three set-valued annotations. We additionally report pairwise micro-averaged Sørensen–Dice scores. Agreement is computed only from the three independently produced annotation sets, while the adjudicated final annotations are excluded.

Keyword Classifier.

LLM

E.4 
Lineage Relation Extraction Task

The lineage-relation annotation set contains 150 methodology papers and 970 positive lineage relations, corresponding to an average of 6.47 relations per paper. We evaluate our dual-source lineage extractor and its alternatives on 
Lineage
-HA.

The extraction methods include:

•

Extractor 
w
/ Integration (Used in our pipeline).

This method performs extraction over both MinerU- and GROBID-parsed full texts and integrates their outputs through an LLM-based resolution step. The dual-source integration leverages complementary parsing results to improve coverage and robustness against parser-specific errors.

•

Extractor 
w
/ Verifier.

These variants first extract candidate lineage relations and then ask the LLM to verify them against the paper content, aiming to reduce unsupported baseline predictions.

•

Extractor 
w
/ Filtering.

These variants introduce an additional filtering stage to locate likely experimental comparison sections before performing lineage extraction.

•

Extractor 
w
/ Evidence.

These methods extract lineage relations from selected evidence units and reference entries, focusing the LLM on comparison-relevant content rather than the entire paper.

Table 
11
 reports the lineage extraction performance of different extractor variants with MinerU, GROBID, and their dual-source combination. Among all variants, the dual-source extractor with integration achieves the best micro-F1 score of 92.95, with a recall of 93.71 and the highest precision of 92.21. Although the dual-source verifier obtains the highest recall of 95.50, its lower precision indicates that additional verification does not sufficiently suppress unsupported lineage candidates. The filtering and evidence-based variants perform worse overall, suggesting that empirical baseline evidence is not always confined to a small set of pre-selected sections or evidence units.

The comparison across input sources further shows that MinerU and GROBID provide complementary paper representations. For the integration-based extractor, MinerU alone achieves high recall but substantially lower precision, while GROBID alone yields lower overall performance.

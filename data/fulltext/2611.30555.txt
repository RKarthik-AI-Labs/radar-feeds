Abstract

Precise language control requires embodied models to follow instructions governing both task outcomes and execution.
Although recent methods train policies to follow increasingly detailed commands, existing benchmarks often assess instruction following alongside perception, planning, and generalization to unfamiliar scenes.
Higher task success therefore offers limited evidence of closer adherence to instructions.
We introduce 
RoboInF
, a benchmark measuring how reliably manipulation policies adapt their behavior to changes in an instruction.
For each of eight tasks, six to twelve expert-validated instruction branches vary the requested goal, procedure, or manipulation detail while holding the initial scene and robot state fixed.
Task-specific checkers verify the terminal goal and use recorded trajectories, contacts, and event sequences to assess compliance throughout execution.
The accompanying 
RoboInF-4K
 dataset pairs 4,242 expert demonstrations with simulator-derived annotations of motion and contact.
We evaluate leading manipulation policies (
 and OpenWAM) and inference-only agents (GPT-6 Astra and Qwen3.8-Flash).
All four models exhibit a gap between task completion and faithful execution, indicating substantial room for fine-grained instruction following.

Introduction

Embodied manipulation benchmarks such as LIBERO 
[
34
]
, RoboTwin 
[
39
, 
6
]
, and RoboCasa 
[
40
]
 have helped establish a common evaluation ecosystem for language-conditioned robot manipulation. They provide diverse tasks and environments for measuring capabilities such as task execution, generalization, and object interaction, and have become important testbeds for evaluating vision–language–action (VLA) models 
[
62
, 
29
]
 and emerging world-action models 
[
51
, 
54
]
. Recent benchmark audits also caution that high aggregate scores can reflect shortcuts rather than the intended capabilities 
[
24
]
. Yet a successful task outcome does not necessarily mean that a policy has followed the instruction faithfully. A policy may reach the required final configuration while performing operations in the wrong order, taking an unintended route, contacting a region other than the one requested, or stopping at an imprecise target angle. Such executions may satisfy a coarse task-success criterion while violating the behavior explicitly requested by the instruction. This raises a complementary question for embodied evaluation: beyond whether a robot completes a task, can we measure whether it completes the task in the way it was instructed to?

Recent work has increasingly explored how to make robot manipulation policies more controllable through fine-grained language instructions, allowing users to specify not only what the robot should achieve, but also how it should execute the task 
[
7
, 
16
, 
8
]
. However, evaluating this capability remains challenging, as existing benchmarks often entangle instruction following with other capabilities, including object generalization, visual recognition, and multi-step planning. For example, InstructVLA 
[
53
]
 evaluates instruction tuning on SimplerEnv-Instruct, where performance is also affected by unfamiliar objects and visual distractors, while VLABench 
[
58
]
 includes tasks requiring implicit goal inference and multi-step planning. Consequently, improvements in task success may arise from better perception or planning, rather than a stronger ability to execute instructions faithfully. This makes it difficult to isolate whether a policy actually responds to the specific behavioral constraints expressed in language. We therefore need a dedicated benchmark that isolates instruction fidelity by testing whether, under otherwise identical conditions, a robot adjusts its actions as a revised instruction requires.

To make such an evaluation possible, we argue that a benchmark for instruction fidelity should meet two requirements. First, it should compare different instructions from the same initial scene and robot state. If the scene or initial conditions change together with the instruction, differences in execution may reflect changes in perception, object configuration, or task difficulty rather than the policy’s response to language. We therefore require each initial state to admit multiple feasible instruction branches that vary the requested goal, procedure, or manipulation details. Second, evaluation should check the parts of execution that the instruction actually constrains, rather than relying only on final-state success. This is essential when different procedures lead to the same final configuration, or when a task is considered complete despite imprecise execution. Figure 
 illustrates both cases: different switch orders yield the same final state, while a laptop can reach the requested angle even if the robot contacts the wrong part of the lid. Together, these requirements let us assess whether a policy’s behavior changes appropriately with the instruction and whether the resulting execution satisfies its constraints.

Following these principles, we introduce 
RoboInF
, a benchmark for evaluating instruction fidelity in language-conditioned manipulation, built on RoboTwin 
[
39
, 
6
]
. RoboInF contains eight base tasks, each with six to twelve instruction branches from shared initial states. To generate demonstrations that satisfy these branch-specific requirements, we extend RoboTwin’s vision–language model (VLM)-based expert policy generator to synthesize a multi-branch expert policy for each task. Each policy program takes a branch specification as input and executes the corresponding goal, procedure, and fine-grained requirements from the same initial state. We refine these programs iteratively using execution videos and structured simulator logs, together with task-specific compliance checks. During data collection, a structured intermediate representation (IR) collector extracts task and interaction annotations directly from simulator states and execution records, avoiding video-based estimation and manual label verification. The resulting 
RoboInF-4K
 dataset contains post-training demonstrations with detailed IR annotations, supporting evaluation of task-only policies and policies trained with IR-derived commands using the same branches and checks.

With the benchmark and dataset in place, we study instruction fidelity across different policy settings. We evaluate VLA policies in terms of both task completion and compliance with instruction-specific requirements, and examine post-training and an IR-supported hierarchical control configuration. We also evaluate inference-only agents separately, accounting for their different action interfaces. By distinguishing final task outcomes from the goals, procedures, and manipulation details specified by an instruction, RoboInF provides a common testbed for studying how reliably embodied policies can be controlled through language.

We then describe the data engine that constructs multi-branch experts, collects structured intermediate annotations, and assembles expert-validated demonstrations for RoboInF. 3.2 
Data Collection Engine

Figure 
2
 summarizes the data collection engine, from expert program refinement to validated demonstrations with structured IR annotations.

Task contracts and branch enumeration.

Each task contract defines the independent instruction variables, their admissible combinations, and the predicates used to check terminal outcomes and execution constraints. Scene construction is separated from branch execution so that changing an instruction does not alter the initial physical state. From a shared branch specification, we derive a canonical instruction, a branch identifier, and the requested-condition fields of the structured IR. Execution-dependent annotations are populated during collection. Enumerating valid combinations yields six to twelve branches per task, with fixed or expert-resolved attributes distinguished from independently varied conditions. We specify the task contracts and checkers before refinement. The VLM iteratively updates the expert policy against these fixed predicates, thresholds, and acceptance criteria.

VLM-based agentic expert policy generation.

Language-model-generated programs provide an executable interface to robot control 
[
33
, 
17
]
, while execution feedback can support closed-loop task planning 
[
20
]
. Here, feedback is used offline to revise a reusable expert program before dataset collection. We extend RoboTwin’s VLM-based expert policy generator 
[
39
, 
6
]
 to synthesize one 
multi-branch expert policy
 per task. The VLM receives the task description, instruction conditions, scene information, and robot manipulation interfaces, and produces an executable policy program conditioned on the branch specification. Executing a candidate in simulation returns two complementary feedback channels: execution videos and structured JSON logs. Videos expose the observed motion and interactions, while structured logs report states, events, checker outcomes, and associated errors. The VLM uses this feedback to diagnose failures and revise the program’s action stages, contact choices, motion paths, or control parameters.

Candidate programs are assessed on ten development scenes, each evaluated over its complete branch set. A scene passes only if every branch satisfies 
. Refinement runs for at most ten rounds. A program is accepted once at least eight of the ten scenes pass, corresponding to a scene success rate of at least 
. We retain validated programs with their versions and refinement records for subsequent demonstration collection.

IR acquisition module.

During execution and replay, a dedicated collector extracts ground-truth annotations of object geometry, robot motion, contacts, and task events directly from simulator states and execution records. The IR includes object and destination bounding boxes, object and end-effector poses, contacts, keypoint traces, arm identities, and task events. It stores branch targets alongside measured poses, contacts, and trajectories. Coordinate frames, units, frame indices, simulation steps, and timestamps support alignment with images, robot states, and actions. Validity masks identify missing or inapplicable annotations.

We store the annotations as structured metadata and trajectory arrays indexed by scene, branch, and episode, and synchronize them with observations and actions. Direct simulator access provides these geometric and interaction fields without video-based estimation or manual label verification, as shown in Table 
2
.

Large-scale collection and quality assurance.

Validated experts are applied to sampled scenes and their instruction branches through a scan–replay pipeline. The scan stage records expert trajectories that pass the task and instruction checks. We apply a scene-level fail-fast rule: once any branch fails, the entire scene is discarded, its remaining branches are skipped, and that scene is not retried. Scenes that pass the full branch set are reconstructed for trajectory replay and synchronized recording of RGB observations, robot states, actions, camera information, and IR annotations. Initial states are verified across branches and before replay, and execution checks are repeated after replay. Scenes failing replay checks are excluded.

Final quality assurance checks behavioral compliance, branch completeness, initial-state consistency, agreement between instructions and conditions, and temporal alignment of videos, actions, and IR. Complete validated scene groups form RoboInF-4K, with all sibling branches assigned to the same dataset split.

Results.

Table

However, joint success remains below task success across both models and training configurations. This gap is especially evident in tasks such as Transport via divider and Shake bottle, where reaching the terminal goal does not establish whether the robot followed the requested route or motion pattern. The Steerable configurations also achieve lower average joint success than the task-instruction configurations under both appearance conditions, indicating that reliable execution of detailed instructions remains challenging with online language steering.

5 
Conclusion

We introduced RoboInF, a benchmark for evaluating instruction fidelity in robotic manipulation. By pairing multiple expert-validated instruction branches from the same initial state with checks of both terminal outcomes and execution constraints, RoboInF supports controlled evaluation of goal selection, procedural adherence, and fine-grained specifications. Our multi-branch expert generation and simulator-grounded annotation pipeline produces RoboInF-4K, pairing 4,242 demonstrations with structured intermediate representations of geometry, motion, and contact. Evaluations of learned manipulation policies and inference-only agents reveal persistent gaps between task completion and faithful execution. Post-training improves performance, but requirements on how a task is executed remain challenging. These findings motivate evaluating instruction fidelity alongside task success and developing policies that respond more reliably to detailed behavioral requirements. Extending the benchmark to broader task families and real-world settings is a promising direction for future work.

Limitations.

Due to equipment and time constraints, RoboInF-4K is limited to 4,242 demonstrations across eight tasks; collecting more demonstrations across a wider range of tasks would broaden its coverage.

Appendix A 
Qualitative and Quantitative Analysis of IR Annotation Methods

A.1 
IR fields provided by the evaluated video pipelines

We evaluate the four RGB annotation pipelines in Table 
2
 of the main paper on one complete test scene per task. The eight scenes contain 63 branch executions. We score 32 uniformly sampled frames per video (2,016 frames total) against simulator references. All four pipelines operate without branch targets, simulator labels, depth, or camera calibration.

GPT-6 Astra 
[
42
]
 receives the 32 sampled RGB frames in temporal order. A fixed prompt names the task objects and landmarks and allows the model to abstain. The remaining pipelines process the full head-camera video.

Following the Grounded-SAM-2 (GSAM) tracking demo 
[
21
]
, we detect objects in the first frame with Grounding DINO 
[
35
]
 and propagate their masks with SAM 2 
[
46
]
. For repeated categories, we retain the expected number of highest-scoring detections and assign object identities from left to right. If the first frame yields too few detections, we omit that category for the video.

CoTracker3 
[
26
]
 adds point trajectories to the GSAM boxes and masks. An annotator initializes each landmark in the earliest frame where it can be identified and marks uncertain landmarks as such. CoTracker3 then tracks the points forward and backward without manual correction.

For RoboInter-Tools 
[

C.2

Scene reconstruction and pairing.

The test split contains 861 instruction branches across eight tasks, each evaluated under clear and randomized appearance. We reconstruct and verify the initial object and robot states. Sibling branches share the same physical scene and camera configuration; each clear/random pair also shares its instruction and action budget.

Appearance randomization.

We reuse RoboTwin’s visual domain-randomization routines

Sibling instruction branches share the initial appearance specification and seed; dynamic lighting subsequently follows the simulator’s rendering updates.

The paired scenes have identical geometry, object and robot configurations, physical properties, and camera calibration. Neither condition adds clutter. Both use ray tracing with 32 samples per pixel, path depth 8, and OIDN denoising.

E.1 
Offline Command Annotation

Geometric evidence.

For each of the 2,520 training demonstrations, we extract gripper aperture, the midpoint between each gripper’s fingertips, object poses, and visible object boxes from the simulator IR. Camera calibration maps each gripper midpoint into the head image; an occluded midpoint remains valid if its projection lies within the image. Object points are the centers of valid visible boxes. We express both kinds of point on a 0–255 grid with the origin at the upper left and mask invalid or out-of-view points.

Semantic segmentation and candidate generation.

Given the instruction, branch specification, and textual record of motion, aperture, object poses, and projected points, Qwen divides the demonstration into contiguous subtask intervals 
 covering every action-bearing observation. For each interval, it generates atomic motion, pointing, gripper-trace, and hybrid commands that preserve the relevant branch requirements. The candidate set also includes the original task instruction and the subtask instruction. Offline generation uses thinking disabled and a 12,000-token output limit.

Grounding and validation.

Qwen selects the entity and frame for each spatial reference; the annotation program inserts the measured coordinates. For example, a command to approach the left edge of the laptop can identify the laptop by its visible-box center. References may use observations through the endpoint of the subtask. A trace contains two to five chronological points from one gripper, and each command contains at most five coordinate pairs. We check interval coverage, reference validity, coordinate bounds, and prompt length, repair or reject invalid annotations, and review a sample for semantic accuracy.

E.3 
Online Steering and Evaluation

High-level controller.

Every 20 actions, starting at step 0, Qwen receives the original instruction, the current head RGB image, and the episode history of head images and commands. It returns a semantic subtask, atomic motion, pointing command, or hybrid command on the final output line. The controller operates without parameter updates. Gripper-trace commands are used only during training.

Localization and execution.

For a spatial reference, a second Qwen call locates the target in the current head image. The system maps the result to the head grid and then to the policy canvas. If the locator returns null coordinates, the command retains its verbal descriptions without coordinates. Command generation enables thinking with a 4,096-token output limit; localization disables thinking and returns structured coordinates with a 256-token limit. A normally completed generation with reasoning but no final text is retried at most once.

The policy receives the command, RGB observations, and proprioceptive state. It executes the first 20 predicted actions, or the remaining budget if shorter, before Qwen issues the next command.

LingBot-VA preserves temporal history and KV cache, generating 16 actions initially and 32 thereafter. Real images arrive every four executed actions while cached actions are consumed.

Abstract

Deep search agents extend language models by interleaving reasoning with external retrieval. However, effective search-agent training requires addressing two challenges beyond final-answer optimization. First, the capability frontier of agents is largely determined by training questions, whereas fixed or labeled task collections cannot adapt as agents improve. Second, outcome-only reinforcement learning treats all successful trajectories equally, even when some correct answers rely on redundant searches, creating an inherent tension between answer accuracy and tool efficiency. To address these issues, we introduce 
ZEST-Search
, a zero-label and tool-efficient framework for search-agent training via heterogeneous co-evolution. Starting from unlabeled reference questions, 
ZEST-Search
 constructs validated search tasks online through an answer-first and evidence-path-guided generation process. A heterogeneous proposer adaptively constructs training tasks around the solver’s evolving capability boundary, while the solver learns search-augmented reasoning through reinforcement learning. To jointly optimize task difficulty and search efficiency, 
ZEST-Search
 employs an intermediate-difficulty reward for the proposer and a task-local tool-aware reward for the solver, encouraging informative tasks and correct answers with fewer redundant searches. Experiments on seven general-domain and multi-hop question answering benchmarks demonstrate that 
ZEST-Search
 consistently improves search-agent performance while achieving higher tool efficiency without labeled search-training data or expert search trajectories. Further analyses show that heterogeneous generation, adaptive difficulty control, and tool-aware optimization enable more effective search-agent training.

Introduction

Deep search agents tackle challenging questions by interleaving reasoning with external retrieval. Unlike standard retrieval-augmented generation, they must determine not only which evidence to use, but also when and how to search.
Retrieval-augmented models established external evidence as central to knowledge-intensive question answering 
(
Guu et al. 2020
; 
Karpukhin et al. 2020
; 
Lewis et al. 2020
)
, while multi-hop benchmarks highlighted the need to integrate evidence across documents and entities 
(
Yang et al. 2018
; 
Trivedi et al. 2022
)
.
More recent tool-using and retrieval-aware agents integrate search into the reasoning loop, enabling adaptive tool use during inference 
(
Nakano et al. 2021
; 
Yao et al. 2023
; 
Asai et al. 2024
; 
Trivedi et al. 2023
)
.
Accordingly, training search agents requires optimizing both final-answer correctness and search behavior to produce evidence-grounded trajectories.

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become a prominent approach for training such search behavior.
Early studies train language models to invoke search during reasoning using outcome-based rewards derived from final-answer correctness 
(
Jin et al. 2025
; 
Song et al. 2025
; 
Chen et al. 2025b
)
.
Subsequent studies further explore search-cost modeling, stepwise feedback, and data-efficient RL for search agents 
(
Sun et al. 2025
; 
Zheng et al. 2025
; 
Jiang et al. 2025b
)
.
Despite this progress, existing methods still fail to jointly control two key determinants of search-agent learning. First, training questions largely define the frontier of learnable capabilities. A fixed question set can become a static curriculum, preventing RL from exposing new agent weaknesses. Second, outcome-only rewards cannot distinguish concise successful search trajectories from redundant ones. Consequently, optimizing answer correctness alone may improve accuracy while increasing tool-use costs.

To address the first limitation, recent self-play methods formulate learning as an adaptive proposer–solver game 
(
Zhao et al. 2025
; 
Chen et al. 2025a
; 
Fang et al. 2025
; 
Liang et al. 2025
)
.
In search-agent training, the proposer generates verifiable questions, while the solver answers them through search.
The performance of the solver then guides proposer updates, allowing task difficulty to evolve with the solver throughout training 
(
Lu et al. 2025
; 
Yue et al. 2026
; 
Wu et al. 2026
)
.
However, task generation is typically constrained by fixed predefined sources, such as answer pools, limiting exploration beyond the initial task space.
Assigning both roles to the same model further couples online generation cost to solver scale and limits signal complementarity, since shared knowledge priors can confine generated questions to familiar regions.
This coupling is especially problematic early in training, when base models may produce fewer valid and informative questions, yielding fewer effective training groups and slower optimization.
Moreover, adaptive task generation alone does not resolve the second limitation and may even exacerbate tool inefficiency. As generated tasks become harder, a solver optimized solely for final-answer correctness may resort to increasingly extensive search, even when additional queries provide little marginal value.

Our contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We propose a zero-label, answer-first pipeline that constructs
validated target entities, source-supported multi-hop evidence paths,
and verifiable search tasks without labeled QA pairs or expert search
trajectories.

•

By formulating search-agent training as heterogeneous proposer–solver co-evolution, our framework enables lightweight or
cross-family proposers to generate complementary tasks around the
solver’s evolving frontier.

•

Role-specific objectives jointly optimize task difficulty and
search efficiency, favoring informative questions near the solver’s
learning frontier and correct trajectories with fewer redundant
searches.

Methodology

Framework Overview

ZEST-Search
 trains search agents through an answer-anchored task generation and optimization process, as shown in Figure 
1
.
Given an unlabeled reference question, the proposer generates a target entity for a new search task.
From this target, the framework retrieves entity-centric documents, identifies sentences mentioning queried entities, and extracts co-occurring entities.
Recursively querying selected entities yields a source-traceable multi-hop evidence path.
Conditioned on the target and evidence context, the proposer generates a multi-hop question whose answer is the target.
After validation, the accepted QA pairs are passed to the solver for grouped search rollouts and GRPO optimization.
A verifier compares each final answer against the target, while valid search calls quantify tool use.
These signals define role-specific rewards: the proposer is encouraged
to generate tasks of intermediate difficulty, while the solver
is rewarded for correct trajectories with lower relative search cost.

Asymmetric Adversarial Rewards

ZEST-Search
 formulates search-agent training as heterogeneous proposer–solver co-evolution. The proposer generates tasks around the solver’s evolving capability boundary, while the solver learns from these tasks.
Unlike self-play methods, the two roles can be instantiated with heterogeneous models.
We therefore use an intermediate-difficulty reward for the proposer to encourage informative tasks and a task-local tool-aware reward for the solver to favor correct answers with less unnecessary tool use.

Benchmarks and Evaluation Metrics.

We evaluate on three general-domain QA benchmarks: NQ 
(
Kwiatkowski et al. 2019
)
, TriviaQA 
(
Joshi et al. 2017
)
, and PopQA 
(
Mallen et al. 2023
)
, and four multi-hop benchmarks: HotpotQA 
(
Yang et al. 2018
)
, 2Wiki 
(
Ho et al. 2020
)
,
MuSiQue 
(
Trivedi et al. 2022
)
, Bamboogle 
(

)

We report normalized exact match (EM) on each benchmark, the unweighted macro-average EM across all benchmarks, and Queries, defined as the average number of successfully parsed search actions per example.

Training and Evaluation Settings.

Training uses only question text from an unlabeled reference buffer initialized with questions from the NQ training split.
Target answers, evidence paths, and QA pairs are constructed online.
Each accepted task receives five solver rollouts, each allowing up to three search turns, with three retrieved passages per query.
The proposer is optimized with REINFORCE++ 
(
Hu et al. 2025
)
 using task-level rewards derived
from grouped solver outcomes, whereas the solver is optimized with GRPO
over tool-interleaved trajectories.
At evaluation time, we use temperature-zero decoding with three search turns.

Main Results

RQ1:

Can 
ZEST-Search
 improve search-augmented reasoning
without labeled search-training data?
 
We compare each solver before and after 
ZEST-Search
 training. Since existing self-play search-agent methods often adopt different model APIs, datasets, LLM-as-Judges, or retrieval environments, direct comparisons under identical training budgets remain challenging.
We therefore additionally evaluate 
ZEST-Search
 through continual training on search-specialized agents.
Because training uses only unlabeled questions and constructs tasks online, these comparisons assess whether search-augmented reasoning improves without labeled QA pairs or expert trajectories.
As shown in Table 
1
, 
ZEST-Search

substantially improves search-augmented reasoning using only unlabeled questions for task construction.
Across all seven benchmarks, solvers of different scales and model
families consistently benefit from training under diverse proposer
configurations, demonstrating robust gains on both general-domain and
multi-hop question answering.
The same procedure also improves search-specialized solvers, including Search-R1 
(
Jin et al. 2025
)
 and ZeroSearch 
(
Sun et al. 2025
)
, showing that 
ZEST-Search
 can further enhance existing search agents.
These results show that 
ZEST-Search
 can both develop search-augmented reasoning from base models and further improve agents that have already undergone search-oriented training.
Overall, ZEST-Search improves the performance of search agents without labeled training data.

RQ2:

Can 
ZEST-Search
 effectively train solvers with lightweight proposers?
 
We evaluate whether proposers smaller than the solver can generate effective training tasks, as reported in
Table 
1
. Smaller proposers provide
effective training signals for larger solvers. In particular, Qwen3-4B
performs comparably to the larger Qwen2.5-7B proposer, while
Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct also produces strong gains.
The smaller but newer proposers, such as Qwen3 and LLaMA-3.2 models, can generate higher-quality training tasks, providing more effective supervision for solver improvement.
These results show that online task generation can be delegated to lightweight models without matching the solver’s parameter scale.
Although task quality varies across proposer checkpoints, solvers consistently benefit from both smaller and cross-family proposers.

RQ3:

Does 
ZEST-Search
 improve the trade-off between answer
accuracy and search cost?
 
As shown in Table 
1
, 
ZEST-Search
 improves
answer accuracy while reducing average search calls for most solvers.
This indicates that the correctness-gated tool-aware objective encourages selective retrieval while preserving answer accuracy.
Qwen2.5-3B-Base is the only exception, showing a slight increase
in search calls alongside a substantial accuracy gain. Because this
weak base model initially underuses search, the increase indicates more
appropriate tool invocation rather than degraded efficiency.
After training, the solver learns to better balance parametric knowledge and retrieval.
Overall, 
ZEST-Search
 improves the accuracy–cost trade-off by promoting selective search and avoiding redundant queries.

RQ4:

How does proposer heterogeneity affect task generation and training dynamics?
 
To analyze how proposer heterogeneity affects training, we compare proposers with different model scales, families, and instruction alignment.
First, cross-family proposers such as Qwen3-4B and LLaMA3.2-3B-Instruct introduce complementary knowledge priors and reasoning patterns, enabling larger solvers to benefit from diverse task distributions without requiring the matching scale of the proposer. This indicates that task generation quality is not strictly bounded by solver capacity.
Beyond task diversity, proposer heterogeneity also affects how quickly useful supervision emerges during training. As shown in Figure 
2
(a), newer or instruction-tuned proposers generate a higher proportion of valid and informative questions at the beginning of training, providing stronger optimization signals and accelerating solver improvement.
Overall, heterogeneous proposer–solver training decouples task generation cost, knowledge priors, and generation quality from solver capacity.

Tool-Efficient Search Optimization Analysis.

Figure 
2
(c) shows the accuracy–efficiency
trade-off across different tool-cost coefficients with 400 training steps. Without the
tool-aware reward, the solver achieves the highest accuracy but uses the
most searches. A moderate coefficient markedly reduces search calls
with only a minor accuracy loss, providing the best overall balance.
This demonstrates that the objective is not to minimize retrieval, but to optimize the accuracy-efficiency trade-off.
Larger coefficients further suppress retrieval but increasingly degrade
accuracy. These results show that the tool-aware reward provides
controllable search regularization: moderate penalties discourage
redundant retrieval, whereas excessive penalties can suppress searches
necessary for correct answers.

Related Work

Search-Augmented Language Models.

Retrieval-augmented language models use external knowledge to improve generation on knowledge-intensive tasks

(
Guu et al. 2020
; 
Karpukhin et al. 2020
; 
Lewis et al. 2020
)
.
Later systems integrate retrieval directly into reasoning, enabling models to issue queries, inspect evidence, and refine their answers

(
Nakano et al. 2021
; 
Yao et al. 2023
; 
Asai et al. 2024
; 
Trivedi et al. 2023
)
.
Search-agent reinforcement learning further optimizes when and how to search, initially through final-answer rewards and later through search-cost modeling, retrieval simulation, process supervision, and step-wise feedback

(
Jin et al. 2025
; 
Song et al. 2025
; 
Chen et al. 2025b
; 
Shi et al. 2025
; 
Sun et al. 2025
; 
Zheng et al. 2025
; 
Jiang et al. 2025b
; 
Ji et al. 2025
)
.
Unlike these methods, which mainly optimize solver behavior on given questions, 
ZEST-Search
 also brings evidence-grounded task construction into the training loop and explicitly optimizes trajectory-level search efficiency.

Proposer–Solver Training for Search Agents.

More broadly, LLM self-improvement methods generate training data online, with some casting task generation and solving as interacting roles

(
Wang et al. 2023
; 
Zhao et al. 2025
; 
Chen et al. 2025a
; 
Zhang et al. 2025b
; 
Castanyer et al. 2026
)
.
For search-agent training, EvolveSearch, Search Self-Play, and Dr. Zero adapt generated tasks according to solver feedback, allowing task difficulty to evolve during training

(
Zhang et al. 2025a
; 
Lu et al. 2025
; 
Yue et al. 2026
)
.
Recent work further incorporates structured evidence or finer-grained process signals. For example, knowledge-graph paths can guide question generation and provide waypoint rewards, while source-level contribution scores can assign credit to retrieved evidence

(
Wu et al. 2026
; 
Arai and Ichikawa 2026
)
.
In contrast, 
ZEST-Search
 generates retrieval-supported target entities and source-supported textual evidence paths online, rather than restricting task construction to fixed answer pools or precomputed graph paths. It also decouples the proposer and solver policies and coordinates their co-evolution through objectives for intermediate task difficulty and search efficiency.

Conclusion

We introduced 
ZEST-Search
, a zero-label framework for
tool-efficient search-agent training through heterogeneous
proposer–solver co-evolution. Rather than relying on predefined answer
pools or labeled QA pairs, 
ZEST-Search
 constructs validated
tasks online through answer-first generation and source-supported
multi-hop evidence paths. Lightweight, non-generative components perform
target validation, path construction, and task filtering without
additional LLM inference. Decoupling the two roles allows lightweight,
instruction-tuned, and cross-family proposers to generate complementary
tasks around the solver’s evolving capability boundary. Role-specific
objectives coordinate their co-evolution by steering the proposer toward
intermediate-difficulty tasks and rewarding correct solver trajectories
with lower relative search costs.
Experiments on multiple benchmarks show that 
ZEST-Search
 improves answer accuracy and tool efficiency without labeled searching data. The framework remains effective with smaller and cross-family proposers, while analyses validate the reward design and task generation. Overall, 
ZEST-Search
 provides a scalable approach to expanding the training frontier and optimizing search efficiency.

Ablation on Multi-Hop Task Complexity.

We further investigate whether increasing the complexity of generated tasks improves the solver’s multi-hop search capability. Specifically, we increase the maximum target path length to 4, allowing EPG to construct evidence paths containing up to four entities. To provide the solver with sufficient interaction budget for resolving these longer paths, we also increase the maximum number of search turns to 4, while keeping all other training and evaluation configurations unchanged. This controlled setting isolates the effect of longer evidence paths and more demanding multi-hop supervision.

As shown in Table 
5
, generating higher-hop tasks provides limited improvements on general QA benchmarks but leads to more substantial gains on multi-hop QA tasks. This suggests that the proposed evidence-path guided generation can effectively construct training tasks with stronger compositional reasoning requirements, enabling the solver to acquire genuine multi-hop search capabilities rather than merely improving single-step retrieval or answer matching.

B.3 Reward Dynamics

Reward Dynamics.

Figure 
5
 shows distinct but coupled reward dynamics for the proposer and solver. The solver reward rises rapidly during early training and then stabilizes at a high level, indicating that the solver progressively acquires and maintains correct, tool-efficient search behavior. In contrast, the proposer reward first increases and then gradually decreases to a stable positive range. This decline should not be interpreted as degraded proposer performance. Under the intermediate-difficulty objective, questions that are initially informative become easier as the solver improves, reducing their proposer reward and continuously shifting the preferred task distribution toward the solver’s evolving capability boundary. The sustained positive proposer reward suggests that the proposer continues to generate useful frontier tasks, while the stable solver reward indicates that this adaptive task generation does not destabilize solver optimization. Short-term fluctuations arise naturally from stochastic online task generation and grouped rollouts. Since the two roles use different reward definitions, their absolute reward values are not directly comparable; the key observation is their stable and coordinated evolution over training.

Appendix C: Lightweight Evidence-Path Construction and
Static Question Validation

C.1 Overview

This section describes the implementation of the evidence-path guided
question construction pipeline used in our training framework.
The goal of this module is to automatically construct corpus-supported
candidate questions from unlabeled references without invoking additional
LLM-based judges or human annotation.
Given a reference question, the pipeline first generates
a searchable target entity, verifies its existence through dense retrieval,
constructs an evidence-supported entity path using lightweight NLP
components,
and finally generates and validates a new question.

Unlike approaches that employ an additional language model or NLI-based
verifier to assess generated questions, our validation procedure relies on
retrieval grounding, entity-level consistency, and deterministic rules. This
design significantly reduces verification overhead while maintaining reliable
control over the generated training distribution.

The complete pipeline consists of four stages:
(1) target entity generation and retrieval-based verification,
(2) lightweight evidence-path construction,
(3) evidence-path guided question generation,
and (4) static question validation before solver rollout.

C.2 Retrieval-based Target Verification

Dense retrieval backbone.

All retrieval operations in the evidence construction module use the same
dense retrieval service. Specifically, we adopt

intfloat/e5-base-v2
 with a pre-built FAISS index over the
Wikipedia-2018 corpus. Each query is encoded with the required

query:

For target verification and evidence expansion, the retriever returns the
top-

environment, where each search action retrieves top-

retrieval results are only used as corpus grounding signals and do not
directly determine answer correctness.

Target entity generation.

The first proposer call does not answer the reference question. Instead, it
is instructed to infer the semantic type of the expected answer and generate
a different searchable entity with a similar semantic category. For example,
if the reference question expects a person, organization, location, or work,
the proposer generates another entity belonging to the same category. For
questions whose answers are numerical or temporal values, the proposer
identifies the corresponding entity or topic that determines such values and
uses it as the target anchor.

The generated target 
 must satisfy two requirements:

1.

it should represent a concrete searchable entity;

2.

it should be supported by the retrieval corpus.

Before retrieval verification, candidates undergo deterministic surface
filtering to remove malformed outputs, including empty strings, placeholders,
non-entity values, URLs, question-like generations, and residual generation
tags.

Retrieval support verification.

A candidate is considered retrieval-supported if its normalized form
satisfies at least one of the following conditions: (i) it exactly matches a
retrieved title; (ii) it and a retrieved title are mutually contained, with
both normalized strings containing at least four characters; (iii) its
token-set Jaccard similarity with a retrieved title is at least 
; or
(iv) it contains at most six tokens and appears within the first 1,200
characters of a retrieved passage.

This stage only verifies that the target entity has corpus support. It does
not perform entity linking, factual verification, or relation verification.

After path construction, one evidence path is selected for each target entity

contains:

The proposer is encouraged to utilize multiple evidence steps when supported
by the context, but shorter questions are allowed when a shorter composition
provides clearer and more reliable evidence.

The generation prompt specifies two desiderata: the question should be
consistent with the supplied evidence context, and the target answer should
not appear explicitly in the question. The deterministic validator directly
enforces the second requirement through normalized alias matching. The first
is encouraged through evidence-conditioned generation but is not separately
verified by an entailment model or an LLM judge.

The complete evidence path is therefore used as a controllable generation
signal rather than merely as additional retrieval information.

C.6 Computational Efficiency

The evidence construction and validation stages introduce no additional LLM
inference beyond the two proposer calls used for target and question
generation. As quantified in Table 
7
, evidence-path
construction requires 59.24 seconds over 500 reference questions, compared with 142.53 seconds for target generation and validation and 111.15 seconds for question generation and static verification.
Thus, the non-generative evidence stage contributes substantially less wall-clock time than either LLM-generation stage while preserving explicit evidence provenance for each accepted candidate.

Static acceptance is deliberately separated from solver-based evaluation.
The former removes malformed candidates and direct answer leakage before
rollout, whereas the latter supplies dynamic correctness, difficulty, and
tool-use signals only after acceptance. This ordering avoids spending solver
rollouts on candidates that already violate inexpensive deterministic
constraints.

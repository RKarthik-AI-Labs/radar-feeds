Abstract

We present CANDID (Candidate-Driven Intervention for Disciplined Execution), an execution-control framework designed to enhance the reliability of local computer-use agents (CUAs) in resource-constrained settings. While local CUAs feature robust baseline proficiency in single-step action prediction, they frequently fail at critical junctions due to rigid action loops or premature DONE decisions. Rather than imposing imprecise cognitive loads through textual reflection, CANDID repurposes parallel test-time scaling into a disciplined, execution-level intervention at sparse, failure-prone points. We introduce a loop-recovery mechanism to filter candidates away from repeated behaviors and a conservative, unanimous DONE gating policy that requires absolute consensus to terminate. Experiments on OSWorld demonstrate that CANDID’s structural control significantly improves success rates, outperforming feedback-based alternatives with minimal execution overhead.

Introduction

Computer-use agents (CUAs) have recently emerged as a promising paradigm for autonomous interaction with graphical user interfaces (GUIs), enabling multimodal agents to execute real-world desktop and web tasks by understanding screenshots and executing GUI actions. Recent advances in multimodal foundation models 
Qin et al. (2025)
; 
Wang et al. (2025)
; 
Xue et al. (2026)
; 
Xu et al. (2025)
; 
Lin et al. (2024)
 and agentic frameworks 
Agashe et al. (2024)
; 
Agashe et al. (2025)
; 
Gonzalez-Pumariega et al. (2026)
; 
Yang et al. (2025)
 have significantly improved long-horizon GUI interaction capabilities across increasingly realistic environments 
Xie et al. (2024)
; 
Zhou et al. (2024)
; 
Bonatti et al. (2024)
; 
Deng et al. (2023)
. In particular, modern CUA systems leverage hierarchical planning 
Agashe et al. (2024)
, memory retrieval 
Liu et al. (2025)
, and test-time scaling 
Yang et al. (2025)
 strategies to improve execution robustness and task success rates.

Deploying CUAs under local, resource-constrained settings is practically important, and small language model (SLM)-based agentic systems have been increasingly proposed toward this goal 
Belcak et al. (2025)
. Yet such deployment remains highly challenging: unlike large cloud-based systems that rely on expensive multi-agent orchestration 
Song et al. (2026)
; 
Yang et al. (2026)
; 
Han et al. (2026)
 or proprietary reasoning models 
Anthropic (2026a)
; 
Singh et al. (2026)
, local CUAs operate with limited model sizes and weaker multimodal reasoning. Under these conditions, simply increasing execution horizons or enlarging multimodal histories often fails to reliably improve performance. The central obstacle is that local agents fail in two coupled ways: they fall into repetitive action loops, or terminate prematurely before completing the task. Moreover, naively scaling the step budget or contextual history merely shifts the failure pattern between the two, rather than resolving both.

Prior methods for recovering failed agent trajectories primarily rely on textual reflection or verifier feedback 
Shinn et al. (2023)
; 
Han et al. (2026)
, where the agent inspects its own recent trajectory, generates natural-language feedback, and incorporates it before revising its next action. However, we argue that such feedback-based approaches provide limited benefit for lightweight local CUAs, whose reasoning capabilities are often insufficient either to generate meaningful textual feedback about the failure or to convert given feedback into distinct, executable GUI actions. These limitations prevent agents from escaping repetitive action loops or improving termination decisions under ambiguous completion states, often causing the model to still repeat its failed behavior under a restated rationale.

To overcome these limitations, we instead build on a key observation about local CUA behavior: although they struggle to assess trajectory progress, they remain capable of generating multiple plausible actions—which we refer to as 
candidates
—from the same interface state. This suggests that the bottleneck lies not in the availability of good candidates, but in committing to one when progress or completion is uncertain. We therefore propose CANDID, a candidate-based control framework that treats candidate disagreement as an execution-control signal, rather than asking the model to reflect on its own failure. CANDID instantiates this principle through two modules: (1) a loop-recovery module that identifies repetitive behavior through rule-based detection and selects an alternative candidate, and (2) a unanimous DONE gating module that accepts termination only when all sampled candidates agree. CANDID applies this sparse intervention only at these failure-prone points, concentrating computation where it is most beneficial while keeping overhead low.

We evaluate CANDID on the OSWorld benchmark 
Xie et al. (2024)
 using multiple local CUA models, including Qwen3-VL 
Bai et al. (2025)
 and OpenCUA 
Wang et al. (2025)
 variants. Experimental results show that CANDID consistently improves task success rates across all evaluated models, outperforming both feedback-based interventions and a test-time scaling baseline. Further analyses show that its two control modules address complementary failure modes, and that sparse intervention is more effective than applying candidate selection uniformly throughout execution.

Our contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We identify repetitive action loops and premature false success as dominant coupled failure modes in local CUAs that cannot be reliably resolved through naive execution scaling or textual feedback.

•

We propose CANDID, a sparse, candidate-based control framework that leverages multiple candidates generated by the same local agent at failure-prone steps, through 
loop-recovery candidate filtering
 and 
unanimous
 
DONE
 
gating
, without modifying the model.

•

We demonstrate that CANDID consistently improves task success across multiple local CUAs, outperforming feedback-based and test-time scaling baselines, and through targeted ablations we show that these gains stem specifically from CANDID’s sparse, candidate-driven intervention design with minimal runtime overhead.

Prior autonomous-agent frameworks often use prompt-level verification or self-reflection to mitigate execution failures 
Han et al. (2026)
; 
Pan et al. (2024)
; 
Shinn et al. (2023)
. These methods work well with proprietary, large-scale reasoning models, whose stronger language understanding lets verbal feedback translate into corrective actions. Inspired by VLAA-GUI 
Han et al. (2026)
, we implement two representative self-feedback mechanisms: a 
Loop Breaker
, which detects repetitive behavior and appends a textual recovery reflection, and a 
Verifier
, which rejects premature 
DONE
 predictions and provides corrective feedback.

However, such textual reflection offers limited recovery capability for smaller, local-scale CUA models, as verbal critiques often fail to induce a distinct executable action once the agent enters a localized loop or faces goal ambiguity. As shown in Table 
1
, Loop Breaker feedback frequently leads the agent to repeat the same action or target and ultimately fail; for instance, after repeatedly attempting to enable a toggle switch, the reflection merely restates the same instruction rather than suggesting an alternative action. Similarly, after a rejected 
DONE
 prediction, the agent regenerates 
DONE
 or repeats a recent target, again resulting in failure. These results highlight the limited effectiveness of textual feedback for local CUAs.

Generating Failure-Escaping Candidates

Although local CUAs often remain trapped in repetitive loops or terminate prematurely, these failures do not necessarily indicate an inability to generate useful executable actions. As illustrated in Fig. 
3
, sampling multiple actions from the same execution state can reveal executable alternatives that escape the current failure pattern: an agent that repeatedly presses 
Ctrl+V
 despite an empty clipboard may instead generate an action that types the URL directly, while an agent that prematurely declares completion may generate a continuation action that resumes document editing. This suggests that the bottleneck lies not in generating executable alternatives, but in committing to a single action despite disagreement among plausible candidates. This capability is not limited to isolated examples: when the agent exhibits repetitive-action behavior, all four sampled candidates are distinct at the exact-action level in 47.7% of cases, whereas complete candidate collapse occurs in only 8.4% (Appendix 
B.1
).

3 
Method

We propose CANDID, a sparse, candidate-based execution-control framework for local CUAs that intervenes at failure-prone steps to address two coupled failure modes: repetitive action loops and premature 
DONE
 decisions. Our key intuition is that local CUAs can often generate multiple plausible candidates from the same interface state but fail by committing to a single one under uncertain execution states. Instead of sampling throughout execution, CANDID generates a small set of candidates only at these high-risk points, recovering from loops by selecting an alternative candidate and preventing premature termination by requiring unanimous agreement before accepting 
DONE
.

3.1 
Loop-Recovery Candidate Selection

Fig. 
4
(a) illustrates CANDID’s loop-recovery pipeline. At each step, the agent predicts its next action from the current screenshot and recent history. When the proposed action is identified as a potential repetitive loop and confirmed as stagnant, CANDID samples alternative candidates, filters out those matching the detected loop pattern, and executes one of the remaining candidates.

Loop detection.

Judging whether the agent is stuck in a loop is itself unreliable: the local CUA often cannot assess its own progress, while pure rule-based heuristics risk flagging legitimate repetition as a loop. We therefore use a two-stage gate that separates cheap detection from reliable confirmation. A lightweight rule-based detector first compares the proposed action against a sliding window of recent actions (governed by a window size, a minimum repeat count, and a target-coordinate tolerance) and flags repetitive patterns—for example, repeated scrolling is flagged as 
repeated_targetless_action_type
. Only for flagged cases does the local model then confirm whether the trajectory is genuinely stuck in a loop. This confines the model’s harder judgment to the rare cases where it is needed, while leaving the initial action untouched otherwise. Further details and sensitivity analysis are in Appendix 
A.1
 and Appendix 
B.2
, respectively.

Recovery candidate generation.

Simply requesting a single new action from the same context after a loop is confirmed often reproduces the same failed action. Instead, once a loop is confirmed, the local agent samples a small set of executable candidates (a hyperparameter set to four in our main experiments) from the current environment state, leveraging its demonstrated ability to produce diverse actions under repeated sampling. These candidates may include both repeated and alternative actions, and are passed to the filtering stage.

Filtering and selection.

Before the agent selects among candidates, CANDID first narrows the pool to reduce selection overhead: malformed or non-executable actions are removed, 
DONE
 is disallowed during loop recovery, candidates matching the detected loop pattern are excluded, and duplicates are merged. If no valid candidate remains, CANDID falls back to the original proposal; if exactly one remains, it is executed directly; otherwise, the agent selects among the filtered candidates based on the task instruction and recent trajectory.

3.2 
Unanimous DONE Gating

Local CUAs frequently misjudge task completion, often terminating prematurely (Section 
2.2
), motivating a conservative approach to accepting 
DONE
. Fig. 
4
(b) illustrates CANDID’s unanimous 
DONE
 gating module, which is triggered only when the agent proposes 
DONE
. Rather than accepting a single termination prediction, CANDID samples additional candidates from the same context and accepts 
DONE
 only when all valid candidates agree (Case 1); even a single continuation candidate, such as 
WAIT
 or 
click
, is treated as evidence that the task may still be unfinished, in which case termination is rejected and all 
DONE
 candidates are removed (Case 2). This conservative policy uses the agent’s own candidate diversity to challenge unsupported 
DONE
 decisions without relying on an external verifier. We further show in Section 
4.3
 that this unanimous policy more effectively suppresses premature completions than a majority voting.

Candidate selection.

Once 
DONE
 candidates are removed, the remaining continuation candidates are processed for execution. In Case 2, for example, the sampled set contains 
DONE
, 
WAIT
, and 
click
; after filtering, only 
WAIT
 and 
click
 remain as continuation candidates, and exact duplicates are merged. If a single continuation candidate remains, it is executed directly; otherwise, the local agent selects one from the remaining continuation candidates based on the task instruction, current screenshot, and recent trajectory. The selector is restricted to choosing among the generated candidates and cannot introduce a new action.

4.1 
Setup

Benchmark and Evaluation Setting.

We evaluate our framework primarily on OSWorld 
Xie et al. (2024)
, excluding eight Google Drive-related tasks and reporting results on 361 tasks across five domains. We additionally assess cross-benchmark generalization on WindowsAgentArena 
Bonatti et al. (2024)
 with 154 tasks, with detailed settings and results provided in Appendix 
B.4
. All agents use screenshot observations, generate PyAutoGUI-style actions, and use four recent screenshots as history (
) for the main experiments.

Models.

Our evaluation covers three local computer-use agents: Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B-Instruct, Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct, and OpenCUA-7B. This model selection encompasses both general-purpose vision-language models adapted to desktop control and models specialized for computer-use behavior, allowing us to test the proposed control mechanisms across different model scales and agent families.

Candidate sampling configuration.

For both loop recovery and unanimous 
DONE
 selection, we fix the number of additional sampled candidates to four.
For Qwen-based agents, we use a temperature of 0.7 for default action generation and 
DONE
 candidate sampling, and a higher temperature of 1.0 for loop-recovery candidate generation to encourage greater action diversity under repetitive execution patterns.
For OpenCUA-7B, the default action generation uses greedy decoding (temperature 0.0), while candidate generation uses a temperature of 0.7 for both loop recovery and 
DONE
 selection.

Inference-Time Control Baselines.

We compare CANDID against several inference-time control baselines. For test-time scaling, we apply GTA-1 
Yang et al. (2025)
, using the local CUAs as the planner while retaining the original GTA-1-7B grounding model and sampling four plans. We follow the publicly released GTA-1 implementation, including its available prompting and inference procedure, without modifying the disclosed components other than replacing the planner with the evaluated local CUA. We also implement step-wise 
Self-Guidance
, which generates compact progress feedback after each step and appends it to the next action-generation prompt. Finally, we implement VLAA-GUI-style 
Han et al. (2026)
 
Loop Breaker
 and 
Verifier
 modules by adapting their core intervention concepts for local CUA evaluation, rather than reproducing the full multi-agent architecture. Additional details are provided in Appendix 
C
.

Ablation and Design Analysis

Complementary effects of control modules.

Fig. 
5
 shows that the two control modules affect distinct failure modes. Loop-only substantially reduces repetitive action loops but shifts some failures toward premature false success, while Done-only suppresses premature false success at the cost of increased execution exhaustion. Combining both modules mitigates this trade-off and achieves the highest average score of 38.8%, demonstrating that loop recovery and conservative termination control are most effective when applied jointly.

Majority vs. Unanimous DONE.

Table 
5
 compares majority voting with the proposed unanimous termination policy. Majority voting provides only a marginal improvement over the baseline, with a redirection rate of 5.53%. In contrast, unanimous selection redirects 49.93% of proposed terminations and reduces false success from 241.33 to 171.67, improving task accuracy from 26.06% to 30.60%. Although this increases max-step failures, the overall result indicates that majority agreement is too weak to counter the model’s termination bias.

While unanimous gating more effectively suppresses premature termination, it does not constitute a reliable semantic completion verifier. In the task-level proxy analysis, it achieves 56.7% precision and 26.3% specificity, compared with 54.3% and 15.2% for the VLAA-GUI-style Verifier, respectively (Appendix 
B.3.2
). Thus, CANDID primarily acts as a conservative filter against unsupported termination rather than solving completion verification itself.

CANDID increases prompt-token usage by only 19%, while completion tokens, despite a larger relative increase from candidate generation and selection, remain negligible compared with prompt tokens. CANDID also shortens the average trajectory from 25.6 to 24.4 steps per task, partially offsetting the additional inference cost. Consequently, total execution time increases by only 10% (359.0
396.1 s/task), indicating that CANDID improves task performance with modest additional cost.

6 
Conclusion

We presented CANDID, a candidate-based execution-control framework for local computer-use agents that addresses two coupled failure modes: repetitive action loops and premature DONE decisions, both of which persist under naive execution scaling and textual feedback. By leveraging alternative actions generated by the local agent and intervening only at failure-prone decision points, CANDID recovers from repetitive behavior and challenges unsupported termination. Experiments on OSWorld demonstrate consistent improvements across multiple local CUAs over feedback-based alternatives, with minimal execution overhead. These results suggest that selectively allocating inference-time computation to high-risk execution states can be more effective than uniformly scaling computation throughout the trajectory.

Limitations

Our framework focuses on lightweight execution-time control for local computer-use agents and is evaluated primarily on OSWorld desktop environments. While the proposed mechanisms consistently improve robustness across multiple local CUAs, the current study does not explore broader settings such as mobile environments, large-scale cloud agents, or long-horizon multi-agent orchestration. CANDID also depends on the base agent producing sufficiently diverse and useful candidates; when sampled candidates collapse to the same failed behavior, candidate-based recovery provides limited benefit. Moreover, the current loop detector targets relatively local repetitive patterns and may miss longer-range or irregular failure cycles. Our candidate selection strategy relies on lightweight heuristic filtering and local reranking, and we do not yet integrate CANDID with higher-level planning or memory modules. Extending the framework with adaptive triggering, stronger verification, and higher-level agentic signals remains an important direction for future work.

Appendix A 
CANDID Implementation

A.1 
Loop Detection Details

This section details the loop detection procedure used by the loop-recovery module in Section 
3.1
. The implementation follows a two-stage detection. First, a deterministic rule detector checks whether the newly proposed action would extend a recent repetitive pattern. Second, the local model confirms that the trajectory is stagnant before recovery candidates are generated.

Action signatures.

This section describes three feedback-style baselines implemented for the comparison. Self-Guidance is our own lightweight prompt-based intervention. The Loop Breaker and Verifier are 
VLAA-GUI-style
 implementations: they are inspired by the STOP and RECOVER components described in VLAA-GUI, but they are adapted to our PyAutoGUI action space and local-model setting rather than reproduced exactly.

Self-Guidance

Self-Guidance runs a separate diagnostic prompt before normal action generation
and injects the resulting text into the next action prompt as 
Guidance
.
The guidance model discusses progress, local loops, what to avoid, the next
direction, and whether task completion looks unsafe.

The system prompt asks the model to diagnose the desktop trajectory:

The user message contains the task instruction, recent screenshots and actions,

C.2 
VLAA-GUI-Style Loop Breaker

The VLAA-GUI-style Loop Breaker is a reflection module that runs before action generation when previous responses exist. It follows the RECOVER idea from VLAA-GUI: inspect recent trajectory evidence, decide whether the agent is stuck in repetitive no-progress behavior, and emit a strategy-change signal. In our implementation, the module does not directly choose an action. Instead, when it returns a loop signal, it writes a textual loop-recovery constraint into the next action prompt.

Prompt skeleton.

The system prompt is:

System Prompt

You are a reflection agent for a desktop automation task. Analyze the recent trajectory and current screenshot for repetitive no-progress behavior. Focus only on loop recovery. Do not judge whether the whole task is complete. Return only a valid JSON object.

At each step, the user message includes the latest assistant response, the parsed action, and optionally the current screenshot. The requested schema is:

Requested Output Schema

{"progress_signal": "what visibly changed, or no visible progress",

"loop_detected": true/false,

"loop_evidence": "short evidence",

"strategy_signal": "KEEP or SWITCH",

"avoid_actions": ["short action/target patterns to avoid"],

"escape_direction": "short direction for changing approach"}

Output use.

If 
loop_detected=true
 or 
strategy_signal=SWITCH
, the module formats the result into a constraint block:

Control Block Appended to the User Prompt

- strategy_signal: SWITCH

- loop_evidence: <evidence>

- avoid_actions: <patterns>

- escape_direction: <direction>

The next action must change approach and must not match
avoid_actions.

This block is injected into the next action-generation prompt. The enforcement
mode is prompt-only: unlike our candidate-based loop recovery, the VLAA-GUI-style
Loop Breaker does not sample multiple executable candidates or remove repeated
actions through a hard filter.

C.3 
VLAA-GUI-Style Completion Verifier

The VLAA-GUI-style Verifier is activated only when the normal action model proposes 
DONE
. It follows the STOP idea from VLAA-GUI: accept task termination only when the current screenshot or recent trajectory provides direct evidence that every task requirement is satisfied. Our implementation is intentionally conservative and rejects ambiguous completion claims.

Prompt skeleton.

The system prompt is:

System Prompt

You are an extremely strict task completion verifier for desktop automation. Decide whether the task is already fully completed right now. Mark completion only if every requirement has direct, unambiguous evidence in the current screenshot or recent trajectory text. Do not assume hidden state or intent. For side effects such as saving, exporting, sending, creating, deleting, or changing settings, require visible or trajectory evidence that the side effect was committed. Prefer false negatives over false positives. Return only a valid JSON object.

The user message includes the task instruction, recent trajectory text, the candidate final response, and the parsed termination action. The requested schema is:

Requested Output Schema

{"complete": true/false,

"reason": "short evidence-based reason",

"missing_steps": "what evidence/action is still needed, or empty if
complete"}

Output use.

If the Verifier returns 
complete=false
, or if it returns

complete=true
 while still mentioning uncertainty or missing steps, the agent rejects the termination. The current action is converted to a wait-style continuation, and the next prompt receives feedback of the form:

Control Block Appended to the User Prompt

Completion verifier rejected the previous terminate action:

- reason: <reason>

- missing_steps: <missing evidence or action>

Continue the task. Do not terminate again until the missing evidence is visible.

Thus, the VLAA-GUI-style Verifier is a feedback mechanism rather than a candidate-selection mechanism: it can block a 
DONE
 action and provide textual correction, but it does not itself select among concrete continuation actions.

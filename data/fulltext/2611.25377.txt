Abstract.

People often watch videos to find information, but locating relevant moments can be difficult and time-consuming.
Existing strategies like scrubbing rely primarily on visual scanning and linear search, providing limited support for goal-directed navigation.
We present 
VideoTrail
, an AI-powered browser extension that supports information seeking in videos through a hierarchical visualization.
Inspired by information foraging theory, 
VideoTrail
 surfaces semantic and structural cues as information scent, creating an explorable roadmap of segments.
The system is multimodal and adaptive, prioritizing visual or textual scent based on the video’s content.
In a user study (
), 
VideoTrail
 helped participants efficiently locate key moments and enabled new navigation strategies compared to traditional timelines, highlighting how exposing an adaptive video structure can enhance goal-directed viewing.

Keywords: 
Video navigation, AI-assisted video understanding, interactive visualization, adaptive interface, information foraging theory

1. 
Introduction

People often watch online videos to seek information — whether learning how to fix a toaster, planning a trip, or reviewing a math concept.
However, video platforms such as YouTube are primarily designed for linear playback and passive consumption 
(
Weir et al., 2015
; 
Liu et al., 2018
; 
Kim et al., 2014a
)
, providing little support for 
goal-directed viewing
, where users watch with a specific objective rather than pure entertainment 
(
Seo et al., 2021
; 
Pavel et al., 2014
)
.
As a result, much of this information remains locked inside black boxes of video.
Even with a clear viewing goal, finding relevant moments is time-consuming and challenging, often requiring manual scrubbing through long, unstructured timelines 
(
van Houten et al., 2004
; 
Yang et al., 2025
)
.

In this paper, we explore how video interfaces can return control to users.
Our approach is guided by two complementary ideas.
First, recent advances in multimodal AI enable richer video understanding and new forms of interaction 
(
Tang et al., 2026
; 
Li et al., 2025
; 
Maaz et al., 2024
; 
Hua et al., 2025
)
, which we leverage to support goal-directed viewing.
Second, to ensure that we design useful interactions for viewers, we draw on information foraging theory (IFT) 
(
Pirolli and Card, 1999
; 
Pirolli and Card, 2005
)
, which models information seeking as a selective process of evaluating cues or “scent.”
Applied to video, this process is known as 
video foraging
 
(
van Houten, 2009
; 
van Houten et al., 2004
)
,
and 
we extend this framing to a complementary task we call 
reforaging
: rediscovering information in familiar videos.

We conducted a formative study (
) to examine current foraging practices 
and collect feedback on our initial design ideas.

Consistent with IFT, users rarely watched videos sequentially; instead, they iteratively skimmed, jumped, and refined their search to locate relevant moments.
However, existing skimming strategies often rely on a single modality (e.g., visual scrubbing) 
(
Pavel et al., 2014
; 
Matejka et al., 2013
; 
Fraser et al., 2020
)
, which provides weak information scent for certain videos (e.g., interviews).
They also largely assume linear navigation 
(
Jin et al., 2017
; 
Christel et al., 1999
; 
Truong et al., 2021
)
, while goal-directed viewing is selective and uneven
— requiring deep engagement with some segments while skipping others entirely.
Consequently, users may struggle to efficiently and accurately assess the informational
value of clips 
in both familiar and unfamiliar videos
 
(
Christel, 2008
; 
Albertson, 2013
)
.

To address these challenges, we introduce 
VideoTrail
, an AI-powered browser extension for foraging and reforaging through 
semantic navigation
.
Instead of a linear timeline, 
VideoTrail
 visualizes a video as a hierarchy of segments (
Fig. 1
), creating an explorable and searchable roadmap.
The hierarchical representation enables users to maintain a high-level overview while progressively drilling down into segments of interest, supporting diverse viewing goals.
To guide foraging, 
VideoTrail
 adaptively weights visual vs. textual information scent based on video content.
It also generates contextual collections and color scales to further aid exploration.
Underlying the system is a multimodal LLM (MLLM) pipeline that automatically extracts detailed semantic structure from videos.

We evaluated 
VideoTrail
 through a two-part user study (
) combining controlled comparisons with YouTube and open-ended exploration of participant-selected videos.
Results show that 
VideoTrail
’s adaptive, hierarchical summaries support foraging across various video types, lowering the cognitive cost of exploration and allowing users to quickly locate new information and revisit previously encountered content.
Participants also developed a clearer understanding of video structure and discovered unexpected moments of interest, suggesting further opportunities for AI-powered visualizations to aid navigation and sensemaking of temporal data.

Our contributions include:

•

A formative study
 characterizing the limitations of current video interfaces and design opportunities for goal-directed viewing through the lens of IFT.

•

VideoTrail
, a browser extension
 that supports 
both
 foraging and reforaging through multimodal, hierarchical visualizations that adapt to video content.

•

User study findings
 demonstrating how surfacing semantic structure and contextual information scent enhances video skimming and navigation beyond linear timelines.

2. 
Related Work

As AI makes it easier and faster to extract structured information from videos 
(
Tang et al., 2026
; 
Hua et al., 2025
)
, we focus on the complementary challenge of making that structure useful for goal-directed viewing.
Our approach bridges work in video summarization and representation, structured video navigation, AI-assisted video understanding, and information foraging.

2.1. 
Video Summarization and Visualization

Video summarization approaches are typically categorized as extractive or abstractive 
(
Alaa et al., 2024
; 
Christel et al., 1999
; 
Gupta and Sharma, 2023
)
.

Extractive
 methods select and preserve content from the original video, often through visual formats such as image collages 
(
Chen et al., 2012
; 
Mei et al., 2009
; 
Goldman et al., 2006
)
, storyboards 
(
Furini et al., 2007
; 
Macer and Thomas, 1996
; 
Narasimhan et al., 2022
; 
Hürst and Darzentas, 2012
; 
Dawes, 2004
)
, or highlight reels 
(
Vo and Vo, 2025
; 
Jin et al., 2017
; 
Lin et al., 2024
; 
Kim et al., 2025
; 
Narasimhan et al., 2021
; 
Patel et al., 2024
; 
Wu et al., 2022
; 
Wang et al., 2024a
)
.
Conversely, 
abstractive
 approaches generate new representations, such as stylized comic strips 
(
Pęśko et al., 2019
; 
Wang et al., 2012
; 
Boreczky et al., 2000
; 
Calic et al., 2007
; 
Wang et al., 2025
)
 or text summaries 
(
Wang et al., 2022
; 
Palaskar et al., 2019
; 
Dilawari and Khan, 2019
)
.

Recent work pairs extractive keyframes with abstractive captions for richer, multimodal context 
(
Barua et al., 2025
; 
Hua et al., 2025
; 
Lee et al., 2025
; 
Kudo et al., 2023
)
.
Visual analytics offers another approach for summarizing videos 
(
Jorge and Chambel, 2013
; 
Zeng et al., 2020
)
, for example, through shot clusterings 
(
Junyent et al., 2015
)
, character networks 
(
Vicol et al., 2018
; 
Zhang et al., 2014
)
, and annotated timelines 
(
Kurzhals et al., 2016
; 
Li et al., 2024
)
.

Building on this work, we design interactive, multimodal video representations to surface information scent and support active, goal-directed exploration.

2.2. 
Navigating Videos Through Structured Representations

Prior interfaces augment navigation by enhancing video timelines with grid-based scrubbing 
(
Matejka et al., 2013
)
, dynamic speed adjustments 
(
Pongnumkul et al., 2010
)
, or interaction overlays 
(
Kim et al., 2014b
; 
Al-Hajri et al., 2014
)
.
Video Tapestry 
(
Barnes et al., 2010
)
, SpiralTape 
(
Liu et al., 2016
)
, and VideoTrees 
(
Jansen et al., 2008
)
 use zoomable, multi-scale representations to navigate videos, but rely primarily on visual cues.

Other systems expose higher-level semantic structure to facilitate skimming and navigation, often via video chapters 
(
Ventura et al., 2025
; 
Fraser et al., 2020
; 
Pu et al., 2025
)
 (e.g., YouTube 
(
Help, 2026
)
) or hierarchical summaries 
(
Girgensohn et al., 2011
; 
Jiang et al., 2016
; 
Van Daele et al., 2024
; 
Weir et al., 2015
)
.
Structured interfaces such as Video Digests 
(
Pavel et al., 2014
)
 and Visual Transcripts 
(
Shin et al., 2015
)
 support lecture videos, while others focus on music 
(
Wang et al., 2021
)
 or makeup tutorials 
(
Truong et al., 2021
)
.
Recent works also explore nonlinear concept maps 
(
Liu et al., 2018
)
 and dependency graphs 
(
Chen et al., 2024
; 
Yang et al., 2022
)
.
However, these systems often rely on domain-specific heuristics or use fixed (e.g., two-level) hierarchies.

With 
VideoTrail
, we investigate how to surface multimodal video structure that adapts to different content types and enables navigation at multiple granularities based on user goals.

2.3. 
AI-Assisted Video Understanding

Multimodal AI now enables on-demand video summaries without manual annotation 
(
Weir et al., 2015
; 
Pavel et al., 2014
; 
Kim et al., 2014b
; 
Psallidas and Spyrou, 2023
)
 or specialized pipelines 
(
Truong et al., 2021
; 
Chen et al., 2012
)
.
Recent MLLM-based systems support conversational interaction, allowing users to query videos and locate specific timestamps 
(
Li et al., 2025
; 
Maaz et al., 2024
; 
Tang et al., 2026
)
.
WatchWithMe 
(
Chi et al., 2025
)
 facilitates guided watching of review videos, StoryNavi 
(
Xu et al., 2024
)
 enables narrative-based reconstruction, and TLDW 
(
Zhang, 2025
)
 generates personalized highlight reels.
YouTube also recently integrated an AI-powered “ask” feature 
(
Shrivastava, 2025
)
.

While these systems prioritize quick, text-based access to content, complementary work uses AI to restructure video into interactive representations that enable deeper comprehension and reasoning 
(
Xia et al., 2024
)
.
For instance, VideoMix 
(
Yang et al., 2025
)
 and TutoAI 
(
Chen et al., 2024
)
 extract video structure to support cross-video summarization and mixed-media tutorial authoring.

Our work instead uses AI-extracted structure to support ongoing information seeking within a single video, helping viewers assess segment relevance and move between levels of abstraction when deciding where to explore next.

2.4. 
Information Foraging and Reforaging

Information foraging theory (IFT) models how people seek information on the web by following “scent” — cues that signal value — across “patches,” or structural units of information 
(
Pirolli and Card, 1999
; 
Pirolli and Card, 2005
)
.
IFT has informed the design of systems for online research 
(
Rachatasumrit et al., 2021
)
, document exploration 
(
Fok et al., 2024
)
, and LLM-assisted sensemaking 
(
Suh et al., 2023
)
.
Extending IFT to video, 
van Houten (2009)
 introduced 
video foraging
, which frames contiguous video segments as patches.
Because video is time-based, navigation is particularly costly, making strong information scent critical 
(
van Houten et al., 2004
)
.
While foraging occurs both within and across videos 
(
van Houten, 2009
)
, our focus is the former.

Much IFT work focuses on finding information in new environments 
(
Rachatasumrit et al., 2021
; 
Suh et al., 2023
; 
Fok et al., 2024
; 
van Houten et al., 2004
)
, but seeking previously encountered content often relies on different cues and strategies 
(
Tyler and Teevan, 2010
; 
Capra and Perez-Quinones, 2005
)
.
Video interfaces have supported revisiting information through personal viewing histories 
(
Al-Hajri et al., 2014
)
, bookmarks 
(
Kim et al., 2014a
)
, and synchronized views of scripts and summaries 
(
Pavel et al., 2015
)
.
We extend this work by foregrounding the task of 
reforaging
, i.e., refinding moments in familiar videos.
Specifically, we explore how generative AI can support both foraging and reforaging by exposing semantic structure and adaptive multimodal cues as information scent.

3. 
Formative Study: Exploring Design Opportunities for Video Foraging and Reforaging

We interviewed six participants (
F1-6
) at Harvard University to (1) understand existing workflows and challenges when seeking information within videos and (2) collect feedback on an early prototype, which we used as a probe 
(
Boehner et al., 2007
; 
Graham and Rouncefield, 2008
)
 to evaluate our initial design ideas.

In each 30-minute session, participants first described their current practices for goal-directed viewing on YouTube, then explored two self-selected videos with our prototype: one familiar and one unfamiliar.
Videos ranged from visual how-tos (e.g., recipes, art tutorials) and math explainers to dialogue-driven content (e.g., video essays, interviews), allowing us to examine how a hierarchical, multimodal visualization could support diverse content types and information needs.

3.1. 
Video Foraging Practices & Challenges

Users distinguished goal-based consumption from entertainment-driven viewing, which is typically sequential:

“Comedy shows [and] movies always have surprising turning points… so I watch them from start to finish, because that’s the art they’re trying to make”
 (F2).
In contrast, during 
information seeking
, participants rarely watched videos linearly.
Instead, they selectively navigated and sampled segments to identify relevant or interesting content, especially in longer videos (
).
This behavior mirrors the process of iteratively assessing information scent to decide how to navigate between patches (i.e., video segments) in IFT 
(
Pirolli and Card, 2005
; 
van Houten, 2009
)
.

All participants described 
skimming
 as their main way of finding information in videos, using strategies such as fast-forwarding or increasing playback speed (
), scrubbing the timeline (
), or reading the transcript (
).
However, three recurring challenges emerged from these approaches.

3.1.1. 
Weak information scent from single-modality cues

Five participants described skimming as a “blind” trial-and-error process because contextual cues are typically 
limited to a single modality
 — e.g., visual thumbnails for scrubbing or text for transcripts.
As videos are inherently multimodal, this obscures key signals about a segment’s informational value 
(
Jorge and Chambel, 2013
; 
Wingstedt et al., 2010
)
.
F3 shared that 
“lectures in particular”
 are challenging to skim, 
“because just scrubbing through the video, it doesn’t look that different.”

Similarly, F4 wanted more semantic context when navigating video essays: 
“I’d like to see at a glance, what the overall argument is. I don’t want to constantly click to get that information.”

3.1.2. 
Limited support for nonlinear information seeking

While current skimming methods enable faster traversal of time, they largely 
assume linear, uniform consumption
 and do not account for differences in informational value across segments (
).
This creates a mismatch with goal-directed viewing, which participants described as inherently selective and nonlinear.
Rather than traversing chronologically, they wanted to filter segments by intent: 
“I’m usually looking for something pretty specific so it’d be great if I could just explore those aspects [of the video]”
 (F6).
However, with existing interfaces, users frequently had to probe multiple clips before finding relevant content, increasing interaction cost.
As F3 explained, 
“it takes me forever to find the interesting parts… there’s just so much random stuff to sort through.”

3.1.3. 
Challenges persist when reforaging familiar videos.

Prior familiarity did not eliminate these navigation challenges.
For example, F1 described how he 
“often like[s] to revisit”
 math explainers, but still has 
“to guess where things are”
 each time.
Participants also emphasized the 
need for adaptive depth
, especially when rewatching content.
While scrubbing a familiar video, F5 noted, 
“Actually, maybe I’d skip this part since I already know it from [before], but here, I’d want to dive deeper to learn more.”

Thus, reforaging involves not only relocating previously encountered information, but also deciding what to skip, revisit, or explore further.

3.2. 
Design Probe Feedback

These findings suggest opportunities to better support 
nonlinear, selective
 information seeking, 
whether viewers are exploring a video for the first time or returning to familiar content.
To explore this space, we used our early prototype to probe two initial design ideas: representing videos as a navigable semantic hierarchy, and combining visual and textual cues to strengthen information scent (see 
App. A
).

3.2.1. 
Strengths

All six participants found the 
hierarchical visualization
 useful — particularly for foraging long-form content.
F2 felt it offered 
“more precise [and] convenient”
 navigation because of the 
“semantic segments that reveal the video’s structure.”

F6 appreciated being able to focus on specific portions of an art video: 
“I like how I can just expand the section I’m interested in, and then maybe I could take a screenshot and use that as my mini tutorial… [that] would be super useful for revisiting in the future too.”

Participants also valued the system’s diverse, 
multimodal cues
 (
).
F1 said, 
“Even just seeing the keywords and descriptions [along with] the keyframe was useful for finding specific information.”

3.2.2. 
Areas for improvement

The probe also revealed opportunities to refine our design.

Four participants asked to 
customize scent:
 
“For some videos, I don’t think the keywords would be that helpful. But for something like this [video essay], I’d actually want to see even more text”
 (F5).

Participants also suggested ways to reduce visual complexity, e.g., prioritizing 
progressive expansion
 instead of showing the full video hierarchy at once (
).

Additionally, three wanted to annotate and save segments, suggesting more iterative, personalized ways to seek and revisit information.

App. A
 includes a more detailed account of how our prototype evolved after the formative study.

3.3. 
Design Goals

Together, participants’ existing practices, probe feedback, and IFT informed three design goals for supporting both video foraging and reforaging:

D1.

Help viewers locate and relocate relevant segments.

D2.

Enable nonlinear, semantic navigation with progressive disclosure.

D3.

Surface salient cues for both dialogue- and visual-driven content.

To address these goals, we developed a data pipeline (
Sec. 4
) and 
final interface
 (
Sec. 5
) that transform unstructured videos into navigable, hierarchical structures.

Our core contribution is the latter, but we first describe the pipeline as it defines the semantic representation our system visualizes and interacts with.

4. 
Data Extraction Pipeline: Surfacing Hierarchical Video Structure With AI

Figure 2. 
Overview of our AI-powered data pipeline.
 We collect overall characteristics about the input video, followed by recursive segmentation to form the hierarchical data structure. The pipeline also returns a list of suggested segment collections.
Pipeline for extracting hierarchical video structure. The diagram shows four steps: extracting video metadata, segmenting into initial layers, recursively splitting into deeper layers, and generating thematic collections.

VideoTrail
 operates over a hierarchical video representation.
To generate this representation automatically, we use an MLLM-based pipeline that recursively segments a video and captures content at multiple levels of granularity.

4.1. 
Pipeline Overview

Given any YouTube video as input, our pipeline outputs multimodal, hierarchical segment data and a list of suggested collections (
Fig. 2
).
Data extraction is fully automated and consists of four key steps.
Thresholds (e.g., max segments per layer) were tuned to balance coverage and skimmability, but can be adjusted.

Step 1: Determine overall video characteristics.

First, we extract high-level metadata, including the input video’s primary 
modality
 (visual or dialogue)
1
1

1

              
              
              
            We currently adopt a binary notion of modality based on our formative findings.
, determined by which channel plays a more central role in conveying content.
We use this to prioritize words or visuals during analysis, and later guide how information is presented to users (
D3
).
The model then identifies 2-4 
semantic dimensions
 that describe how the video varies (e.g., “actionability” in 
Fig. 1
).
Each dimension is defined by endpoints (e.g., “theoretical” to “highly actionable”) and mapped to a color scale, facilitating comparison along these axes (
D1
).
To support skimming, we generate a 1-2 sentence 
summary
 and 3-5 
keywords
.
Keywords cover aspects such as 
visual
, 
dialogue
, 
sound
, 
mood
, or 
theme
 
(
Jorge and Chambel, 2013
; 
Wingstedt et al., 2010
)
, and each comes with a short description of how it relates to the video.

Step 2: Segment first layer.

The video is partitioned into timestamped segments to create an initial semantic outline.
Each segment returns multimodal data: a 
title
, 
keyframe
, 
summary
, 1-3 
keywords
, and 
ratings
 for the specified dimensions (range: [0,1] or [-1,1] for diverging traits like sentiment).
Keyframes are selected based on relevance to the title and summary.
To improve skimmability, the LLM crops busy keyframes (e.g., lots of text or complex elements) to focus on salient regions.
We validate segments by merging them if too many are returned (
7) and adjusting highly uneven boundaries (i.e., one segment is 
2.5x longer than the others).
Finally, we select 2-3 
highlights
 to enable quick access to key moments (
D1
).

Step 3: Split segments recursively to form deeper layers.

We repeat this process to split segments into subsegments, forming a multi-level hierarchy (
D2
).
Splitting is context-aware: subsegments inherit their parent’s context while adding finer detail.
Each parent segment can have up to 5 children and 1-2 highlights.
To avoid over-fragmentation, we stop when the max depth is reached (4 layers), segments become too short (
10 seconds per YouTube chapter guidelines 
(
Help, 2026
)
) or the model determines that further splitting does not add meaningful structure (e.g., intro or end screen) 
(
Ventura et al., 2025
; 
Pu et al., 2025
)
.

Step 4: Generate suggested collections.

Using the resulting hierarchy, we generate 3-5 
suggested collections
; each groups semantically related clips that represent a cross-cutting theme (e.g., “Culinary Tour & Highlights” in a travel vlog).
Collection segments do not need to be contiguous, offering another nonlinear way to explore the video (
D1
).
The final video data is outputted as JSON.

4.2. 
Implementation & Performance

The pipeline, implemented in Python, uses 
gemini-3-flash-previ ew
 
(
DeepMind, 2025
)
 to extract most video data, passing YouTube URLs directly as input.
We extract keyframes with yt-dlp 
(
yt-dlp, 2026
)
 and use 
claude-haiku -4-5
 
(
Anthropic, 2026
)
 to identify salient cropping regions.
Most pipeline steps are parallelized to reduce latency (e.g., segment splitting, keyframe extraction, collection generation), and prompts are included as Supplementary Materials.

With this pipeline, a 30-minute video (e.g., 
DWS News
 interview
) takes 
7 minutes to process and generates 
2
00 segments across four layers; a 3-hour video (e.g., 
Insider Food
 documentary
) takes 
15.5 minutes and yields 
600 segments.

While this latency limits lightweight, on-demand use, our focus is prototyping useful downstream interactions over the resulting structure (assessed in 
Sec. 7
) rather than optimizing preprocessing speed.
We also expect data generation to become faster as multimodal inference improves.
For reference, we evaluated our pipeline against a recent baseline for video chapter and summary generation and found comparable performance; full methods and results are reported in 
App. D
.

5. 
VideoTrail
 Design

VideoTrail
 is an AI-powered browser extension that supports video foraging through enacting our design goals (
Fig. 3
).
Leveraging structured outputs from our pipeline, 
VideoTrail
 decomposes videos into semantic segments and connects them into a hierarchical visualization.
Users navigate the hierarchy by following the information scent of segments, which adapts based on video content.
While individual layers may resemble YouTube chapters 
(
Help, 2026
)
, 
VideoTrail
 extends this paradigm by making segments interactive and enabling exploration across multiple levels of detail 
(
Min et al., 2025
; 
Van Daele et al., 2024
; 
Li et al., 2021
)
.

5.1. 
Interface Overview

VideoTrail
 integrates three main views.
The left 
YouTube Panel
 preserves the native video player, description, and comments (
Fig. 3
A).

On the right, we augment YouTube’s UI with a custom interface.
At the top, we display the video’s 
Summary & Collections
.
The 
summary
 tab shows the global summary and keywords (
Fig. 3
B), while

collections
 offers a place to save and organize segments of interest — similar to bookmarking locations on a map 
(
iPhone User Guide, 2026
; 
Support, 2026
)
.

Below, the 
Video Map
 displays our main hierarchical visualization, which the user can expand, search, or customize (
Fig. 3
C).
As the video plays, the active path in the hierarchy is highlighted.
Conversely, selecting a segment in the Video Map updates playback to the corresponding moment, enabling rapid, context-aware navigation.

5.2. 
Segment Nodes

The base unit of our visualization is the 
segment node
, which represents a contiguous segment (patch) in the video.
Each node consists of a 
title
, 
colorbar
, 
keyframe
, 
summary
, and segment-specific 
keywords
 that together shape its scent (
D1
).
The colorbar indicates the segment’s rating for the current dimension (e.g., “activity pace” in 
Fig. 3
C).
Hovering over the keyframe plays a 5-second preview, and segments can be saved via the “collect” button.

5.2.1. 
Adaptive scent

Segment nodes surface or hide information to adapt to video content and preserve skimmability (
D3
).
For 
visual-driven
 videos (e.g., travel vlog in 
Fig. 4
), 
VideoTrail
 emphasizes the keyframe (visual cue) and hides summary text, using keywords to provide quick context.
For 
dialogue-driven
 content (e.g., talking head video), we prioritize the segment summary (text cue), while only showing a partial keyframe, which likely carries less scent.
Keywords are also hidden in this case to reduce visual clutter.

Nodes support 
semantic zoom
 
(
Suh et al., 2024
; 
Suh et al., 2023
)
, where information density adjusts based on zoom level (
D2
).
As the user zooms in, all segments (regardless of video type) progressively reveal their full keyframe and summary text (
Fig. B1
).

5.3. 
From Segments to Hierarchy

Segments are organized into 
layers
, which are connected to form the video 
hierarchy
 (
D2
).
The top layer provides a high-level overview, while deeper layers expand nodes into subsegments, revealing increasingly fine-grained detail (
Fig. 1
B).
Users can click any node marked “expand” to navigate; this reveals its subsegments in the next layer.
Clicking again collapses nodes up one level.
Expansion dynamically adjusts the visualization layout to preserve segment chronology (
Fig. 3
C).
Edges along the active path are highlighted with a darker opacity to preserve context.
To focus exploration and prevent visual overload, only one node per layer can be expanded at a time.

Our formative probe explored a “full view” exposing the entire hierarchy simultaneously, but participants found it difficult to navigate for more complex videos (see 
App. A
 for more details).

5.4. 
Exploring the Hierarchy

VideoTrail
 provides support for both targeted information-finding and open-ended discovery (
D1
).

5.4.1. 
Semantic search

Users can locate specific information via 
semantic search
 (
Fig. 3
C).
Expanding the search box allows inputting a natural language query or viewing question suggestions (
Fig. 5
A).
Rather than returning an isolated textual answer, 
VideoTrail
 identifies relevant segments (
Fig. 5
B) and spotlights them in the hierarchy (
Fig. 5
C).
This enables users to interpret results in context using the video’s structure, explore nearby segments, or jump directly to the corresponding moment in the video (
D2
).
Users can navigate between results via the search box, which expands the path to the corresponding segment, or by traversing the visualization itself.

5.4.2. 
Additional exploration controls

At the top of the Video Map, users can switch the attribute encoded by colorbars via the 
visualize
 dropdown,
or add custom scales (via the “+” button) to specify new dimensions to explore 
(
Yeh et al., 2026a
)
 (
Fig. 3
C).
Users can also 
sort
 nodes based on their semantic ratings, surfacing relevant moments (e.g., most energetic segments).

For less targeted goals (e.g., “I want to discover interesting segments”), the 
highlights
 toggle reveals recommended segments at each layer of the hierarchy, forming a guided exploration path (
D2
, 
Fig. B2
).
Additionally, the 
collections
 view displays system-suggested collections that cover key video themes (
Fig. B3
).
Upon opening a collection, corresponding segments in the Video Map are highlighted — similar to search.

5.5. 
Extension Implementation

VideoTrail
 is a browser extension with a TypeScript/React frontend and Python/Flask backend.
Our visualization is built on top of React Flow 
(
Flow, 2025
)
, with custom Sankey-style SVG edges.
Video data (from our pipeline) is served from S3.
We use 
gemini-3-flash-pre view
 
(
DeepMind, 2025
)
 to power all on-the-fly AI features, i.e., search and custom colors.
Both functions take the hierarchical structure as input, along with the search query or new rating dimension.
For search, we perform a heuristic, breadth-first traversal.
At each level, we evaluate relevance and only expand relevant nodes, pruning the remaining subtrees.
Prompts are included as Supplementary Materials.

6. 
User Study

To evaluate 
VideoTrail
’s support for video foraging 
and reforaging
, we conducted a two-part user study consisting of (1) a controlled comparison with YouTube and (2) an open-ended exploration task using participant-selected videos.
Our study was guided by three research questions:

RQ1.

Does 
VideoTrail
 effectively support information seeking inside videos, and how does it compare to existing 
workflows
?

RQ2.

Which aspects of 
VideoTrail
 do users find most useful for foraging and reforaging videos?

RQ3.

How do users perceive the AI-generated video structure?

6.1. 
Study Design

The study consisted of two complementary phases.
During the 
comparison task
, participants completed standardized information finding tasks using 
VideoTrail
 and YouTube, allowing us to compare both workflows under controlled conditions.

For an ecologically valid, holistic comparison, we selected YouTube as a mature, widely used platform that all participants already used for goal-directed viewing; 
VideoTrail
 also directly augments its interface.
Participants could freely use any available YouTube feature — including the AI chat 
(
Shrivastava, 2025
)
 — to reflect realistic use.

For the 
exploration task
, participants selected their own videos and goals to explore with 
VideoTrail
, capturing interaction strategies across varied content in a more naturalistic setting.

6.2. 
Participants

We recruited 16 participants (
P1-16
), aged 18-30, from mailing lists at Harvard University.
All participants watched YouTube at least once a week, with 12 watching on a daily basis.
Their strategies for finding information in videos included scrubbing the timeline (
), jumping to timestamps in the description or comments (
), adjusting playback speed or fast forwarding (
), or simply watching the full video (
).
Four participants also leveraged YouTube’s “most replayed” visualization to find interesting moments.
None had previously used LLMs to navigate or understand video content.

6.3. 
Materials

Our study focuses on long-form videos based on formative feedback indicating this as an ideal use case (
Sec. 3
), and to ensure that participants could not just complete tasks by watching a video in full.
All videos were preprocessed and loaded into 
VideoTrail
 prior to the study session.
The full list of videos and questions used across both tasks is provided in the Supplementary Materials.

6.3.1. 
Comparison task.

We wanted to evaluate utility for videos with different primary modalities.
Thus, our corpus included two 
visual
-driven videos (travel vlog, recipe) and two 
dialogue
-driven videos (product review, advice) with similar durations (
35 minutes) and information complexity.
The advice video and travel vlog are shown in 
Fig. 1
 and 
Fig. 3
, respectively.
We included two stimuli per category to reduce confounding effects due to a particular video’s characteristics (e.g., one being inherently easier to find information in).
Following 
Yang et al. (2025)
, each participant explored one video of each category during the study.
We also ensured that participants had not previously watched their assigned videos.

For each video, participants completed four information-seeking tasks of increasing complexity:

1.

Overview:
 provide a 1-2 sentence summary of the video.

2.

Retrieval:
 locate a specific segment.

3.

Evidence:
 explain a claim made in the video.

4.

Synthesis:
 compare two concepts.

These categories were informed by prior evaluations of video understanding and navigation systems 
(
Shin et al., 2015
; 
Truong et al., 2021
; 
Chi et al., 2025
)
.
A fixed set of questions was curated for each video through iterative testing to ensure answers were discoverable within a reasonable time frame.

6.3.2. 
Exploration task.

Participants chose two long-form videos (
20 minutes) to explore: one familiar and one unfamiliar.
Selected videos averaged 41.2 minutes in duration (
min:
 20.5 minutes, 
max:
 3.2 hours), ranging from a cheese documentary and politician interview to a luxury airline review and 3D modeling software tutorial.
The most common video types included explainers about math, history, or psychology (
), video essays about current events or popular media (
), reviews about different products or experiences (
), and how-to tutorials (
).

6.4. 
Procedure

Sessions lasted one hour and took place over Zoom.
We asked participants to think aloud throughout the study, and with their consent, we collected screen/audio recordings along with system logs for further analysis.
Participants received $20 in compensation.

6.4.1. 
Pre-task interview (5 min)

We first probed participants’ strategies for finding information in YouTube videos and prior experience foraging videos with LLMs (reported above).

6.4.2. 
Comparison task (25 min)

Participants used both 
VideoTrail
 and YouTube to answer questions about two videos (one per interface).
Interface order and video assignment were counterbalanced.
Before using 
VideoTrail
, participants received a five-minute tutorial.
After each condition, they filled out a brief Likert-scale survey probing utility and ease of use.
Items were adapted from the System Usability Scale (SUS) 
(
Brooke and others, 1996
)
 and NASA-TLX 
(
Hart and Staveland, 1988
)
, along with validated criteria for evaluating video interfaces 
(
Gupta and Sharma, 2023
; 
Pu et al., 2011
; 
Airaldi et al., 2025
)
.
Each condition took 
10 minutes to complete (including the survey).

6.4.3. 
Exploration task (20 min)

Next, participants used 
VideoTrail
 to freely explore their two videos — each toward a self-selected information goal (
10 minutes each).
The short time frame simulates realistic constraints when users may need to quickly glean information from long videos.

6.4.4. 
Post-task interview (10 min)

To conclude, we conducted semi-structured interviews with participants to collect qualitative feedback about using 
VideoTrail
, including which features they found most useful.
We also asked for ideas to improve the system, and how participants perceived the AI-generated video structure.

6.5. 
Data Analysis

We adopted a mixed-methods approach to data analysis.
For the comparison task, we measured performance by analyzing response accuracies and completion times across conditions.
Responses were scored against reference answers using a predefined rubric (see Supplementary Materials).
From screen recordings and system logs, we collected a quantitative account of feature usage for both tasks (e.g., number of segments expanded, searches submitted).
For all statistical comparisons, we used linear mixed models and exploratory simple effects analyses with BH correction.
We also synthesized qualitative themes from think-aloud and interview transcripts.

8. 
Discussion & Future Work

Our findings highlight the value of making AI-extracted video structure visible and interactive to support information foraging.
We reflect on these insights, current limitations, and opportunities for future work.

8.1. 
Supporting Goal-Directed Navigation Through Hierarchical Visualization

With 
VideoTrail
, we explored how hierarchical visualization can augment timeline-based navigation for goal-directed viewing.

While bounded by study scope, our results suggest two primary benefits.
First, surfacing video structure enabled 
faster and more accurate
 foraging than 
existing workflows
 — and even AI chat (
Sec. 7.1
).
This aligns with Nielsen’s “recognition over recall” heuristic 
(
Group, 2024
)
:
by externalizing structure as a cognitive aid 
(
Scaife and Rogers, 1996
; 
Spence, 1985
; 
Yeh et al., 2026a
)
, 
VideoTrail
 allows users to visually identify relevant segments, which is easier than formulating search queries from scratch.
Second, the hierarchy supported foraging in both new and familiar videos by enabling navigation at 
multiple levels of abstraction
.
Rather than relying on repeated linear search, users could skim segments at different granularities based on their information needs 
(
Truong et al., 2021
; 
Suh et al., 2023
)
, which some even described as “fun” (
Sec. 7.2
).

Future work should further examine when this flexibility is valuable for information seeking, and when simpler representations are sufficient.

Beyond navigation, 
VideoTrail
’s hierarchies helped viewers quickly identify clickbait and misleading content, suggesting that richer structural previews can enable more informed, intentional consumption 
(
McCrosky and Geurkink, 2021
; 
Chaudhary et al., 2022
)
.
Hierarchical summaries have also been shown to improve accessibility 
(
Van Daele et al., 2024
)
, consistent with participant feedback that surfacing video structure could support uses such as language learning (
Sec. 7.3
).
More broadly, this structured visualization approach could help make other complex temporal data more 
semantically
 navigable, aiding foraging and reforaging across music 
(
Huh et al., 2026
; 
Wang et al., 2021
)
, podcasts 
(
Park et al., 2024
; 
Wang et al., 2024a
)
, and event sequences 
(
Chen et al., 2018
)
.

8.2. 
Designing Interactions Around AI-Extracted Structure

Our work demonstrates how AI-extracted structure, although imperfect (
Sec. 7.3
), can enable new interactions with unstructured video beyond conversation.

To guide future systems built around such structure (in video and beyond), we distill design principles for making these representations useful, appropriately granular, and inspectable.

Ground interactions in interpretable structure.

Users valued how 
VideoTrail
 visualized search results in the hierarchy, enabling easy verification compared to isolated text and timestamps (
Sec. 7.2
).
In contrast, AI-suggested highlights were harder to interpret, underscoring the need for inspectable outputs linked to source data 
(
Yeh et al., 2026a
; 
Amershi et al., 2019
; 
Wang et al., 2024b
)
.

Adapt structure to (changing) context and intent.

VideoTrail
 currently treats modality as static (
Sec. 4
), although the relative importance of visual and textual cues may shift within a video 
(
Wu and Qu, 2020
; 
Yu et al., 2022
)
.
Useful structure and scent may also depend on user intent and familiarity: foraging new content may benefit from stronger overview cues, while reforaging may favor deeper access to remembered moments.
Future systems could therefore adapt segmentation depth and surfaced scent as video content and user goals evolve 
(
Wu et al., 2022
; 
Narasimhan et al., 2021
; 
Pu et al., 2011
)
.

Leverage structure for parallel exploration.

Selective expansion helped participants focus attention, but they noted how parallel exploration across branches in the hierarchy could support richer video navigation and sensemaking (
Sec. 7.2
).
For instance, systems could surface related moments during playback, or expand search and collection results side by side to facilitate comparison and discovery 
(
Suh et al., 2023
; 
Zheng et al., 2026
; 
Liu et al., 2025
)
.

8.3. 
From One to Many: 
Foraging and Reforaging
 Across Video Collections

A natural next step is moving beyond single videos to support foraging and reforaging across 
collections
 of videos 
(
Dale et al., 2012
; 
Hezel et al., 2022
)
, e.g., when comparing YouTube results 
(
Pérez Ortiz et al., 2022
; 
van Houten, 2009
)
 or learning from multiple lectures 
(
Pavel et al., 2014
)
.
As the volume of online video — including increasingly on-demand, AI-generated content — continues to grow, 
users may need new ways to navigate information both within and across videos 
(
Peronikolis and Panagiotakis, 2024
; 
Van Daele et al., 2024
; 
Yeh et al., 2026b
; 
Zheng et al., 2026
)
.

Multimodal models now make large-scale video analysis increasingly feasible 
(
Wu and Qu, 2020
; 
Yang et al., 2025
)
, creating opportunities for AI-augmented foraging interfaces.
While recent work explores semantic retrieval over media collections 
(
Gesuelli, 2026
; 
Huynh et al., 2025
)
, a key challenge remains in 
visualizing and surfacing connections between videos at scale
.
From an IFT perspective, each video can be viewed as an information patch with inspectable structure and scent 
(
Pirolli and Card, 1999
; 
van Houten, 2009
)
.
Systems could use AI to extract and organize these signals across videos, extending 
VideoTrail
’s semantic dimensions and hierarchical views to compare, filter, and navigate segments at multiple granularities (
Sec. 7.2
).
For reforaging, visualizing structural context and adaptive scent could help users recognize and refind previously encountered moments.
Together, these AI-generated structures could transform video collections into interactive information spaces that support goal-directed exploration for both new and familiar content.

9. 
Conclusion

We introduced 
VideoTrail
, an AI-powered browser extension that reframes video navigation as interactive foraging over hierarchical structure.
The system visualizes videos as multi-level semantic roadmaps, surfacing multimodal, adaptive information scent to guide exploration.
In a user study (
), 
VideoTrail
 enabled more efficient and accurate information seeking compared to 
existing workflows
.

VideoTrail

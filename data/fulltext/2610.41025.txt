Abstract

When a language-model agent fails a multi-step task, can another model find the decisive mistake and fix it with a single action? We introduce ReplayRCA, a benchmark that scores such diagnoses by 
intervention
 rather than by agreement with annotations. The environment is restored to the exact state preceding the chosen step, the proposed action replaces the agent’s own, and the same agent continues on its own. The score, which we call 
causal repair
, is the continuation success rate minus that of a paired placebo that replays the agent’s original action through the same mechanism, so that success the agent would have had anyway is removed. Two tracks share this protocol: a post-hoc 
autopsy
, where the diagnoser sees the whole failed run and is told that it failed, and 
live oversight
, where a supervisor watches the run unfold and may intervene once, before an irreversible action, without knowing whether the run will fail. Tasks come from OpsWorld, a deterministic, procedurally generated back-office environment with six difficulty levels, the last of which is multimodal (QR codes, photographed forms, handwriting), so that fresh instances can be generated at will, which limits the memorisation of released items, and every restoration is verified. On 62 calibrated systematic failures, the highest-scoring of 13 frontier models (Grok 4.7) reaches a causal repair of 
 points; none of the 78 pairwise differences survives multiple-comparison correction, so the observed order is not a reliable hierarchy; in the configurations we could run, the flagship models of the two largest vendors, GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.1, come 8th and 12th (the Claude models ran below their top reasoning effort, which an ablation shows costs Claude Haiku 5.5 15.2 points), and 5 items are repaired by no model. Forcing a strong model to intervene at the last step costs it 17.1 points, so the structural shortcut that dominates annotation-based attribution benchmarks does not transfer. On 87 runs, the two best live supervisors are not separable, and one of them is a small open-weight model (DeepSeek V4.1 Flash); the supervisor that is among the best post-hoc diagnosers (GPT-6.1 Sol) is the weakest live one, with the largest gap between explaining and preventing (
 points on the same runs, 
), so post-hoc diagnostic performance can be a poor proxy for live oversight performance. The benchmark is offered as a reusable protocol rather than as a leaderboard: the environment and the evaluation code are released at 
https://github.com/jeremy1392/RCAreplay
.

1 
Introduction

Language-model agents now execute long sequences of consequential actions: they refund customers, cancel orders, edit inventories, modify code. When such a run fails, someone has to understand why, and the natural candidate is another model. A growing line of work therefore evaluates 
failure attribution
: given a failed trajectory, name the responsible agent and the decisive step 
(
Zhang et al., 2025
; 
Liu et al., 2026
; 
Zhang et al., 2026b
; 
Chen et al., 2026
)
. These benchmarks score a diagnosis by comparing it with a human or model label. Two problems follow. First, labels are themselves contestable: an annotator’s “decisive step” is a judgement, and a different but equally valid repair point is scored as wrong. Second, label-based scores are vulnerable to structural shortcuts: 
Naagarajan and Streif (2026)
 show that always naming the last step is correct 67–96% of the time on existing suites, and falls to near chance only on the subset of failures whose decisive step can be confirmed by replay.

Replay offers a different standard of truth. If the world can be restored to the state it was in before step 
 and the agent can be made to continue from a corrected action, then a diagnosis is right exactly when the correction makes the agent succeed. Several recent methods use such counterfactual re-execution to 
produce
 attributions or repairs 
(
Zhang et al., 2026a
; 
Shah, 2026
; 
Bonagiri et al., 2026
; 
Lin et al., 2026
; 
Luan et al., 2026
)
. We turn it into the 
scoring rule
 of a benchmark for the models that do the diagnosing, and we add the control that 
Luan et al. (2026)
 identify as missing from rerun-based evaluation: a paired placebo, so that the agent’s own propensity to recover on a second try is not credited to the diagnoser.

The same mechanism lets us ask a second question that attribution benchmarks cannot: does the ability to explain a failure after the fact imply the ability to 
prevent
 it? In the oversight track, a supervisor model watches the run as it happens, is shown each irreversible action before it executes, and may replace one of them. It is never told whether the run is going to fail, and runs that were going to succeed are mixed in so that false alarms are measured. This is the setting of scalable oversight and AI control 
(
Bowman et al., 2022
; 
Kenton et al., 2024
; 
Greenblatt et al., 2023
; 
Kale et al., 2025
)
, applied to honest mistakes rather than to adversarial agents, and scored by consequences.

Contributions.

1.

A scoring protocol.
 A single replacement action, applied by restoring the exact prior state, continued by the original agent, and scored against a paired placebo (§
3
). The score needs no judge model and no annotation; the replay is the ground truth.

2.

Two tracks on the same failures.
 Post-hoc autopsy and live oversight share the environment, the runs and the metric, so that the two abilities can be compared model by model (§
3.3
).

3.

OpsWorld.
 A deterministic, procedurally generated back-office environment with verified state restoration, 15 tools, three task families, six difficulty levels and a multimodal level with QR codes and handwriting (§
4
). Items are generated from a seed, so fresh instances can be produced at will (the released instances and the structure of the generator remain learnable), and the pool is harvested from the systematic failures of current agents, so it can in principle be regenerated as agents improve (a property we discuss but could not yet test, §
7
).

4.

Results.
 13 frontier models in the autopsy track and five supervisors in the oversight track, each at the highest reasoning effort that fits a 64 000-token output budget (the vendor’s maximum for all but the Anthropic models, which ran at 
high
, §
4.2
), with paired statistics (§
5
). No pairwise difference at the top of the autopsy ranking survives correction, so the observed order is not a reliable hierarchy; the flagship models do not lead in the configurations tested, a structural last-step baseline is far below free choice, a small open-weight supervisor is not separable from the lead of the oversight track, and the supervisor that is among the best post-hoc diagnosers is the weakest live one.

2 
Related work

Failure attribution benchmarks.

Who&When 
(
Zhang et al., 2025
)
 introduced step-level attribution for multi-agent failures, with reported step accuracies that remain low; Who&When Pro 
(
Liu et al., 2026
)
 and LongRCA 
(
Zhang et al., 2026b
)
 extend it to harder and longer natural traces. TraceElephant 
(
Chen et al., 2026
)
 shows that full execution traces and reproducible environments improve attribution. CatchBench 
(
Zhao et al., 2026
)
 asks when a failure can be caught. All of these score against labels; 
Naagarajan and Streif (2026)
 show how much of the resulting performance is structural. ReplayRCA keeps the task (find the decisive step) but replaces the label with an outcome.

Counterfactual replay as a method.

AgenTracer 
(
Zhang et al., 2026a
)
 uses counterfactual replay and fault injection to build training labels for a tracer model. Causal Agent Replay 
(
Shah, 2026
)
 formalises a run as a structural causal model, intervenes on one step and re-executes under the same stochastic policy, with Shapley credit for interacting steps, validated on synthetic models. CausalFlow 
(
Bonagiri et al., 2026
)
 computes causal responsibility scores by step-level intervention and generates minimal repairs that flip the outcome. REFLECT 
(
Lin et al., 2026
)
 tests a candidate error step by patching it and replaying. SymTrace 
(
Luan et al., 2026
)
 reconstructs the execution up to an anchor from logs and regenerates the downstream trajectory; on their SymFail dataset, unguided reruns repair only 6.90% of failures, which motivates distinguishing causal repair from stochastic repair. AgentDebug 
(
Zhu et al., 2025
)
 and AgentDebugX 
(
Zhu et al., 2026
)
 detect critical errors and feed corrections back to the agent. Counterfactual re-execution is therefore not new, and we do not claim it. What these works share is that replay serves 
their own method
: it validates a repair that CausalFlow generates, refines an attribution that REFLECT proposes, or measures how much SymTrace’s reconstruction recovers. ReplayRCA differs in four respects that, taken together, we have not found elsewhere: replay is the scoring rule of a benchmark for 
arbitrary
 diagnoser models, so that any model is evaluated by the same procedure; every score is relative to a paired placebo at the same step, which none of the above reports; the same failures and the same metric serve a post-hoc and a live track, so that explaining and preventing are compared model by model; and the environment is procedural and restorable with verification, so that items are regenerated rather than curated. What the score measures is also narrower and more operational than root-cause attribution: a diagnosis is credited for an 
effective intervention
, which can coincide with the earliest cause of a failure but need not (§
6
).

Oversight and intervention.

Scalable oversight asks whether weaker overseers can supervise stronger systems 
(
Bowman et al., 2022
; 
Kenton et al., 2024
; 
Burns et al., 2024
)
; AI control studies trusted monitoring of possibly adversarial agents 
(
Greenblatt et al., 2023
)
, and 
Kale et al. (2025)
 stress-test weak monitors of agents against covert misbehaviour. 
Overman and Bayati (2026)
 show that weaker overseers can constrain a stronger misaligned agent on a modified SWE-bench. StepShield 
(
Felicia et al., 2026
)
 studies 
when
 to intervene on rogue agents, BLINDSPOT 
(
Asif et al., 2026
)
 defines a response set (block, roll back, request approval, escalate) for long-horizon tool-using agents, and LoopArena 
(
Wang et al., 2026
)
 evaluates models as runtime controllers of a coding agent. Our oversight track differs in three ways: the agent is not adversarial, the supervisor acts by replacing an action rather than by blocking, and the score is the causal effect of that action measured by replay.

Evaluation integrity.

Automatic evaluations can be gamed by null outputs 
(
Zheng et al., 2024
)
 and judge models by single tokens 
(
Zhao et al., 2025
)
; JudgeBench 
(
Tan et al., 2025
)
 measures judges against objective correctness. Static item pools suffer contamination, which LiveBench 
(
White et al., 2024
)
 mitigates by refreshing questions. Agent benchmarks such as SWE-bench 
(
Jimenez et al., 2024
)
 and 
-bench 
(
Yao et al., 2024
)
 score final outcomes with executable checks; procedural science environments 
(
Jansen et al., 2024
; 
Gandhi et al., 2025
; 
Zheng et al., 2026
)
 generate tasks rather than curate them. ReplayRCA combines executable outcome checks, procedural generation and a replay-based score, and uses no model judge anywhere in the scoring path.

3.3 
The two tracks

Autopsy.

The diagnoser receives the task given to the worker, the worker’s system prompt, the list of tools, and the complete trajectory, including the images the worker opened, and is told that the run failed the acceptance check (not why). It must answer with a step index and one replacement action, as a JSON object (prompt in Appendix 
B
). An answer that is not well formed, names an unknown tool, or chooses a step outside the trajectory is scored 
; a single re-ask is issued when the reply contains no JSON object.

Live oversight.

The supervisor watches the recorded run step by step. Before every 
irreversible
 action (a refund, a cancellation, an e-mail, an inventory adjustment, or the final completion call) it is shown the run so far and the pending action, and answers 
proceed
 or 
intervene
 with one replacement action. The first intervention is applied as in Eq. (
1
), at that checkpoint; the supervisor may intervene at most once per run and is never told how the run ends. Runs that were going to succeed are mixed with runs that were going to fail, so that interventions on good runs (false alarms) are scored by their actual effect, and every intervention is charged an interruption cost of 
 points. The net score of a supervisor on a run is 
, and 
 if it never intervened.

Why a single action.

A single replacement action cannot carry a plan. Free-text arguments of the replacement action (
reason
, 
summary
) are discarded before execution and the remaining string arguments are truncated, and tool observations never echo free text back, so a diagnoser cannot communicate with the worker except through the state of the world. The score therefore measures whether the diagnoser understood 
where
 the trajectory could still be bent and 
with what information or action
, which is the content of a root-cause diagnosis.

4 
OpsWorld

OpsWorld simulates the back office of an online retailer: customers, orders, refunds, internal documents, support tickets, inventory in three warehouses, and an outbox. The world state is a dictionary, every tool is a deterministic function of state and arguments, and a task is fully determined by a task family, a seed and a difficulty level, so any instance can be regenerated and any state restored; each item stores a hash of its initial state, and evaluation refuses an item whose generator has changed. The 15 tools (Appendix 
A
) read and search records and documents, page through lists five entries at a time, and perform the five irreversible actions listed above. Two further tools, 
view_attachment
 and 
scan_code
, exist only at the multimodal level.

Task families and traps.

Three families cover the kinds of back-office work in which a single overlooked fact changes the right action. In 
late delivery
, a customer asks for the compensation they are owed; the worker must find the current policy among an outdated one, apply thresholds and percentages, deduct earlier refunds, exclude promotional orders, handle a namesake customer, read an operational memo with an innocuous title that shifts promised dates for a strike period, apply an annual cap in delivery-date order, and ignore a false claim in the ticket. In 
recall
, a product recall must be applied to every order of affected production lots, where the lot set is a range with an exception, an amendment memo adds a lot, a legacy SKU code and a bundle product hide affected orders, some orders are already fully or partly refunded, and the required action depends on the order status. In 
damage
, warehouse damage reports must be booked as inventory losses, with corrections that replace rather than add, duplicates, cartons of different sizes, warehouse aliases, transit damage booked at the destination, partial repairs and retracted reports. Levels 1–3 enable a fixed subset of these traps, level 4 samples four or five of them at random, level 5 enables all of them, and level 6 additionally moves part of the information into images: delivery slips whose order number is only in a QR code and whose true delivery date is handwritten in day/month order, packing labels whose printed lot is stale on relabelled boxes while the QR code is authoritative, and photographed damage forms whose product is identified only by the label’s QR code, sometimes with a handwritten, initialled correction of the quantity. Images are rendered deterministically from the attachment specification (rotation, noise and blur included); a standard decoder reads 97% of the rendered QR codes, and 
scan_code
 simulates a reliable scanner. Figure

Checker and oracle.

The checker compares the final state with the expected effects exactly: refunds to the cent per order, cancellations, net inventory movement per SKU and warehouse, and required e-mails (recipient and mandatory content). An oracle solver derived from the expected effects passes every generated instance, and the empty policy fails every instance; both are verified over 720 instances across the six levels before any paid run. No model judge is used anywhere.

4.1 
Building the item pool

Harvest.

Worker agents attempt generated tasks. A failure becomes a candidate item only if it is 
systematic
: the worker fails at least two of three independent fresh attempts. Episodes that ended for infrastructure reasons (API errors, truncated outputs) or by exhausting the step budget are excluded, since a single intervention cannot repair inefficiency. The workers are deliberately small models, and two of them are open-weight (DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, Qwen 3.8 Flash), so that continuations remain fast and reproducible: 66% of the final items come from open-weight workers. In all, the workers made 792 first attempts at generated tasks of levels 4–6 (the self-renewal pilot of §
5.6
 included); 124 ended in a clean failure and were re-attempted twice, a further 31 reached the step budget and were set aside (26 of them had nonetheless completed the task by then and count as successes in Appendix 
C
), 3 ended in an infrastructure error, 2 came from a fifth worker (
minimax/minimax-m3.1-flash
) that was tried and abandoned, and 78 systematic failures entered calibration together with 65 recorded successes. Worker success rates are in Appendix 
C
, over the 787 first attempts of the four workers that ran to completion.

Calibration.

Replay-based scores are only informative when the outcome of a run is stable under restart. For every candidate run we replay the worker’s own action at its first checkpoint 
 times and keep failures whose spontaneous success is at most 20% and successes whose spontaneous success is at least 80%. Of 143 candidate runs, 103 survive (62 failures, 41 successes); the mean spontaneous success of the retained failures is 3.5%. The 62 retained failures are the autopsy items; they have 66 steps on average (median 59, range 33–152), and rendered for the diagnoser an item is 14 347 tokens long on average (at most 57 655). The oversight track uses the same 62 failures together with 25 calibrated successes as control runs, 87 runs in all seen by every supervisor (93 runs with the control runs that only some supervisors saw, §
5.3
). Neither the task family nor the level reveals the outcome: the controls were drawn to match the failures’ distribution over both. The worker does: successes that pass the 80% criterion come mostly from the strongest worker (Qwen 3.8 Flash: 18 of the 25 control runs, against 0 of the failing runs), whereas every failure comes from one of the three other workers. The supervisor is never told which worker produced a run, but a worker’s style is visible in the transcript; §
5.3
 tests whether supervisors use it. A run has 17 checkpoints on average (6–38), each a separate call to the supervisor.

4.2 
Models, reasoning effort and statistics

We evaluate 13 diagnosers in the autopsy track and five supervisors in the oversight track, all through one API gateway, each at the highest reasoning effort its provider exposes that fits a shared output budget of 64 000 tokens (reasoning and answer). For Anthropic models this is 
high
: at their top setting these models fill the entire budget (Claude Sonnet 5.5 produced 93 000 reasoning tokens on one diagnosis without answering within 64 000). With a larger output budget, 
max
 is feasible for the smallest Anthropic model, and the ablation in §
5.6
 shows that it is worth 15.2 points to Claude Haiku 5.5; the Anthropic entries of the main table should therefore be read as lower bounds, and running the three larger Claude models at 
max

1

Two models (GLM 5.3, DeepSeek V4 Pro) accept text only; they receive a placeholder in place of each image and are marked as such. Every scoring statistic is paired at the item or run level: bootstrap confidence intervals over items (

random-step replay
, picks a step uniformly and replays the agent’s own action; its causal repair is zero by construction up to sampling noise and its raw success rate shows how much recovery is spontaneous. A second structural baseline,

A label would rank differently.

Had we scored agreement with a label instead of the outcome, taking as the label of each item the step most often chosen by the other models, GPT-6 Astra would rank first (it chose the consensus step on 87% of the items) and Grok 4.7 3rd; the rank correlation between that label score and causal repair across the 13 models is 0.43 (Table

Does the worker’s identity leak the outcome?

Every failing run comes from one of the three weaker workers and 18 of the 25 control runs from Qwen 3.8 Flash (§
4.1
), so a supervisor that recognised a worker’s style could in principle intervene on the weaker workers’ runs and let Qwen’s pass, and score well without reading the run. Such a policy would show up on the succeeding runs as false alarms concentrated on the weaker workers’ successes, which is why we added 6 further calibrated successes of those workers, supervised by four of the five supervisors, all but Gemini 3.8 Flash (Table

). The opposite holds: over the 59 (supervisor, run) pairs that involve a success of a weaker worker, the supervisors raise 5 false alarms (8%), against 16 (18%) over the 90 pairs that involve a Qwen success. Removing every Qwen run leaves 69 to 75 runs per supervisor, all from the three weaker workers, and on them the ordering of the five supervisors is the same as in Table

On this evidence, knowing where a run went wrong, once told that it did, is not the same skill as recognising, action by action and without that information, the moment at which one intervention still changes the outcome; the size of the gap, whose interval is wide, and whether it is specific to this model remain to be established on more runs and more models.

Self-renewal pilot.

A benchmark harvested from the failures of current agents can be re-harvested from stronger agents. We attempted this with Qwen 3.8 Flash, which succeeds far more often than DeepSeek V4.1 Flash on the same tasks (Appendix 
C
): 210 first attempts on 70 late-delivery tasks of levels 4–6, each failure re-attempted twice as in the main harvest. Only 3 failures were systematic, and none survived calibration (their spontaneous success under replay was 60%, 80%, 80%), so no item could be scored. The same procedure applied to DeepSeek V4.1 Flash on the first 20 of these tasks had yielded 21 calibrated failures. The outcome is informative in a different way than intended: with a worker that passes 92% of these tasks at first attempt, robust systematic failures become rare, and the number of episodes needed to harvest them grows faster than the worker’s success rate alone would suggest. Whether the failures of stronger workers are harder to repair therefore remains untested here.

6 
Discussion

What the score measures.

Causal repair is an interventional quantity: the change in the worker’s success caused by one action at one point, relative to what the worker would have done. It is bounded above by what one action can achieve, which is why the 5 items repaired by no model are informative: some failures combine several independent mistakes (the oracle selection among the tested models, 72.3% of continuations, describes those models, not that bound). It is bounded below by the placebo, which is why a diagnoser that always targets the final step, or always makes the worker re-read the policy, cannot score by regularity alone. The score needs no judge model, so it cannot be gamed through the judge 
(
Zheng et al., 2024
; 
Zhao et al., 2025
)
, and the items are generated from seeds, so fresh instances can replace any that leak into training data; both properties follow from the design rather than from curation.

Effective intervention is not root-cause attribution.

Causal repair credits the step and action from which one change still leads to success, which is not always the step at which the failure originated. A worker that skipped the policy at step 5 and issued the wrong refund at step 40 can be repaired at step 5 (make it read) or at step 40 (issue the right amount); both are valid answers under our score, and Table 
2
 shows that diagnosers choose both kinds. A root-cause label would accept only one. We regard this as the right standard for the questions the benchmark asks, since what matters to an operator is whether a correction works, and the position analysis (Table 
11
) shows that early and late repairs differ in how much they rely on the worker thereafter; but a study of 
why
 agents fail, as opposed to how their failures can be repaired, needs attribution labels in addition, and the two can be combined on the same runs.

Scope of the conclusions.

The results come from one simulated environment, two task families that produce systematic failures, and a pool in which 41 of the 62 items come from one worker. What transfers, in our view, is the protocol and the two findings that do not depend on the particular ranking: that a structural shortcut which dominates label-based suites fails under replay, and that live oversight and post-hoc diagnosis can rank the same models differently. Which models lead, and by how much, is a property of this pool at these reasoning settings, and the intervals of Table 
1
 say how little of the ordering is established. Confirming the findings requires a second environment with a different failure structure, workers of several strengths, and more runs in the oversight track.

Small supervisors.

The most practically relevant result is in the oversight track: a small open-weight model is not outperformed by any other supervisor tested, and prevents significantly more damage than a frontier model that is among the best post-hoc diagnosers. If this holds at scale, supervising deployed agents, which requires a call before every irreversible action, is within reach of small models, and the autopsy ranking alone would have pointed to the wrong candidates. The result also cautions against selecting supervisors by their performance on explanation tasks.

Why the benchmark should not saturate quickly.

The headline scores leave much room above them (even an oracle choice among the tested models succeeds in 72.3% of continuations), the top models are tied, and the hardest items resist every model. Beyond that, the pool is a function of the agents it is harvested from: as workers improve, their systematic failures become subtler, and the pool can be regenerated without changing the environment or the metric. Our attempt to harvest robust failures from a stronger worker found none at the same harvesting effort (§
5.6
): the mechanism is sound in principle, but the number of episodes it needs grows quickly with worker strength, and whether the surviving failures are harder to repair is an open question.

7 
Limitations

•

Sample size.
 With 62 items and 87 runs, confidence intervals are about 
 points, and the top of the autopsy ranking is not resolved: the split-half reliability of the ranking is 0.47, and separating two models 10 points apart with 80% power needs about 84 items (Appendix 
E
). The pipeline is resumable and cached, so the pool can be grown incrementally (Appendix 
G
).

•

One simulated environment.
 OpsWorld is a simulation of back-office work with three task families, and one of them (
damage
) produces almost no systematic failures with the current workers, so the pool is dominated by two families. The protocol only requires a restorable environment and an executable checker, and transfers to containers, databases or virtual machines, but this is not demonstrated here.

•

Reasoning effort.
 Anthropic models were run at 
high
 rather than at their top effort, for the reason given in §
4.2
. The ablation shows that 
max
 is worth 15.2 points to Claude Haiku 5.5, so the ranks of the four Claude models in Table 
1
 are lower bounds and the cross-vendor comparison is not at equal effort; the larger Claude models could not be run at 
max
 with the compute available for this study.

•

Worker diversity.
 A single worker dominates the pool (DeepSeek V4.1 Flash: 41; Claude Haiku 5.5: 11; GPT-6 Luna: 10 items), and all workers are small models; continuations with a frontier worker require one to two orders of magnitude more compute per intervention and were beyond this study.

•

Self-renewal untested.
 The claim that the pool can be re-harvested from stronger agents is a design property, not a measured one: our pilot with a stronger worker produced no calibrated failure at the scale we could run (§
5.6
).

•

One action, one intervention.
 Some failures need more than one correction, and the oversight track allows exactly one intervention; both are design choices that keep the score interpretable and could be relaxed.

•

Nondeterminism and drift.
 Workers are stochastic, which the 
 continuations and the placebo address but do not eliminate, and API-served models change over time; the open-weight workers can be pinned, the others cannot.

•

Prompt sensitivity.
 Each track uses one prompt (Appendix 
B
). One rewording of the autopsy prompt moved one model by 
 points, within its sampling noise (§
5.6
), but that is one model and one rewording, the oversight prompt was not varied, and the forced-last-step ablation shows that an instruction can move scores by a large margin.

8 
Conclusion

ReplayRCA scores a diagnosis of an agent failure by the only standard that does not require trusting a label or a judge: whether applying it makes the agent succeed, over and above what the agent would have done anyway. On that standard, current frontier models repair about half of calibrated systematic failures after the fact, no pairwise difference among the highest-scoring of them survives correction, which neither establishes a hierarchy nor shows equivalence, the flagship models do not lead at the reasoning settings we could run, and for one of the four models evaluated in both roles, post-hoc diagnostic performance is a poor proxy for the ability to prevent a failure in real time (
 points on the same runs, 
); whether other models show the same gap remains open. The lasting contribution, we believe, is the protocol: a diagnosis or an intervention is scored by its consequences, not by the plausibility of its explanation, and the same score serves both the post-hoc and the live setting. Distribution over the 62 items of the causal repair averaged over the 13 models: 35% of the items are below 20 points and 19% above 80.

Reliability of the ranking.

Splitting the 62 items at random into two halves and ranking the 13 models on each, the Spearman correlation between the two rankings is 0.47 on average over 2 000 splits (5th to 95th percentile 0.14 to 0.75; 0.62 after Spearman–Brown correction to the full length): the ends of the ranking are stable, its middle is not. The number of continuations is not the limiting factor. Re-scoring every intervention with random subsets of 
 (3, 2, 1) of the 
 continuations changes a model’s score by 0.8 (1.4, 2.0, 3.2) points on average and keeps a rank correlation of 0.98 (0.95, 0.90, 0.79) with the full ranking. The standard deviation of the per-item difference between two models of the top six is 33 points, so detecting a difference of 10 points with 80% power at the 5% level needs about 84 items, and a difference of 5 points about 337.

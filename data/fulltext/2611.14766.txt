Abstract

All social interactions are situated in 
time
. Previous studies have shown that humans are able to use implicit temporal cues, such as the duration of a pause, to infer others’ mental states. But how do humans use temporal cues for dynamic, fine-grained 
social coordination
? And can AI systems do the same, without “experiencing” continuous real-time as humans do? We study these questions using a cooperative card game task called 
The Mind
, which requires players to achieve a shared goal across repeated interactions but forbids explicit communication: the only available signal is the passage of time.
We evaluate 150 humans, and two classes of AI models which reflect different ways of operationalizing time in artificial systems: computer-use agents (CUAs), and standard text-prompted LLMs. We find that human behavior is best captured by a Bayesian strategy that infers a partner’s belief from their timing—and similarly for frontier models.
However, unlike humans, none of the tested models seem to efficiently adapt their behaviors to their partners’ over the course of the game. From a cognitive perspective, our findings suggest that humans perform implicit probabilistic reasoning about continuous-time signals in dynamic social settings, and that such reasoning mechanisms may emerge without real-time experience. Practically,
our study highlights new challenges for AI systems to coordinate efficiently with other agents by learning to interpret and adapt to their implicit signals in dynamic settings.

1 
Introduction

Humans draw rich social inferences through all kinds of signals, from linguistic expressions to gestures to the placement of objects.
But humans also infer complex information about other agents’ mental states
in the 
absence
 of explicit communicative signal:
that is, based on 
the passage of time
. For example, the duration of silence before an utterance in a conversation can provide cues into a speaker’s trustworthiness or cognitive state 
(
Christenfeld, 1995
; 
Kohtz and Niebuhr, 2017
; 
Ziano and Wang, 2021
, e.g.,)
, and solving a complex task in a short amount of time might suggest that the agent had prior knowledge about the task 
(
Berke et al., 2026
, e.g.,)
. These findings provide evidence that humans use temporal information to draw one-shot social inferences, but we still lack a systematic account of how humans use time as a social signal to dynamically coordinate with other participants.

It also remains unclear whether AI systems can similarly use temporal information for social coordination. As AI agents are increasingly used in real-time interactions 
(
Nakano et al., 2021
; 
Li et al., 2026
, e.g.,)
, it seems desirable for AI agents to leverage time as an information source about human users or other agents. However, while large language models (LLMs) have been evaluated in diverse social tasks 
(
Tran et al., 2025
; 
Sidji et al., 2024
; 
Zhu et al., 2025
; 
Yim et al., 2024
)
, most studies have investigated communication based on explicit signals, such as words or non-linguistic symbols. And studies of temporal reasoning typically represent time using explicit timestamps or durations 
(
Fatemi et al., 2025
; 
Gupta et al., 2023
; 
Wang and Zhao, 2024
)
, leaving underexplored how AI systems perform in settings where time unfolds continuously and must be inferred through interaction.

Moreover, investigating time-based social coordination in AI models is not just motivated on practical grounds. It is not even clear whether current AI models are the right 
kind of thing
 to be able to use time like humans do. LLMs can only iteratively receive discrete prompts, instead of existing in the continuous flow of real-time like biological organisms. Even Computer-Use Agents (CUAs), which can dynamically interact with graphical user interfaces, still perceive the environment through discrete screenshots rather than continuous visual input. It is therefore worth investigating whether such AI systems can perform complex collaborative tasks that require time-based inferences.

Building upon prior work using games to investigate cognition in humans 
(
Newell and Simon, 1972
; 
Dubey et al., 2018
; 
Allen et al., 2024
)
 and AI models 
(
Silver et al., 2016
; 
Vinyals et al., 2019
; 
Bakhtin et al., 2022
; 
Liang et al., 2025
)
,
we evaluate humans and models on a cooperative task based on a card game called 
The Mind
. In our simplified version of the game, there are two players. On level 
, each player is randomly dealt 
 cards from a deck of cards numbered 1 through 100. The players share a cooperative goal: play all dealt cards in ascending order without any communication (no talking, gesturing, winking, etc.). 
Beyond the cards in their own hands and cards that have been played, the only information players have is the passage of time.
 This form of coordination lacks any kind of explicit signaling or grounding of symbols into meanings---and yet, people successfully play the game, with minimal training. They even find it fun.

Using

•

RQ1 (Performance):
 How successfully and efficiently do humans and models perform?

•

RQ2 (Strategy):
 What cognitive strategy best characterizes human and model behavior?

•

RQ3 (Adaptation):
 Do humans and models adapt their behaviors to increase success and efficiency with their partner across repeated interactions?

•

RQ4 (Behavior Consistency):
 Do models show consistent behavior when prompted to reason about time at different granularities?

We implement five types of cognitive strategies as fixed partners, and evaluate how three types of players interact with them:
(1) humans; (2) CUAs, which operate through a real-time graphical user interface; and (3) standard LLMs, which can only reason about time through prompts.
We find that both humans’ and frontier LLMs’ behaviors are best characterized by a Bayesian strategy that reasons about time to infer the partner’s state (
Section
 
4.2
). However, although frontier LLMs can match or even outperform humans in task success (
Section
 
4.1
), humans show greater adaptation to their partners’ behavior over the course of the game (
Section
 
4.3
). LLMs’ performance is also sensitive to how temporal information is represented in the prompt (
Section
 
4.4
).

Our results suggest that humans perform implicit probabilistic reasoning about continuous-time signals in dynamic social 
coordination
 settings. Frontier LLMs may have internalized the same rational inferential strategy that humans use to coordinate under temporal uncertainty. However, this alignment does not appear to be driven by the same experience-driven, online adaptation to a specific partner that characterizes human coordination. LLMs may already outperform humans in aggregate coordination outcomes, but efficiently and consistently adapting to a partner during an interaction remains an open challenge—especially when using implicit temporal signals without linguistic grounding.

Related Work

Humans can rapidly establish shared conventions with a stranger, even when they have no prior coordination history 
(
Clark and Wilkes-Gibbs, 1986
; 
Garrod and Doherty, 1994
, e.g.,)
. Importantly, convention formation does not rely exclusively on linguistic communication. Humans can coordinate through discrete linguistic cues 
(
Steels et al., 2005
)
, discrete non-linguistic cues 
(
Misyak et al., 2016
)
, continuous non-linguistic cues 
(
Chen et al., 2025
)
, and body gestures 
(
Pezzulo et al., 2019
)
.
A growing body of work has also investigated convention formation in artificial agents from different perspectives, including coordination games 
(
Sidji et al., 2024
; 
Shih et al., 2021
)
 and pragmatic reasoning 
(
Yu et al., 2026
)
. Various benchmarks have also evaluated current agent systems’ collaboration ability in complex tasks 
(
Zhu et al., 2025
; 
Shih et al., 2021
; 
Yim et al., 2024
; 
Yee and Koh, 2026
)
. Other studies have specifically examined LLM collaboration in games under implicit or communication-constrained settings 
(
Liang et al., 2025
; 
Buscemi et al., 2026
)
.

Another relevant line of work has investigated temporal inference in humans and LLMs.
Humans infer other people’s beliefs or preferences from response or decision time 
(
Gates et al., 2021
; 
Bavard et al., 2024
; 
Konovalov and Krajbich, 2023
)
. Recently, 
Berke et al. (2026)
 showed that people can infer how long another agent spent distracted purely from the duration of a pause, and that this inference is well captured by a Bayesian model of computation.
Temporal reasoning has also received increasing attention in LLMs, with a growing number of benchmarks designed to assess models’ ability to reason about temporal information 
(
Fatemi et al., 2025
; 
Islakoglu and Kalo, 2025b
; 
Gupta et al., 2023
; 
Wang and Zhao, 2024
; 
Chu et al., 2024
; 
Fatemi et al., 2025
; 
Islakoglu and Kalo, 2025a
; 
Wei et al., 2026
, e.g.,)
. More recently, 
Sehgal et al. (2026)
 show that LLMs exhibit fragile adaptation to wall-clock time pressure in negotiating settings, while 
Wang et al. (2025)
 find that LLMs can associate token counts with the passage of continuous time. However, these evaluations primarily evaluate whether LLMs can reason about explicitly specified and static temporal information, leaving how LLMs represent and track the passage of time during dynamic interaction underexplored.

3 
Methods

3.1 
Overview of Experiments

Our task is based on a cooperative card game called 
The Mind
 (see 
Figure
 
1
). There are two players in the game, and a deck of cards numbered 1 through 100. The game proceeds across ten levels. On level 1, each player is randomly dealt 1 card from the deck; on level 2, each player is randomly dealt 2 cards from the new deck; and so on. On each level, the players share a cooperative goal: play all dealt cards in ascending order (lowest to highest). 
During the game, players know their own cards, the cards that have already been played (and hence how many cards their partner still holds), and the time elapsed since the last play, but they never see their partner’s cards. Accordingly, in our game, by the partner’s 
mental state
, we mean their private knowledge of their own cards, i.e., the value of their lowest card.
 Critically, there are no turns, and players cannot communicate with each other. Players simply play a card when they feel that it is the next lowest. If a player plays a card that is higher than the lowest card held by another player, this counts as an error, and all the lower card(s) are automatically played.

We evaluate three types of players on the task: humans, CUAs, and LLMs. In each experiment, we pair the agent of interest against artificial partners that implement different kinds of cognitive strategies. By manipulating the partner strategies, we can perform controlled analyses on what kind of partners leads to the best cooperation with each agent. 
Note that in our experiments, we did not pair humans with humans for two main reasons. First, identifying a player’s strategy and measuring adaptation requires the partner’s strategy to be known. When both players’ strategies are unknown and co-evolving, it becomes difficult to identify which player is adapting to whom, and the strategy-fitting analysis becomes much noisier. Second, a shared set of partners makes humans, LLMs and CUAs comparable with each other in their adaption and fitting strategies.

We describe the rule-based partner strategies in more detail in 
Section
 
3.2
, and then describe the methods of each experiment in 
Section
 
3.3
.

3.2 
Rule-based Partners

We pair each agent (humans, CUAs, and LLMs) with five kinds of partners implementing different cognitive strategies: Linear +/-Calibration, Bayesian +/-Calibration, and Random. These strategies differ along two separate dimensions: first, the way that time is used (Linear, Bayesian, or Random), and second, whether play speed is adjusted based on their partner’s hesitation time (+/-Calibration). The Linear strategy is motivated by the evidence that adults represent numbers linearly 
(
Siegler and Opfer, 2003
)
, making a linear mapping between the card value and waiting time a plausible simple default. The Bayesian strategy is motivated by prior work that show human temporal reasoning or perception are well described by Bayesian models 
(
Berke et al., 2026
; 
Jazayeri and Shadlen, 2010
, e.g.,)
. The Random strategy is taken as a baseline.

3.3 
Experimental Methods

3.3.1 
Experiment 1: Humans vs. Rule-based partners

We randomly generated 10 game seeds, each defining 10 levels with one pair of hands per level. Each seed was evaluated under 5 different partner conditions: Bayesian +/-Calibration, Linear +/-Calibration, and Random, resulting in 50 seed-by-partner combinations. For all Bayesian and Linear players, we set 
 to 0.5, 
 to 2, and 
 to 0.85. For players with calibration enabled, we set 
 to 0.3. These parameter values were selected based on the results and feedback from the pilot study.

For each seed-by-partner combination, we recruited 3 human participants through Prolific, resulting in 150 total participants. Participants played the game with the rule-based partners through a web-based game interface. Before playing, participants provided informed consent and received detailed instructions on the game rules and the layout of the game interface. They were then required to answer three comprehension questions based on the instructions before proceeding to the game. To encourage efficient cooperation, we provided a bonus up to $2 based on the game duration and success rate apart from the base payment. Experiment details can be found in Appendix 
C
.

3.3.2 
Experiment 2: Computer-use agents (CUAs) vs. Rule-based partners

CUAs are built on large vision-language models. They take multimodal content as input and can use computer tools to interact with and operate GUIs in real-time, making them a natural choice for investigating whether AI agents can use temporal information during social coordination. CUAs interact with the game under the same experimental conditions and through the same interface used by human participants, including instructions and comprehension questions. Therefore, their interaction time is measured using wall-clock time, as it is for human participants.

4 
Results

We now address the four research questions laid out in the Introduction. Note that, unlike humans and CUAs, text-based LLMs do not experience wall-clock time and are prompted at simulated time steps. Comparisons of absolute timing between LLMs and the other players in RQ1 should therefore be interpreted with caution. The remaining research questions are not affected by this difference, as they rely on comparisons 
within

9

In particular, it seems that models may make decisions based on how many times they are prompted to wait, rather than

5 
Discussion & Conclusion

Social interactions in the real world unfold in 
time
. Whether and how different agents use time as a signal is therefore central to understanding social coordination for both humans and AI. In this work, we investigated how humans and AI systems reason about the passage of time, extending prior work on one-shot inferences to a collaborative task involving repeated interactions. We found that humans perform the best with partners that perform Bayesian reasoning about time, and humans themselves also seem to adopt Bayesian reasoning strategies instead of simply mapping between time units and card units. Moreover, humans show evidence of adaptation over the course of the game when playing with Bayesian partners. Taken together, these results suggest that humans make rational use of temporal signals in dynamic interactions, and expect social partners to do the same.

Turning to the AI models, we found that some frontier models can achieve human-level performance and, like humans, show behaviors that are best captured by Bayesian reasoning strategies. However, unlike humans, the models do not seem to adapt to their partner’s behavior as the game progresses. Some actually show worse success rates over the course of a game. The text-based LLMs are also sensitive to time granularity presented in the prompts, and show similar timing patterns whether or not time units are mentioned in the prompt. These findings suggest that while AI models might adopt human-like strategies for temporal coordination, the process by which they arrive at this behavior may differ fundamentally from how humans learn to coordinate with a partner.

We also find that only prompt-based LLMs achieve human-comparable efficiency in the task, even though CUAs act in real-time like humans do. This pattern cannot be explained simply by implementation or reasoning latency, since CUAs do show hesitation times comparable to humans when playing against certain kinds of partners. This observation may instead reflect a difficulty current CUAs face in balancing speed and accuracy as an interaction unfolds, trading longer waiting times for higher accuracy.

To our knowledge, our study is the first to systematically study how humans and AI models use time—and no other communication—to achieve complex cooperative goals.
More broadly, our work suggests new ways to investigate the flexibility and efficiency of social cognition in humans and artificial systems when coordination relies on implicit signals.

6 
Limitations & Future Work

We note a few limitations regarding our study. First, 
as mentioned in 
Section
 
3.3.1
, we focused on players interacting with rule-based partners, rather than directly pairing “live” players with each other 
(with the exception of LLMs paired with LLMs)
. 
This choice enables a finer-grained analysis of strategies used by the players and comparison across different types of players.
 While this design gave us greater control for addressing our research questions, it does not tell us what kinds of strategies would organically arise through player-player interactions. 
Investigating how humans would naturally play the game with each other is an exciting direction for future work.

Second, the strategies we use to model the players’ behavior are necessarily simplified. Players may mix strategies, change strategies across time, and so on. 
Other factors, such as the card value or player’s personal sense of timing, can also influence how player plays the card. 
Additionally, the Bayesian strategy we used in our experiment represents a 
simple
 rational approach to inferring the state of the game, but a 
more sophisticated
 social strategy might involve recursive reasoning about the other agent’s mental state
s 
(
Frank and Goodman, 2012
; 
Baker et al., 2017
, e.g.,)
. We leave a detailed treatment of these more complex strategies for future work.

Third, we note a key difference in the way the task is realized for humans and text-prompted LLMs: humans experience continuous wall-clock time, whereas for LLMs we approximate continuous-time interaction by explicitly specifying waiting durations in prompts. While this distinction is motivated by our broader question—how a non-time-embodied system can perform the task—we note that this complicates direct comparisons between human and LLM playing times. A more direct comparison would test humans in a text-based version of the task
, although this differs drastically from how humans interact in natural settings.

Finally, our experiments use a constrained task setting 
where temporal signals are fully isolated from other forms of communication
. 
This task is ideal for addressing our research questions because it tests the limits of humans’ and models’ ability to reason about time: there is no other channel for information exchange to complete the task. It remains an open question how our findings would extend to settings where
 temporal signals interact with 
language and other communicative
 cues.

All participants were native English speakers residing in the United States. To ensure data quality, we restricted participation to Prolific users with an approval rate above 95%. To guarantee the data collection quality, we used hesitation time on standalone cards (i.e., the cards played at the end of each level after the partner has finished playing) as an attention check, and filtered out participants whose hesitation time exceeded 1 minute (1 participant was excluded on this basis). Prompt of game rule

You and your partner are playing a cooperative card game. This game is similar to a game called The Mind. However, our rules are slightly different, so please read the instructions very carefully.

You and your partner play together as one team.
At each round (called a level), you and your partner are each dealt a new hand from a fresh deck of cards numbered 1 to 100. Cards from previous levels do not carry over or affect the cards dealt in the new level. You receive one card each in level 1, two cards each in level 2, and so on.

Your shared goal is to arrange your and your partner’s cards in ascending order, from lowest to highest. You cannot see your partner’s cards. You only know how many cards they have left and what cards have been played.

How it works:
1. Each level deals you a new hand. In level N, you and your partner each hold N cards.
2. You can only play your lowest card at any time. When you believe it is lower than every card your partner still holds, play it. There are no turns — whenever you sense your lowest card should go next, you play it.
3. If you play a card while your partner still holds a lower one, that is an error — you played too early. When this happens the game pauses automatically, and every card lower than the one you just played is revealed and removed from your partner’s hand.
4. Clear all cards to finish the level and move on. There are 10 levels in total.

The performance of the game is equally based on **how fast you finish** and **how few errors you make**.
In other words, you should play QUICKLY and make as FEW errors as possible.

Abstract.

RAG
 (
RAG
) is a standard approach to enhance the reliability of  
LLM
 (
LLM
) outputs by retrieving documents relevant to a query and supplying them in the 
LLM
 prompt.
Today’s 
RAG
 systems collect, index, and serve these documents from centralized stores. In practice, however, documents are owned by organizations, devices, and individuals who cannot or will not surrender custody of them to a central party, 
e
.
g
., because of privacy, copyright or regulatory compliance.
Existing modern decentralized retrieval techniques either reintroduce a coordinating authority, or rely on a global data structure that requires continuous network-wide coordination (
e
.
g
., DHTs).
We present 
ZeroRAG
, a decentralized routing layer for document retrieval in 
RAG
 that performs semantic query routing across an overlay of peers without global data structures or centralized orchestration.
Documents remain with the peers that own them, and each peer keeps only lightweight local state: a bounded set of neighbor advertisements and a cache of recently routed queries.
Using these, 
ZeroRAG
 steers each query through a best-first walk that progressively rewires the overlay toward semantically relevant neighbors, so recall improves over time as the network self-organizes.
We evaluate 
ZeroRAG
 using three real-world workloads and two decentralized baselines.
We find that 
ZeroRAG
 outperforms baselines in terms of recall and required network messages, approaches the recall and 
LLM
 answer accuracy of a centralized document store, self-heals after catastrophic peer failures, and scales to 
 peers.

Keywords: 
Decentralized retrieval, Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), Approximate nearest neighbor search

1. 
Introduction

\Acfp

LLM have transformed natural language processing because of their strong capabilities across tasks such as text generation, translation, and summarization 
(
Vaswani et al., 2017
; 
Brown et al., 2020
)
.
One of the most popular applications of 
LLM
 is question answering 
(
Zhuang et al., 2023
)
.
In this domain, a fundamental challenge is to ensure the reliability of 
LLM
-generated answers.
However, 
\Acp
LLM are prone to hallucinations where they confidently generate false or misleading information 
(
Alansari and Luqman, 2025
)
.
This makes it difficult to trust the outputs of 
LLM
 without double-checking factuality against other sources of truth 
(
Lee et al., 2024
)
.
These reliability concerns are particularly problematic in high-stakes settings, such as healthcare, finance, or law, where inconsistent or fabricated answers can have serious real-world consequences 
(
Ji et al., 2023
)
.

RAG
 is a popular approach to improving 
LLM
 reliability without retraining or fine-tuning the model 
(
Lewis et al., 2020
; 
Fan et al., 2024
)
.
Rather than relying solely on parametric knowledge embedded in the model itself, 
RAG
 first retrieves relevant documents from an external source based on the user’s query, then includes them in the prompt before generating a response.
Both queries and documents are typically represented as high-dimensional embedding vectors capturing semantic meaning, and retrieval involves finding the embeddings nearest to the query, a process known as  
NNS
 (
NNS
) 
(
Nene and Nayar, 1997
)
.
This allows the 
LLM
 to ground its answers in up-to-date or domain-specific information, improving factual accuracy and consistency.

Existing 
RAG
 systems typically assume that documents are collected, indexed, and served from a centralized store under unified administrative control 
(
Lewis et al., 2020
; 
Fan et al., 2024
; 
Zhao et al., 2026
)
.
While this simplifies the 
RAG
 pipeline and results in low latency for document retrieval, it is not always a viable or desirable architecture.
Data may be inherently distributed across organizations, devices, or individuals who cannot or will not surrender custody of their documents to a central party, whether for reasons of privacy, regulatory compliance, data ownership, or operational boundaries 
(
Guerraoui et al., 2025
; 
Kuo et al., 2025
)
.
A hospital network, for instance, cannot pool patient records into a shared index without additional access control 
(
U.S. Department of Health & Human Services, 1996
; 
Rieke et al., 2020
)
.
Similarly, a federation of research institutions may each hold proprietary datasets they are unwilling to freely share with other parties.
Beyond ownership concerns, centralizing large, heterogeneous, and continuously evolving document collections introduces infrastructure costs and creates a single point of failure 
(
Gilbert and Lynch, 2002
; 
Xu et al., 2025b
; 
Xu et al., 2025c
)
.
Despite these limitations, no general-purpose decentralized retrieval infrastructure exists for 
RAG
: existing systems either assume centralized control, or distribute retrieval only within tightly managed federated or cloud settings that still require some coordinating authority.

We present 
ZeroRAG
, a decentralized routing layer that enables document retrieval by routing queries across a decentralized overlay, without requiring global data structures or centralized orchestration.
Documents remain with the peers that own them and peers themselves are in control whether they want to share documents with the party requesting them.
Retrieval with 
ZeroRAG
 only uses local coordination.
Each peer in ZeroRAG maintains only lightweight local state: compact summaries of the content exposed by neighboring peers and a bounded cache of previously-routed queries, and uses these to guide incoming queries to peers likely to hold documents relevant to that query. This is a key departure from prior decentralized nearest-neighbor systems, which route based only on what peers 
hold
: 
ZeroRAG
 additionally exploits what peers have recently 
asked
, referring query issuers toward peers whose recent query history is semantically aligned, not only their local inventory. For example, REMINDIN’ 
(
Tempich et al., 2004
)
 only learns from past responses and INGA 
(
Löser et al., 2005
)
 routes toward previous query issuers using explicit query topics.
This design leaves owners in control of which documents they share, while peers route queries using document and query embeddings and peer referrals.

The key technical challenge 
ZeroRAG
 solves is effective routing and overlay management without global knowledge or coordination.
Rather than consulting a centralized directory or broadcasting queries to all peers, a query in 
ZeroRAG
 involves a best-first walk over the overlay that adapts as queries flow through the network while also progressively rewiring peer connections toward semantically relevant neighbors.
The result is a retrieval layer for 
RAG
 whose recall improves over time as the overlay organizes, scales gracefully with the number of peers and documents in terms of communication and computation overhead, and remains resilient to peer churn, without requiring network-wide maintenance.

We implement 
ZeroRAG
 and evaluate its efficiency and effectiveness for decentralized document retrieval in 
RAG
 pipelines, using three real-world workloads (
AOL-IA
 
(
Pass et al., 2006
; 
MacAvaney et al., 2022
)
, 
Amazon Electronics
 
(
Ai et al., 2017
)
, and 
PubMedQA
 
(
Jin et al., 2019
)
) and two decentralized baselines (
Semantica
 
(
Neague et al., 2025
)
 and 
DHT-LSH
).
Our experimental evaluation demonstrates that 
ZeroRAG
 consistently outperforms other decentralized baselines while requiring no globally maintained structure.
On the 
AOL-IA
 workload, 
ZeroRAG
 achieves this at one tenth of the message overhead of the strongest baseline (
DHT-LSH
).

ZeroRAG
 approaches the recall and end-to-end 
RAG
 answer accuracy of a centralized document store, without any global index, and 99% of queries locate the best supporting document in at most 13 peer contacts.
Beyond static performance, our ablation studies show that 
ZeroRAG
 self-heals after catastrophic failure in which 50% of all peers crash simultaneously, quickly adapts as new topics are introduced, and scales to networks of up to one hundred thousand peers and documents.
We make the following contributions:

•

We introduce 
ZeroRAG
, a decentralized routing layer for 
RAG
 that enables document retrieval by routing queries across a decentralized overlay without labeled data, shared data structures, or centralized orchestration (
Section
 
3
).

•

We evaluate
three real-world datasets, comparing against centralized and decentralized baselines on recall, 
RAG
 answer accuracy, message efficiency, resilience to peer churn, and topic introduction (
Section
 
4
).
We show
that 
ZeroRAG
 matches or exceeds the performance of decentralized alternatives while scaling gracefully to large peer populations.

2. 
Background and Problem Description

Our aim
is to construct a decentralized document retrieval layer for 
RAG
 pipelines.
We first describe the standard 
RAG
 workflow and then discuss the technical challenges that this work addresses.

2.1. 
Centralized 
RAG
 Workflows

RAG
 (
RAG
) is a standard and popular approach to improve the factual reliability of 
LLM
 outputs by supplying external context relevant to the user query directly in the prompt 
(
Lewis et al., 2020
)
.
Whereas conventional language models depend entirely on parametric knowledge encoded during pre-training, 
RAG
 consults an external data source at inference time, yielding more accurate, up-to-date responses and reducing hallucinations without requiring costly retraining or finetuning.
We illustrate the 
RAG
 workflow in 
Figure
 
1
, using an example where a user asks a medical question, and explain the individual operations next.

The pipeline operates in two phases.
In the offline phase, documents, videos, or other source material are preprocessed: each item is divided into chunks, and every chunk is encoded into a high-dimensional vector by an embedding model 
(
Reimers and Gurevych, 2019
)
 
➊
, with the resulting embeddings stored in a vector database 
➋
.
For presentation clarity, we will refer to chunks as documents throughout this work.
In the online phase, a user query 
➌
 is embedded and forwarded to the retriever 
➍
, which locates the most semantically similar stored vectors 
➎
 and fetches the corresponding documents from the vector database 
➏
.
The original query and the retrieved context are then jointly passed to the 
LLM
, which produces a response grounded in that evidence 
➐
, yielding a more accurate and context-aware answer than generation without retrieval.

The vector database is the central component enabling retrieval. Rather than matching exact keywords, it uses
similarity search over high-dimensional embedding spaces.
Prior to insertion, each document is encoded by a pre-trained  
ML
 (
ML
) model,
typically a sentence transformer 
(
Reimers and Gurevych, 2019
)
.
Encodings
allow the database to capture semantic relationships between queries and documents, supporting
retrieval beyond what keyword-based indices permit 
(
Ma et al., 2026
)
.
Incoming queries are
embedded in the same space, and the database returns the nearest neighbors using
vector distance.
For tractability on
large corpora,  
ANN
 (
ANN
) indexing techniques are
used
to accelerate this search 
(
Li et al., 2020
)
.

2.2. 
Data Ownership and Availability

Our objective is to build a decentralized mechanism for the retrieval of relevant documents.
A vast body of works exists surrounding peer-to-peer technologies and information retrieval 
(
Risson and Moors, 2007
)
.
Our work challenges two
commonplace
assumptions
of these
technologies and 
RAG
: (1) that data can be freely ingested regardless of copyright or access control, and (2) that data must be globally available.

Copyright and access control.

Both peer-to-peer retrieval systems and 
RAG
 architectures typically assume that information is generally available; 
RAG
 systems in particular oftentimes expect to freely ingest any corpus.
We argue that this assumption will collapse under scrutiny when it comes to the intended access control or copyright law 
(
Rosati, 2025
)
.

\Acp
LLM have been shown to be trained on scraped websites, whereas their terms of service prohibit this, and some even on pirated material, completely circumventing access control 
(
Maini et al., 2024
)
.
There have also been various court cases, that were lost by the companies behind the technology, where 
ML
 algorithms generated copyrighted material verbatim 
(
Amjad, 2026
)
.
Strict access control is therefore a hard requirement in 
RAG
 systems, particularly in domains where data sovereignty is key, like healthcare or finance.
By adhering to purely decentralized interactions, 
ZeroRAG
 is compatible with existing access control mechanisms and peers can refuse or allow other peers to access their information at their discretion.

Global data availability.

Major decentralized systems, 
e
.
g
., 
Bitcoin
 
(
Nakamoto, 2008
)
, 
BitTorrent
 
(
Pouwelse et al., 2005
)
, and 
IPFS
 
(
Daniel and Tschorsch, 2022
)
, are built around the assumption that data should be available everywhere, and they solve for this by replicating data across nodes.
As previously discussed, replication is not always legal or permissible: a hospital, for instance, cannot freely copy patient records to arbitrary peers. Data has to stay with the peer that owns it
and peers make no guarantees about availability or access.
Rather than solving the replication problem, 
ZeroRAG
 routes
queries to find data where it already resides, using only local queries and data set descriptions, without replicas
of the underlying documents in the network.
This comes at a cost: because 
ZeroRAG
 maintains no global index, it cannot offer the routing-time guarantees that a system like 
Kademlia
 
(
Maymounkov and Mazières, 2002
)
 provides, and retrieval is therefore best-effort by design.

2.3. 
Towards Decentralized 
RAG

Our routing protocol should, given a user query, locate non-local owners of target or relevant documents to that query under bounded coordination and without centralized control.
Forwarding queries to retrieve documents is a promising approach to decentralizing 
RAG
 workflows.
This allows peers to perform access control on three levels: identity-based (or attribute-based) access control, query-based access control, and view-based (or purpose-based, or context-aware) access control.
Our innovation therefore is in the 
Retriever
 component in the 
RAG
 workflow (step 
➍
 in 
Figure
 
1
), which still receives results from the local vector database, but also combines these with results from (remote) peers in an overlay network.
This introduces two new challenges: (1) 
ZeroRAG
 must quickly find and query peers in the network that hold relevant documents to a given query, and (2) 
ZeroRAG
 must adapt to changes in the available documents over time, 
e
.
g
., due to peers joining and leaving or updating their local stores, causing semantic drift
when
the topology becomes misaligned with the current distribution of documents and queries.

Challenge 1: locality-aware peer sampling.

Simply querying all peers in a decentralized overlay ensures
maximum recall in terms of document retrieval, but cannot work on Internet-scale networks
or data volumes.
Instead, each peer should maintain connections to a small set of semantically proximate peers, such that relevant documents can be retrieved from currently connected neighbors alone, without sacrificing recall.
This is the goal of 
locality
 optimization: steering peer connections toward neighbors whose document collections are likely to satisfy incoming queries 
(
Voulgaris et al., 2004
)
.
For example, in a network of hospitals with different specializations, two pediatric hospitals are more likely to find similar cases in each other’s documents than in those of hospitals with unrelated specializations. However, in semantically homogeneous settings, such as a network of 
only
 pediatric hospitals, all peers hold similar documents, so any neighbor is as good as any other and a locality-aware overlay brings no benefit over random connections. Therefore, we assume a heterogeneous setting.
However, locality optimization cannot be treated as a one-time configuration: peers may leave the network, and the documents they hold may change over time, shifting what constitutes a relevant neighborhood.
The first challenge addressed by 
ZeroRAG
 is 
maintaining temporally adaptive peer connections that remain semantically relevant
 as the network evolves.

Challenge 2: connection reconfiguration.

Existing decentralized retrieval systems that achieve efficient lookup typically do so by maintaining a globally shared index structure, such as a semantic trie 
(
Neague et al., 2025
)
 or a  
DHT
 (
DHT
) 
(
Maymounkov and Mazières, 2002
)
.
These structures require continuous network-wide reorganization as peers join and leave and as document collections evolve.
In our setting, where coordination must remain strictly local, maintaining such a global structure is not an option.
Thus, the second challenge that 
ZeroRAG
 takes on is to 
reconfigure neighborhood connections directly
 as queries and documents evolve.
Our approach is comparable to how  
BGP
 (
BGP
) routes packets without a global directory: each router maintains only local routing tables that are updated incrementally as traffic flows through the network, and efficient paths emerge from these local updates alone.

3.1. 
System Model

Our system model focuses on query routing; we empirically evaluate the accuracy of the downstream 
RAG
 answer, but do not model the quality of the citation or the reduction of hallucinations.

Network and communication.
 
ZeroRAG
 coordinates decentralized document retrieval by maintaining a permissioned decentralized overlay network and reconfiguring connections between peers when queries are issued.
The incoming and outgoing connections by each peer form a directed graph.
Peers joining the 
ZeroRAG
 network can bootstrap into the network by connecting (outgoing) to several random peers, which can be obtained by using existing peer sampling services 
(
Jelasity et al., 2007
; 
Guerraoui et al., 2024
)
.
Threats targeted at the network level, like Sybil 
(
Douceur, 2002
)
 and Eclipse 
(
Heilman et al., 2015
)
 attacks, are beyond the scope of this paper, but we note that peer sampling mechanisms can overcome these after a cold-boot period of several minutes 
(
Stokkink et al., 2023
)
.

ZeroRAG
 only uses primitive request-response messages (presented shortly), does not maintain shared data structures, or require synchronization steps between peers.
Therefore, messages may be asynchronous or drop.
Likewise, peer connections can drop at any time, 
e
.
g
., when a peer goes offline or crashes.
We assume the peer sampling mechanism can detect
dropped connections
and that this
can be implemented with minimal overhead using a ping-pong scheme, message-reception acknowledgments and timeouts.
Beyond this, we do not investigate Byzantine behavior of peers, like attempts to bias others’ search results or black hole attacks 
(
Abdelhamid et al., 2023
)
.

Documents and embeddings.

In 
ZeroRAG
, peers exchange embeddings of documents and queries.
The embeddings are used for indexing and network reconfiguration.
The transfer of the documents corresponding to those embeddings is outside 
ZeroRAG
.
Exchanged embeddings need to be stored by peers and each peer operates a local vector database for this.
We assume that peers use the same embedding model 
, so embeddings produced by different peers remain comparable.
As embeddings are local, routing does not depend on centrally assigned labels or a global topic taxonomy, which was the common approach in the late 2000’s 
(
Loser et al., 2004
; 
Shen et al., 2006
)
.

Peers are expected to accrue documents with attribution over time, 
i
.
e
., a verifiable record of which peer a document originates from, as they exchange them after 
ZeroRAG
 matches their embeddings to queries. The attribution itself is out of the scope of 
ZeroRAG
 and can be implemented with a digital signature.
Within the scope of the paper we are interested in routing, not in the manner in which this history is built.
Therefore, we shortcut the emergent effect of collecting documents by using a 
HISTORY()
 remote procedure call, which gathers the history of accrued documents from neighbors 
in memory
.
When considering deployment of 
ZeroRAG
, this can (and should) be replaced with
a performant storage layer.

3.2. 
High-Level Description

ZeroRAG
 is built around the insight that if a peer’s neighbors hold semantically similar content, then a query is best served by greedily walking toward the most similar peer at each step.
To this end, whenever a peer 
 connects to another peer 
, 
 sends a set of 
advertisements
 to 
.
These advertisements are compact summaries, in the form of document embedding vectors, of the content 
 locally holds and wants to advertise for routing. This allows owners to retain their documents and decide whether and what to disclose, which is not the same as privacy-preserving retrieval: advertisements, query embeddings, and issuer referrals may reveal information even when documents are withheld.
Now, some peer 
 aims to obtain the top-
 documents that are relevant to a query 
.
To facilitate this search, peer 
 maintains a 
frontier
, which is a ranked list of candidate peers to visit, ordered by their estimated relevance to the query.
The best-first walk proceeds by repeatedly popping the highest-scoring peer from the frontier, sending it the query, and collecting its response: a set of relevant local document embeddings together with new 
referrals
 to that peer’s own neighbors.
These referrals are scored against the query and put back into the frontier, extending the search into previously unvisited parts of the network.
Meanwhile, peer 
 keeps track of the document embeddings that it has seen.
After a fixed number of steps, 
 will inspect the these
embeddings and compare their relevance against query 
.
Peer 

then retrieves the top-
 relevant documents from the peers that offer them.

ZeroRAG
 continuously reconnects the overlay itself as queries are issued.
After each query completes, the querier adds a directed edge toward whichever peer returned the most relevant result, evicting the oldest existing connection if the neighbor list is full.
This implicit feedback loop causes the overlay to self-organize around semantic affinity: peers that frequently serve useful results for a given topic accumulate more incoming connections, and future queries on that topic are naturally steered toward them earlier in the walk.
As the overlay network reconnects, peers are increasingly likely to quickly find the documents they need, without any global coordination or shared data structure.
In addition, each peer keeps a bounded routed-query memory: a cache of embedding vectors from queries it has recently responded to, which were issued by other peers.
When scoring an incoming query, a peer consults this memory alongside its neighbor advertisements, and can refer the querier not only toward peers with relevant documents but also toward peers that have recently asked similar questions, and are thus likely to be connected to, or themselves hold, relevant content.

4.6. 
Spreading New Concepts

Our last experiment shows
how fast 
ZeroRAG
 spreads a new topic in a network, analyzing how recall evolves for topics which appear anew, and are very sparsely available, but suddenly become popular with all peers.
To do so, we omit a single topic from the 
PubMedQA
 workload and re-introduce it at the 60% mark into the experiment.
We chose a random topic (“Peptostreptococcus”) that has 10 relevant documents, and we vary how many peers own it initially.
All peers then continuously send queries to find documents on our chosen (initially omitted) topic.
A document starts appearing in other peers’ local data stores only once a query successfully reaches it.

Figure
 
8
 shows the rolling recall@10 for both the topic’s queries per initial owner count and the average for other topics as a gray line.
Seeding the topic at more owners lets recall recover faster once the concept reappears: with 10 or more owners the documents are immediately reachable across the network and the recall becomes 
 almost instantly, causing the lines to overlap.
With less initial owners the documents also spread less fast, with the single-owner case being the slowest, climbing back only as the documents propagate through the overlay once queries begin to reach them.

Takeaway (
Figure
 
8
).
 
ZeroRAG
 adapts to newly introduced, initially sparse topics through local interactions alone, with its speed governed by how widely the new content is initially held.

5. 
Related Work

We discuss works on

RAG
 and nearest-neighbor search, federated and distributed 
RAG
 that scale retrieval without removing a coordinating authority, and decentralized peer-to-peer retrieval and routing, which is closest to our setting.

Table
 
6
 gives an overview.

Retrieval-augmented generation.

RAG
 grounds 
LLM
 generation in external documents retrieved at query time, improving factual accuracy without retraining 
(
Lewis et al., 2020
; 
Fan et al., 2024
; 
Zhao et al., 2026
)
.
Retrieval is typically cast as nearest-neighbor search over high-dimensional embeddings: queries and documents are embedded into a shared space, and the documents closest to the query are returned 
(
Nene and Nayar, 1997
)
.
Because exact search is expensive at scale, practical systems rely on 
ANN
 indexes such as 
HNSW
 graphs 
(
Malkov and Yashunin, 2020
)
 or 
LSH
 
(
Datar et al., 2004
; 
Li et al., 2020
)
.
These techniques assume documents are collected and indexed under a single administrative domain, so the index can be built globally.

ZeroRAG
 targets the opposite: when documents remain with their owners and no global index can be constructed.

Federated and distributed RAG.

Federated approaches route or merge results across independently managed collections: RAGRoute selects which remote sources to query for a given request 
(
Guerraoui et al., 2025
)
, FeB4RAG benchmarks federated search for 
RAG
 
(
Wang et al., 2024
)
, and pFedRAG personalizes retrieval models across federated clients 
(
He et al., 2025
)
.
Others target the privacy or efficiency of the retrieval: FedVSE provides privacy-preserving vector search over federated databases 
(
Fan et al., 2025
)
, PRAG offers end-to-end privacy-preserving 
RAG
 
(
Li et al., 2026
)
, and Proximity caches semantically similar queries to avoid repeated vector-database lookups 
(
Bergman et al., 2025
)
.
A parallel line scales the retrieval substrate itself: DRAGON distributes 
RAG
 retrieval across local and cloud resources 
(
Liu et al., 2025
)
, DGRAG organizes graph-based retrieval over edge-cloud systems 
(
Zhou et al., 2026
)
, and HARMONY and SPIRE build scalable distributed vector databases for high-throughput 
ANN
 search 
(
Xu et al., 2025b
; 
Xu et al., 2025c
)
.
These systems improve scalability, privacy, or cost, but assume a federated, cloud, or managed deployment in which a coordinating component still orchestrates retrieval.

ZeroRAG
 instead routes each query through peer-local state alone.

Decentralized retrieval and routing.

The closest systems to 
ZeroRAG
 use
a decentralized overlay and a local or global state.
One family imposes a shared logical structure that must be maintained as peers and documents change.
Semantica organizes peers into a global 
LLM
-guided semantic tree overlay 
(
Neague et al., 2025
)
, while 
DHT
 designs such as Kademlia and 
LSH
-based distributed indexes place keys in a global address space for approximate lookup 
(
Maymounkov and Mazières, 2002
; 
Datar et al., 2004
)
.
Blockchain-based designs decentralize control through shared ledger state: D-RAG coordinates decentralized 
RAG
 through a blockchain 
(
Andersen et al., 2025
)
, and a related system records a reliability score for each document source on a blockchain so that the trustworthiness of retrieved content cannot be tampered with 
(
Lu et al., 2025
)
.
These approaches remain decentralized but trade a central authority for global coordination.

A second family keeps routing state local but depends on explicit symbolic descriptions.
REMINDIN’ routes semantic queries using social metaphors and observations of previously successful peers 
(
Tempich et al., 2004
)
; DRAG/TARW performs topic-aware random walks given global knowledge of the available topics 
(
Xu et al., 2025a
)
; and SHIMI organizes decentralized agent memory through an explicit semantic hierarchy 
(
Helmi, 2025
)
.
These systems share 
ZeroRAG
’s local-state philosophy, but rely on topic labels, expertise categories, or maintained hierarchies rather than routing directly from unlabeled embeddings.

A third, longer line of decentralized recommendation and nearest-neighbor work anticipates our overlay mechanics.
WHATSUP disseminates news recommendations through an epidemic protocol that clusters users by interest without any central server 
(
Boutet et al., 2013
)
, and a follow-up obfuscation-based protocol makes such profile-based collaborative filtering privacy-preserving 
(
Boutet et al., 2015
)
.
Most directly, Cluster-and-Conquer constructs a 
-nearest-neighbor graph by starting from a random overlay and greedily improving it through neighbor-of-neighbor exploration 
(
Giakkoupis et al., 2021
)
, the same locality-exploiting principle 
ZeroRAG
 uses to rewire its overlay.
These build on classical decentralized overlay techniques such as gossip-based peer sampling 
(
Jelasity et al., 2007
)
, semantic-proximity content search 
(
Voulgaris et al., 2004
)
, and the broader literature on robust peer-to-peer search 
(
Risson and Moors, 2007
)
.

ZeroRAG
 routes from unlabeled query and document embeddings and, crucially, additionally remembers recently routed queries, letting peers steer toward neighbors with semantically similar request histories.

Overall, prior work occupies only part of the design space.
Shared-structure methods are decentralized and often unlabeled, but require global maintenance; some local routing methods reduce coordination, but rely on labeled or structured semantics; and federated vector-search systems improve scalability, but are not fully decentralized.
To our knowledge, 
ZeroRAG
 is unique in treating retrieval as a querier-based local routing problem: it avoids the global maintenance that 
Semantica
 and Kademlia require, while remaining fully decentralized, unlike centralized or federated alternatives.

6. 
Summary

We presented 
ZeroRAG
, a decentralized routing layer for 
RAG
 that locates relevant documents across an overlay of peers without any central authority, labeled data, or global index.
Each peer keeps only a bounded set of neighbor advertisements and a small cache of recently routed queries, routing each query through a best-first walk that continuously reconfigures the overlay toward semantically relevant neighbors.
Our evaluation on the 
AOL-IA
, 
Amazon Electronics
, and 
PubMedQA
 workloads shows that 
ZeroRAG
 consistently outperforms 
Semantica
 and 
DHT-LSH
, approaches the recall of a centralized 
HNSW
 index in an end-to-end 
RAG
 pipeline (
 vs. 
 at comparable accuracy).

ZeroRAG
 recovers to 
 recall after losing half the network, scales to 
 peers, and absorbs new topics through local interactions alone.
These results indicate that decentralization is a practical foundation for 
RAG
, even when documents remain with the peers that own them.

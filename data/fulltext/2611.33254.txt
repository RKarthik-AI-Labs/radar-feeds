Abstract

Online discussion of generative AI has become increasingly contentious, and communities devoted to supporting, opposing, or debating AI have emerged on social media.
Some of these communities take anti-AI actions, such as deploying crawler tarpits and data-poisoning services to interfere with data collection and model training.
Yet, the scope of these actions and their relationship to online discourse remain poorly understood.
In this paper, we present the first measurement study of online conflict over generative AI, from toxic discourse to anti-AI actions.
We collect 931,640 posts from 25 Reddit communities, label them with a large language model detector validated against human labels, identify 3,312 anti-AI actions, and trace the output of three deployed anti-AI tools.
Our analysis reveals that 30.0% of posts in communities devoted to this conflict are toxic, compared to 4.4% in general AI communities.
Anti-AI actions in these communities mainly target AI crawling or model training data, such as image cloaking and data poisoning.
We further examine the implications of these anti-AI actions for AI training, by analyzing the text and code generated from deployed tools.
Notably, 66.5% of the code samples generated by a data-poisoning service contain fabricated facts, misused APIs, or broken logic, yet 93.6% passes a public code-curation pipeline, close to the rate for real source code (95.0%).
For crawler tarpit output, adding a period to the end of each paragraph increases the curation survival rate from 16.4% to 91.9%.
We also find that crawler tarpit and data-poisoning output has already entered Common Crawl, a widely used public web archive, and that crawler tarpit output has also entered FineWeb, a released pretraining dataset.
We hope our study can facilitate a better understanding of online conflict over generative AI and its implications for content moderation and AI training.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) has drawn growing public concern, as half of U.S. adults now say they are more concerned than excited about its growing use 
[
46
]
.
Within this landscape, online discussion of AI has become increasingly contentious and adversarial 
[
76
, 
60
, 
114
]
.
Some communities criticize AI systems, AI companies, training practices, AI-generated art, or data scraping, while others defend the use of AI and attack those who oppose it.
Several new terms have spread with this polarization, such as “AI slop” for low-quality AI-generated content, and derogatory names such as “clanker” for AI systems, “AI bros” for people who promote AI, and “cogsucker” for people attached to AI chatbots 
[
37
, 
51
]
.
In 2025, Merriam-Webster even named “slop” its word of the year 
[
62
]
.
On Reddit, anti-AI communities have grown quickly.
For instance, in just 15 months after its first post in March 2025, r_antiai, the largest anti-AI subreddit we study, received 47,035 posts.

The conflict over generative AI does not stay in discourse.
Artists and online communities discuss and undertake anti-AI actions, and sometimes build anti-AI tools against data scraping and model training 
[

.
These countermeasures, such as scraper-blocking rules, crawler tarpits, and data-poisoning services

, have spread widely.
In this context, a 
crawler tarpit
 answers a crawler with an endless maze of generated pages to consume its time and bandwidth, while a 
data-poisoning service
 answers it with text and code that reads normally and is factually or logically wrong.
For example, in January 2025, a developer released Nepenthes, a crawler tarpit tool that traps AI crawlers in such a maze, drawing considerable media attention 
[
113
, 
49
]
.
The data-poisoning tool Nightshade was downloaded 250,000 times within five days of its release 
[
33
]
.
By July 2025, more than one million Cloudflare customers had chosen to block AI crawlers 
[
15
]
.

However, the research community still lacks a systematic understanding of online conflict over generative AI, from toxic discourse to anti-AI actions.
Existing studies of AI-related Reddit discussions measure trust, topics, sentiment, and reactions to generative AI 
[
76
, 
13
, 
110
]
, and treat AI as the subject of discussion.
Prior studies of poisoning attacks focus on methods proposed by the researchers themselves 
[
12
, 
105
, 
85
]
, and few examine the data-poisoning tools deployed in the wild.
It remains unclear who these toxic posts are attacking, what anti-AI actions these communities take in practice, and whether the output of deployed anti-AI tools reaches pretraining data.
These gaps hinder online platforms from moderating toxic content in these communities and AI practitioners from developing effective data cleaning strategies.

Our Work.
  
In this paper, we present the first measurement study that follows online conflict over generative AI, from toxic discourse to anti-AI actions.
We refer to subreddits whose purpose is support for, opposition to, or debate about AI as 
polarized AI communities
, and to broad subreddits about AI in general as 
general AI communities
.
As shown in 
Figure 1
, our study covers three layers: toxic discourse, the anti-AI tools and actions these communities discuss, and the text and code that deployed tools generate.
Specifically, we focus on the following research questions:

•

RQ1:
 How prevalent is toxicity in polarized and general AI communities, and whom or what does it attack?

•

RQ2:
 What anti-AI tools and actions do these communities discuss or report deploying, and how can they be classified?

•

RQ3:
 Do the text and code outputs of anti-AI tools reach web-scale crawls, and does pretraining-data curation remove them?

To answer these questions, we collect 931,640 posts from 21 polarized AI communities and four general AI communities on Reddit, spanning from November 2022 to June 2026 (see 
Section 3.1
).
Regarding analysis, we start by labeling all posts with a large language model (LLM) detector validated against human labels, and compare the prevalence and targets of toxicity in polarized and general AI communities, as well as their changes over time (
RQ1
).
We then identify the anti-AI actions these communities discuss and any tools used to carry them out, classify each action into an action family, such as image cloaking or data poisoning, and by the stage of the AI lifecycle it targets, and relate each community’s toxicity targets to the actions it discusses (
RQ2
).
Finally, we select three anti-AI tools that generate text or code, including one data-poisoning service and two crawler tarpits.
We manually annotate the defects in the poisoning service’s output, and follow the output of all three through crawler access and capture by a public web archive to four public curation pipelines and a released pretraining dataset (
RQ3
).

Main Findings.
  
We make the following main findings:

•

Compared with general AI communities, polarized AI communities show a higher toxicity rate and have different targets.
In polarized AI communities, 30.0% of posts are toxic, 6.8 times the rate of general AI communities (4.4%), and in pro-AI communities 67.2% of toxic posts attack anti-AI people.
In general AI communities, 67.5% of toxic posts attack AI systems and companies (see 
Section 3
).

•

Polarized AI communities mainly discuss actions against the data that AI models are trained on, and few actions involve data poisoning.
In polarized AI communities, 55.8% of actions target crawling or the pretraining corpus, mainly through image cloaking, while only 4.6% of all actions involve data poisoning.
Communities in which a larger share of toxic posts attacks pro-AI people discuss image cloaking, data poisoning, and crawler tarpits more often, while within the same subreddit, authors who post anti-AI actions are less likely than other authors to post toxic content.
Both sides also turn platform moderation tools, such as reporting, voting, and copyright detection, against each other (see 
Section 4
).

•

Tool output reaches real-world web-scale crawls and could survive public curation pipelines.
We find that Common Crawl, a public web archive, stores 564 distinct documents from three endpoints disclosed on Reddit, and one Nepenthes page captured in early 2025 is present in FineWeb, a released pretraining dataset 
[
72
]
.
Code generated by Poison Fountain, a data-poisoning service, survives the StarCoder2 code-curation pipeline at 93.6% (vs. 95.0% for raw GitHub source) despite defects in 66.5% of its documents, suggesting that curation retains documents based on their format (see 
Section 5
).

Contributions.
  
Our work makes four main contributions.
(a) We present the first measurement study that follows online conflict over generative AI, from toxic discourse to anti-AI actions.
By analyzing 931,640 posts from 25 subreddits and the output of three deployed tools, we show 
whom
 toxicity in this conflict attacks, 
which
 anti-AI actions communities discuss and take, and 
whether
 the output of their tools reaches pretraining data.
These findings offer platform moderators and AI practitioners insights into the toxicity and the tool output that this conflict produces.
(b) We provide a toxicity-and-target codebook, a 766-post human-labeled reference set, a benchmark of eight LLM-based detectors, and a validated procedure for discovering and classifying anti-AI actions at scale.
(c) We characterize the output of three in-the-wild data-poisoning and crawler tarpit tools and evaluate it against individual filters, four reproduced curation pipelines, and three published poisoning attacks.
Our results show that curation retains documents based on their format and does not examine content correctness.
(d) We verify that output from crawler tarpits and a data-poisoning service has already entered Common Crawl, and that one crawler tarpit page has entered released FineWeb.
We hope our study encourages research on online conflict over generative AI to follow what communities build and deploy, in addition to what they say.

Background and Related Work

Online Hate and AI Discourse.
  
Online hate and harassment have long been studied as a security problem, spanning toxic content, brigading, and raiding 
[
102
, 
88
, 
91
, 
115
]
.
On Twitch, for instance, 98% of hate raid messages consist of identity-based attacks 
[
35
]
.
On Reddit, discussion is divided into subreddits with their own moderators and norms 
[
77
]
, and political subreddits maintain distinctive toxicity norms 
[
84
]
.
In parallel, a growing body of work measures how social media users discuss generative AI, including trust and distrust toward it on Reddit 
[
76
]
, the reactions of students and faculty 
[
110
]
, the topics and sentiment of AI-related discourse 
[
13
]
, and the prevalence of AI-generated media in art subreddits 
[
60
]
.
These studies establish how social media users perceive generative AI.
However, they treat AI as the subject of discussion, and whom toxicity in online conflict over generative AI attacks remains underexplored.
Our work aims to fill this gap.

Creator Resistance and Anti-AI Countermeasures.
  
Artists have raised concerns about generative AI that include style mimicry, consent, and labor displacement 
[
114
, 
42
, 
55
]
.
Website operators increasingly restrict AI crawlers, and between 2023 and 2024 over 28% of the most actively maintained, critical sources in C4 became fully restricted 
[
58
]
.
Research groups have built protective image perturbations 
[
27
]
, such as Glaze 
[
93
]
 and Nightshade, which can corrupt a prompt in Stable Diffusion XL with fewer than 100 poisoned images 
[
94

, and follow-up work shows that such protections weaken once model trainers adapt 
[
81

 or apply simple preprocessing such as upscaling 
[
39
]
.
Outside the research community, crawler tarpits such as Nepenthes and Iocaine serve endless mazes of generated pages 
[
113
, 
64
, 
49
]
, and data-poisoning services such as Poison Fountain serve generated text and code with deliberate errors 
[
89
]
.
These studies establish why creators resist generative AI and how effective research-built protections are.
However, which anti-AI actions online communities themselves discuss and take remains underexplored.

Poisoning Web-Scale Training Data.
  
Poisoning web-scale training datasets is practical, as an attacker could have poisoned 0.01% of LAION-400M or COYO-700M for about $60 by purchasing expired domains that the datasets still reference 
[
12
]
.
Poisoned data has been shown to affect instruction tuning 
[
105
]
, code completion 
[
92
, 
2
]
, and reinforcement learning from human feedback 
[
85
]
.
During pretraining, 250 poisoned documents suffice to backdoor language models from 600M to 13B parameters 
[
96
]
.
These studies establish that a small amount of poisoned web data can change model behavior.
However, the attacks they evaluate are designed by researchers, and whether the output of tools that online communities deploy reaches web-scale crawls remains underexplored.
Our work aims to fill this gap.

Pretraining-Data Curation.
  
Web-scale pretraining corpora are built from raw crawl data by a fixed sequence of filters, namely a 
curation pipeline
 
[
83
, 
74
, 
72
]
.
A typical pipeline first extracts the main text from each HTML page, then identifies its language, then applies rule-based quality heuristics, and finally removes near-duplicate documents.
C4 established widely used line-level rules, RefinedWeb and Dolma scaled the quality heuristics 
[
83
, 
74
, 
95
]
, and FineWeb-Edu and DCLM-baseline add learned quality classifiers 
[
72
, 
54
]
.
For code, StarCoder2 adds syntactic and structural heuristics to license selection and near-duplicate removal 
[
48
, 
59
]
.
However, a strong assumption behind these pipelines is that a document’s surface quality reflects its content, which has not been tested against fluent content that is deliberately wrong.
In this paper, we test this assumption with the output of deployed tools and published poisoning attacks.

Prevalence and Targets of Toxicity

In this section, we address 
RQ1
 by comparing toxicity rates and targets in polarized and general AI communities.
We first select the communities and build the corpus, then evaluate automated detectors against human labels, and finally measure toxicity across the full corpus.

Community Selection and Corpus

Choice of Reddit.
  
Reddit is a social platform where discussion is organized into user-created communities, namely 
subreddits
, each with its own moderators and norms 
[
77
]
.
In a subreddit, a user submits a post with a title and an optional body, and other users reply with comments.
We select Reddit for two main reasons.
First, several subreddits are devoted to opposing, supporting, or debating AI, such as r_antiai, r_DefendingAIArt, and r_aiwars, alongside large subreddits for broad AI discussion.
This structure allows us to compare the two sides of the conflict with each other and with broad AI discussion on the same platform.
Second, Reddit threads announce anti-AI tool releases and disclose deployed endpoints, and Poison Fountain’s developer announces and discusses the service in its own subreddit, r_PoisonFountain.
The same platform therefore allows us to follow the conflict from discourse to the tools it produces.

Candidate Selection.
  
To identify candidate communities, we assemble 74 subreddits, namely 39 general AI candidate subreddits reported by prior work 
[
76
]
 and 35 polarized AI candidate subreddits found through Reddit search, links among related subreddits, and cross-posts.
For each candidate, we read its name, its public description, and 20 posts sampled at random across the study period.
A candidate qualifies as a polarized AI community when its name or description states a purpose of support for, opposition to, or recurring debate about AI or AI-generated content.
When the name or description is ambiguous or outdated, the candidate qualifies if at least 11 of the 20 sampled posts concern support for, opposition to, or debate about AI.
A general AI candidate subreddit qualifies when it is a broad, public-facing subreddit whose discussion spans AI tools, models, companies, news, and social impacts 
[
76
]
.
The same 20 posts determine stance, with recurring support for AI indicating pro-AI, recurring opposition indicating anti-AI, and recurring posts from both positions indicating mixed.
Every selected subreddit contains at least 100 raw posts in the study period.
Among the qualifying general AI candidate subreddits, we keep the four with the most raw posts.
In the end, we select 21 polarized AI communities, namely 15 anti-AI, four pro-AI, and two mixed, together with four general AI communities.

Collection.
  
Using Pushshift 
[

 and Arctic Shift 
[

We keep every post in these subreddits, because a preliminary audit shows that selecting posts by keyword retains fewer than one third of the manually sampled posts from polarized AI communities (

Cleaning.
  

We apply the same text-based cleaning to both corpora.
Following prior Reddit work 
[
76
]
, we remove posts whose author or body is marked as “removed” or “deleted.”
We also remove posts with an empty body and posts whose body contains fewer than five whitespace-separated words.
We then deduplicate the remaining records by Reddit post ID.
Toxicity and target annotation use only the title and post body, excluding images, external links, comments, the subreddit in which each post appeared, and other contextual information.
Tool discovery uses the raw titles and post bodies, which retains the title-only and link-only announcements that cleaning removes (
Section 4.1
).

Codebook and Human Annotation

Sampling.
  
To measure toxicity in these communities, we first sample posts from each corpus for human annotation.
We calculate the sample size with Cochran’s formula for estimating a population proportion 
[
17
]
, at a 95% confidence level and a 
 percentage-point margin of error with 
, and then apply the finite-population correction.
This yields 382 posts for the polarized corpus and 384 for the general corpus.
We draw the polarized sample proportionally across subreddits and the general sample by simple random sampling, both with seed 
.

Toxicity and Target Labels.
  
We annotate each sampled post with two labels, which serve as the ground truth for evaluating automated detectors in 
Section 3.3
.
The first is 
toxicity
, for which we follow prior work 
[
44
, 
43
]
 and use the Perspective API 
[
75
]
 definition: “a rude, disrespectful, or unreasonable comment that is likely to make you leave a discussion.”
The second is the 
target
, namely the one primary entity that the toxic language attacks, and non-toxic posts receive the label NA.
To keep strong criticism separate from toxicity, the codebook includes boundary rules, for instance that criticism of AI systems or companies is non-toxic unless it becomes rude or insulting.

Detector Evaluation

We evaluate automated detectors against the human reference labels and select the best-performing one to label the full corpus.
We first introduce the detectors and metrics, then report toxicity detection and target classification, and finally select the detector for the analysis in

We evaluate two families of detectors.
The first is two zero-shot embedding baselines, all-MiniLM-L6-v2

Target Classification.
  
Because non-toxic predictions receive the target label NA, target scores combine errors in toxicity detection with errors in target assignment.
We therefore report them on both the full 766-post sample and the 112 posts that the human labels mark toxic (
Appendix 
F
).
To check whether a detector with higher recall assigns targets better, we compare GPT-5.4-mini with GPT-5-mini, which reaches the highest recall in 
Table 3
 and scores highest on the toxic subset.
On the full sample, GPT-5.4-mini scores higher than GPT-5-mini (Cohen’s 
 0.59 vs. 0.51).
On the toxic subset, GPT-5-mini scores higher (0.62 vs. 0.41), because it labels more posts toxic and therefore assigns a target to more of them.
When both models use the same toxicity labels, their target assignment is similar.

Model Selection.
  
Based on the above analysis, we select GPT-5.4-mini for both toxicity detection and target assignment.
On 373 posts it labels toxic in general AI communities, its target labels agree with human labels in 95.1% of the posts both mark toxic (Cohen’s 
).
In the rest of this paper, toxicity rates and targets come from GPT-5.4-mini’s labels unless stated otherwise.

Measurement

Using GPT-5.4-mini, we label the 64,776 cleaned posts from polarized AI communities and the 270,871 from general AI communities, and obtain valid labels for all but two general posts.

Toxicity Rate.
  
Overall, polarized AI communities are far more toxic than general AI communities.
As shown in 
Table 4
, 30.0% of posts in polarized AI communities are toxic, compared to 4.4% in general AI communities, a more than sixfold difference.
The difference is statistically significant when each subreddit is treated as one observation (26.3% vs. 4.4%, exact permutation test, 
).
We conduct several robustness checks to validate this finding, including correction for detector error and leaving out one community at a time.
Full details are provided in 
Appendix 
F

This suggests that toxicity concentrates in polarized AI communities, while broad AI discussion remains largely civil.

Toxic Targets.
  
We find that the main target of toxicity differs by community type (

).
In pro-AI communities, 67.2% of toxic posts attack anti-AI people, for instance by calling them

This pattern is attributable to the three largest anti-AI subreddits, r_antiai, r_ArtistHate, and r_Ai_art_is_not_art, while smaller ones such as r_clankers and r_aislop mainly attack AI systems and companies.

This indicates that toxicity in polarized AI communities mainly attacks people, which makes it closer to attacks between the two sides than to criticism of a technology.

Temporal Patterns.
  
Polarized AI communities grow quickly over the study period, and their toxicity rate rises from 2023 to 2024 and remains stable afterward (
Figure 7
).
Their monthly cleaned posts rise from 124 in January 2023 to 4,712 in January 2026.
Their yearly toxicity rate rises from 24.3% in 2023 to 30.3% in 2024, and from 2024 onward their monthly rate remains between 26.7% and 35.2%, compared to below 8% in every month for general AI communities.
Major AI events coincide with large changes in post volume and little change in toxicity rate.
For instance, polarized post volume rises by 141% around GPT-4o image generation and the Ghibli controversy in March 2025, while their monthly toxicity rate remains between 28.4% and 32.1% from February to April 2025 (
Appendix 
G
).
This pattern indicates that these events bring more posts into the conflict without making its discussion more toxic.

Take-Aways:

Toxicity in polarized AI communities differs from that in general AI communities in both rate and target.
Its rate is more than six times the general rate (30.0% vs. 4.4%), and toxic posts in pro-AI communities and in the largest anti-AI communities most often attack the opposing side, whereas toxicity in general AI communities mainly attacks AI systems and companies.
These findings indicate that platforms hosting polarized AI communities mainly face attacks between the two sides, with criticism of AI playing a smaller part.

Anti-AI Tools and Actions

Building on the toxicity targets measured in 
Section 3
, we address 
RQ2
 by examining the anti-AI tools and actions these communities discuss.
Our analysis covers the types of these actions, the lifecycle stages they target, how each side discusses them, and whether a community’s toxicity targets relate to the actions it discusses.

Identifying and Classifying Tools and Actions

Challenges.
  
Identifying anti-AI actions in these communities poses three challenges.
First, posts about anti-AI tools and actions form a small part of the corpus, and the 931,640 posts are too many to read manually.
Second, a post that mentions a tool does not necessarily describe an anti-AI action.
Some tool names are common words, such as Mist, Cara, and Hive, and many posts name a tool only in passing.
Third, many actions, such as opting out of training or pressuring AI users, name no tool, so a search for tool names misses them.

Discovery.
  
To address these challenges, we combine a keyword scan, LLM-based extraction, and a coverage audit.
The corpus discusses tools from research groups and companies, such as Glaze 
[

[

Extraction.
  
To separate actions from passing mentions, GPT-5.4-mini then reads each candidate and assigns an action label, named tools, an action family, a targeted lifecycle stage, and a stance.
A post receives the action label when it proposes, shares, announces, asks how to use, or substantively discusses a concrete anti-AI action or tool.
The prompt provides each label with a short description and example tools, a format that prior work reports to be more reliable than worked examples alone

When a post describes no action, the model leaves the other fields empty.
In the end, GPT-5.4-mini identifies 3,312 posts that describe an action (13.9% of the candidates), 1,678 from polarized AI communities and 1,634 from general AI communities.
We refer to these posts as actions in the rest of the paper.

Action Taxonomy.
  
We classify each action along two dimensions, its action family and the lifecycle stage it targets.
For families, we normalize the extracted tool names to canonical names and remove six out-of-scope families, such as AI products and detection-evasion tools.
In the end, we obtain eight families with named tools (

Although Nightshade poisons text-to-image models, it does so by perturbing images, so the extraction assigns most posts about it to image cloaking.

For example, Nepenthes targets crawler and data collection, Glaze targets the pretraining corpus, and detection, legal action, and boycott target no stage.
In this paper, the pretraining corpus also covers the fine-tuning data that image cloaking targets.

Anti-AI Action Patterns

Targeted Lifecycle Stages.
  
Overall, each action family mainly targets one stage, so the stages that actions target differ between polarized and general AI communities, and few actions involve data poisoning.
As shown in 
Figure 4

For instance, image cloaking and data poisoning target the pretraining corpus, crawler tarpits and access blocking target crawler and data collection, and detection, legal action, and boycott mainly target no stage.
In polarized AI communities, 936 of the 1,678 actions (55.8%) target crawling or the pretraining corpus, 564 of them image cloaking.
In contrast, in general AI communities, 1,243 of the 1,634 actions (76.1%) target no stage, 924 of them detection.
Only 151 actions (4.6%) involve data poisoning.

Action Families in Polarized and General AI Communities.
  
We find that polarized and general AI communities discuss different families of actions (

Polarized AI communities hold 91.3% of the image-cloaking actions and 82.8% of the data-poisoning actions, while general AI communities hold 85.6% of the detection and provenance actions.
When each of the 15 subreddits with at least 10 actions is treated as one observation, the difference in detection and provenance is statistically significant (48.3% vs. 10.0%, exact permutation test with Holm correction,

The differences in image cloaking and data poisoning are not statistically significant after the correction (

Overall, general AI communities mainly discuss detecting AI-written text, for instance with GPTZero or Turnitin, while polarized AI communities discuss tools that act on training data.
This may be because many of these posts concern schoolwork, as 42.9% of the detection actions in general AI communities mention essays, assignments, students, teachers, or plagiarism.

Stance toward Anti-AI Tools.
  
In every community type, most posts about image cloaking, data poisoning, and crawler tarpits only discuss these tools, and the remaining posts differ by side (

The difference in stance across community types is statistically significant (

In anti-AI communities, 30.7% of these posts advocate using a tool and 7.5% give how-to guidance, almost all for image cloaking.
In pro-AI communities, 60 of the 63 such posts concern image cloaking, and 22.2% criticize it, compared to 4.9% in the other community types (Fisher’s exact test,

For instance, a post in a pro-AI community claims that new image models learn from cloaked images without trouble (

Of the 14 such posts that criticize these tools, six question whether the tools work and none opposes their use in principle, while two state that artists are free to use them.
Attempts to bypass these tools are rare.
A keyword search over the posts in pro-AI and mixed communities finds 31 that mention removing, bypassing, or training on cloaked images.
Among them, 14 report a de-cloaking tool, removal code, or a training test in which the cloak fails.

Actions toward the Opposing Side.
  
Beyond countermeasures against AI systems, the two sides also act against each other.
Actions in the harassment/pressure family make up 7.5% of actions in pro-AI communities (12 of 161), all from r_DefendingAIArt, compared to 2.0% in mixed, 1.1% in anti-AI, and 0.7% in general AI communities.
Manual inspection reveals that the two sides use similar tactics (
Appendix

In pro-AI communities, five of the 12 posts propose a concrete action, such as reporting anti-AI users to Reddit’s administrators or calling for coordinated voting, while the other seven document harassment they receive from the anti-AI side.
In anti-AI communities, five of the 13 posts describe a concrete action, such as reporting AI-generated images as spam or rehosting AI-generated videos to trigger copyright detection.
Take membership-based bans as an example.
In 2023, the moderators of one anti-AI subreddit announce that they are banning pro-AI users by hand while the bots that normally ban them are down.
In 2026, a post in a pro-AI subreddit asks why the community does not use a bot that bans members of anti-AI subreddits.
These results indicate that the two sides also use platform moderation tools against each other.

Releases and Deployment Claims.
  
Posts alone do not show whether these communities release and deploy tools, so here we center our analysis on whole threads, including their comments.
In our data, 465 threads discuss nine tools across the action families, such as Glaze, Nightshade, GPTZero, and Poison Fountain.
We label the roles of each thread, such as announcing a release or claiming a deployment, with an LLM, and these labels agree with human coding at Cohen’s

Among them, 108 threads announce a release and 57 claim a deployment.
Anti-AI communities contribute 70.4% of the release threads and 77.2% of the deployment threads.
However, 46.1% of the anti-AI release threads also contain skepticism, suggesting that these releases are questioned within the same communities.

Toxicity Targets and Anti-AI Actions

Section 3
shows that toxicity in polarized AI communities mainly attacks people, and the above analysis shows that these communities hold most of the image-cloaking and data-poisoning actions.
We therefore investigate whether the target of a community’s toxicity relates to the actions it discusses, first across communities and then across individual authors.

Measurement.
  
To compare communities, we treat each of the 15 subreddits with at least 10 actions as one observation.
For each subreddit, we first compute the share of its toxic posts that attack each group and the share of its actions in each family, and then measure the association between the two shares with Spearman’s rank correlation, tested by permutation.
To compare authors, we treat each Reddit account as one author and mark each of the 175,246 authors in our data by whether they post toxic content and whether they post an anti-AI action.
Because an author with more posts has more chances to do both, we stratify authors by posting volume and adjust for it with logistic regression.

Community Level.
  
We find that the target of a community’s toxicity is associated with the actions it discusses (
Figure 9
).
Communities in which a larger share of toxic posts attacks pro-AI people discuss image cloaking, data poisoning, and crawler tarpits more often (Spearman’s 
, permutation test, 
).
In contrast, communities in which a larger share attacks AI systems and companies discuss these three families less often (
, 
) and discuss actions that target no lifecycle stage more often (
, 
).
For instance, in anti-AI subreddits such as r_ArtistHate, r_Ai_art_is_not_art, and r_antiai, pro-AI people receive 46.3% to 59.8% of the toxicity.
Image cloaking accounts for 616 (74.4%) of the 828 cloaking, poisoning, and crawler tarpit actions in the 15 subreddits.
This indicates that these associations mainly reflect image cloaking.
All three associations remain statistically significant after a Holm correction and keep their sign under the robustness checks in 
Appendix 
J
, such as leaving out one community at a time and correcting the target labels for detector error.
This association does not establish that toxicity toward pro-AI people leads to action, which the author-level analysis below examines.

Author Level.
  
However, across individual authors, we find that toxic posting and anti-AI action are linked only through posting activity and community.
Of the 2,464 authors who post an anti-AI action, referred to as action authors, 27.4% also post toxic content, compared to 11.4% of all 175,246 authors (unadjusted odds ratio 3.00).
Most of this difference is attributable to posting activity, since 73.3% of all authors contribute a single post, while action authors concentrate among the most active.
As shown in 
Table 7
, a smaller difference remains within most posting-volume strata, for instance 59.8% vs. 41.4% among authors with 6 to 20 posts.
Adjusting for posting volume lowers the odds ratio to 1.41 (logistic regression).
Also adjusting for each author’s main subreddit lowers it to 0.69, so within the same subreddit, action authors are less likely than other authors to post toxic content.
This may be because many action posts exchange information about a tool.
For instance, in anti-AI communities, most cloaking, poisoning, and crawler tarpit posts only discuss these tools or give how-to guidance (
Table 6
).
Among authors who post both toxic and action content, toxicity comes first no more often than expected when each author’s post timestamps are shuffled.
This suggests that toxicity and action align across communities and separate across authors.

Case Study: r_PoisonFountain

To show how an anti-AI tool is built and deployed in practice, we take r_PoisonFountain as a case study.
It is the only subreddit in our data built around a single deployed anti-AI tool, namely Poison Fountain, a web service operated by its developer that generates poisoned code and text on every request 
[
89
]
.
The service runs outside Reddit, and website operators forward crawler requests to its feed through their server configurations.
In February 2026, the developer creates the subreddit to announce the service, and then posts configurations for common web servers and platforms, such as Nginx, Apache, and Netlify, together with a Tor mirror of the feed.
From February to June 2026, the developer announces 11 new versions of the generator, each of which takes effect at the feed without any action from the website operators.
Notably, the developer writes 53.6% of the 291 posts with a known author and 22.9% of the 3,182 comments.
Across the 306 threads, an effectiveness claim appears in 39.5%, while skepticism appears in 16.3%.
This suggests that data poisoning on the anti-AI side depends on a few developers who maintain a shared service, while most participants promote, discuss, or question it.

Take-Aways:

In polarized AI communities, most anti-AI actions target the data that AI models are trained on, mainly through image cloaking, while only 4.6% of all actions involve data poisoning.
Pro-AI communities criticize these tools more often than other community types, most often by questioning whether they work.
Communities in which a larger share of toxic posts attacks pro-AI people discuss image cloaking, data poisoning, and crawler tarpits more often, while within the same community, action authors are less likely than other authors to post toxic content.
These findings imply that the people who discuss anti-AI actions are largely different from the ones who post toxic content.

Characterizing Poison and Crawler Tarpit Output

To address 
RQ3
, this section follows the output of three text and code tools from the endpoints to a pretraining corpus, analyzing what the tools generate, whether crawlers capture their output, and whether curation removes it.
We first describe the tools and the experimental setup, and then follow their output from the endpoint to Common Crawl and a released pretraining corpus.

Scope and Tool Selection

Scope.
  
We study public web endpoints, namely the URLs at which tools serve their output, that a crawler can reach without privileged access, a setting in which poisoning web-scale training data is practical 
[
12
]
.
The tools in this setting have two goals, availability and integrity, which established taxonomies of attacks on machine-learning systems separate 
[
104
, 
20
]
.
Crawler tarpits target availability by consuming crawler time and bandwidth, so we measure what they cost a crawler in addition to whether their output survives curation.
Data-poisoning services target integrity, and since a small number of poisoned documents can change the behavior of a language model (
Section 2
), they may affect a model once their documents enter a pretraining corpus.
Along this path, a tool operator controls only what its endpoint serves, while the corpus builder controls crawling, extraction, filtering, deduplication, and admission to the corpus.
Common Crawl 
[
22
]
 is the source of FineWeb 
[
72
]
, C4 
[
83
]
, and DCLM-baseline 
[
54
]
, so content it captures can enter the three text pipelines we evaluate.
StarCoder2 
[
59
]
 relies on repository files, so for code we evaluate whether Poison Fountain code would pass StarCoder2 if uploaded to public repositories.
The repository generator Slopinator already automates this step by wrapping poisoned code into repositories and uploading them to GitHub 
[
101
]
.

Tool Selection.
  
In our previous analysis, we identify 151 data-poisoning and 64 crawler tarpit actions, most of which concern tools that generate text or code.
We manually inspect the 43 threads that make testable claims about the output of these tools.
These threads disagree on whether this output passes pretraining-data filters, with 11 asserting that it does and 16 expecting the filters to remove it.
Additionally, four threads doubt that the output reaches training corpora, while one asserts that it does.
We focus on these tools for two reasons.
First, image cloaking already has a body of robustness work (
Section 2
) and requires a separate image-training pipeline.
Second, the output of deployed text and code tools has no prior measurement, and Poison Fountain targets language models.
We select tools that cover distinct generation mechanisms, and a tool enters our evaluation when it runs its own generator and when its output can be collected or generated locally.

Crawler Access and Capture

Generated output can enter a pretraining corpus only if a crawler fetches and stores it.
To examine this, we first inspect whether the public endpoints expose their output to crawlers, and then search the Common Crawl index 
[
21
]
 for pages that these endpoints served.

Crawler Access.
  
Using bounded requests, we inspect the 
robots.txt
 files, sitemaps, and response headers of the public endpoints.
Poison Fountain returns generated content for every path, including 
robots.txt
, and its feed accepts unauthenticated requests.
Nepenthes excludes only its download directories, leaving the generated maze crawlable by default.

Crawler Cost.
  
Besides reaching the output, a crawler that enters a crawler tarpit may never run out of new URLs.
We crawl a local Iocaine instance and a static control site built from real web text, matched on links per page and page size so that only the size of the URL space differs.
The crawl of the control site ends after 395 requests, whereas Iocaine yields 4.37 new URLs per request and still has 5,053 URLs queued when our cap of 1,500 requests is reached.
This suggests that such a crawl stops only at the crawler’s own limits.

Choice of Common Crawl.
  
The challenge of measuring capture mainly lies in the lack of transparency of crawlers, as commercial crawlers, including those run by AI vendors, publish neither what they fetch nor what they store.
Therefore, we rely on Common Crawl 
[
22
]
, which publishes both an index and the archived pages.
This brings two benefits.
First, we can verify the exact bytes that a crawler stored.
Second, Common Crawl is the source of the three text corpora whose pipelines we reproduce, so capture and curation lie on one data path.

Verified Captures.
  
We extract endpoint candidates from three sources, namely the posts and comments in r_PoisonFountain, the deployment threads in 
Section 4.2
, and 4,022 posts outside the keyword-scan candidates that a separate scan flags.
The scan flags posts that link to code repositories or to data-poisoning and crawler tarpit domains, or that mention crawler blocking, such as robots.txt rules and GPTBot blocks, or opt-out registries.
Together, these sources yield 173 candidates.
We retain endpoints that community members disclose as serving generated content at a public URL, which removes news links, code hosts, placeholders, and the vendor of a bot-blocking service.
This leaves eight endpoints.
For each remaining endpoint, we query the Common Crawl index and download every archived page to check whether it contains tool output.
Note that the presence of a domain in Common Crawl does not indicate that its tool output is stored, so we count only pages whose content we verify.
In the end, three endpoints contain tool output archived across at least two monthly crawls each, namely the Poison Fountain feed, the Nepenthes maze, and a self-built Markov crawler tarpit on a blogger’s website.
In the six monthly crawls from January through June 2026, Common Crawl recorded 704 captures from these endpoints and stored 564 distinct documents, as shown in 
Table 11
.
All three captured endpoints are operated by the developers of their respective tools.
Through manual inspection, we identify why the other five endpoints contain no tool output: one blocks AI crawlers, one renders its content in the browser so that Common Crawl stores the page without its content, one returns only rate-limit responses, one serves ordinary blog pages, and one independent Poison Fountain deployment has no Common Crawl record at all.
We also query the two hosts run by the developer of Iocaine, namely its documentation site and code forge, from which Common Crawl stores 4,353 distinct documents, 88.5% of all captured tool output.
Since no post in our data discloses these two hosts, we exclude them from 
Table 11
.

Generation Strategy and Duplication.
  
How many distinct documents a crawler stores depends on how a tool generates its content.

Poison Fountain Code.
  
Overall, StarCoder2 does not separate Poison Fountain code from real source code.
As shown in 
Table 12
, Poison Fountain code survives at 93.6%, compared to 95.0% for raw GitHub source and 95.3% for The Stack.
Specifically, Poison Fountain code is 99.7% syntactically valid, matching real code, and its unresolved-import rate is lower than The Stack’s (3.0% vs. 10.0%).
Among Python documents that use the standard library, the share that references a nonexistent name in it is also comparable (3.4% vs. 3.8%).
As a result, 65.2% of the audited code documents that StarCoder2 retains are defective, compared to 66.5% before curation.
This indicates that StarCoder2 checks the form of source code and does not check its API usage and program logic.

Poison Fountain Text.
  
In contrast, the survival of Poison Fountain text depends on the pipeline, at 0.1% under DCLM-baseline, 13.0% under FineWeb, and 50.8% under C4.
These rates exceed those of the raw crawl (8.7% and 15.3% under FineWeb and C4) because only 28.1% of the raw crawl is English.
Poison Fountain text also has a lower median perplexity than FineWeb (31 vs. 42), so a predictability measure ranks it above real web text.
Moreover, most removals of Poison Fountain text come from a single Gopher rule, which requires most words to contain a letter, and this rule removes real technical documentation at an even higher rate.
This highlights that the pipelines remove Poison Fountain text for its documentation format, and none removes it for its defects.

Crawler Tarpit Text.
  
The three crawler tarpit corpora we generate survive at 0.0% to 6.7% under the text pipelines, and Iocaine and Nepenthes fail for different reasons.
Iocaine’s output contains only titles, metadata, and links, so the Gopher quality heuristic 
[
82
]

In contrast, Nepenthes passes that heuristic almost entirely, since its Markov generator runs over real prose and reproduces normal word, symbol, and stopword distributions.
Each pipeline still removes it at a different stage, namely FineWeb’s own quality filter, C4’s terminal-punctuation rule, and DCLM-baseline’s learned classifier.
Note that each pipeline applies its own main-text extractor, namely Trafilatura 
[
6
]

[
54

The extractor changes the stage at which a crawler tarpit is removed (

Archived Pages.
  
We also run the pages that Common Crawl stores (
Section 5.4
) through the same pipelines, separately for each endpoint, after checking each against the hash that Common Crawl records.
All ten archived Poison Fountain code documents pass StarCoder2, consistent with the 93.6% rate on the code we collect from the feed, and the four archived text documents are too few to support a rate.
The Nepenthes maze stays within a few points of the rates on the Nepenthes pages we generate, at 3.0%, 6.1%, and 5.9% under FineWeb, C4, and DCLM-baseline.
These results indicate that, for these two tools, the rates on the output we collect or generate also hold for the output that crawlers actually stored.

Page Format.
  
However, the self-built crawler tarpit in 
Table 11

To separate page format from content, we rebuild its page format, fill it with text from a Markov generator we control, and then change one format property at a time (
Table 13

Our rebuilt version survives FineWeb at 91.9%.
Removing terminal punctuation lowers this rate to 16.4%.
Raising symbol density or removing stopwords removes almost all pages under FineWeb and leaves C4 unchanged, because C4 runs no Gopher stage.
Increasing the link count from 5 to 60 lowers survival under C4 and leaves FineWeb unchanged, because FineWeb’s extractor discards the list of links as navigation while C4’s full-page render retains it.
This shows that crawler tarpit survival depends on page format, and each pipeline responds to different format properties.

Learned Quality Classifiers.
  
The learned quality classifiers also fail to separate generated from genuine content, and they score some generated content higher than genuine content.
For example, a FineWeb-Edu classifier scores Poison Fountain text above FineWeb (1.21 vs. 1.14).
This may be because the FineWeb-Edu classifier is trained to rate the educational value of a page 
[
72
]
, and most Poison Fountain text is written as technical documentation.
When we score each corpus directly with DCLM-baseline’s classifier, it rates 42.6% of Poison Fountain text above its keep threshold, compared to 11.2% of real English pages from the raw crawl.
This highlights that surface quality, whether scored by rules or by a learned classifier, is a poor indicator of semantic correctness.

Case Study: Nepenthes Page

As demonstrated in our previous analysis, crawlers capture tool output, and curation does not remove all of it.
Capture by Common Crawl does not place a document in a pretraining corpus, so we search a released one, namely FineWeb 
[
72
]
.
FineWeb is built from Common Crawl’s monthly crawls up to June 2025 
[
40
]
.
Of our three endpoints, only the Nepenthes maze falls inside that window, with 53 of its pages captured in four crawls in 2025, which precede the 2026 crawls in 
Table 11
.
We scan two of these crawls, which hold 36 of the captured pages and more than 443 million FineWeb documents in total, and find one maze page.
Its text is Markov output, for instance 
“the Red Sox against the Dutch, was largely outside of the population of Las Vegas.”

Notably, FineWeb’s language filter scores it as English with a confidence of 0.985.
The timestamp of this page also matches the Common Crawl capture to the second, so the released document and the archived page come from the same fetch.
One page is what our reproduced pipeline predicts, since FineWeb retains 3.0% of the archived maze pages (
Section 5.5
) and the crawl that contains the page holds 34 of the captures.
Tool output therefore reaches a released pretraining corpus, at the low rate our reproduced pipelines measure.

Take-Aways:

Tool output reaches web-scale crawls, and the curation pipelines we reproduce do not separate its defective content from clean content of the same kind.
For instance, Poison Fountain code survives StarCoder2 at 93.6% despite defects in 66.5% of audited code documents, and crawler tarpit survival depends on page format.
Published poisoning attacks also pass these pipelines at rates close to their clean counterparts, and one archived Nepenthes page is present in released FineWeb.
These findings suggest that AI practitioners cannot rely on surface-quality filters to keep such output out of pretraining data, and need checks on content correctness or on the provenance of their sources.

Discussion

Our analysis, moving from toxic discourse to anti-AI actions and tool output, shows that toxicity in polarized AI communities mainly attacks people, while the output of anti-AI tools already reaches web-scale crawls.
Our findings have implications for online platforms and AI practitioners.

Recommendations to Online Platforms.
  
Our study reveals that toxic posts in pro-AI communities and in the largest anti-AI communities most often attack people on the opposing side.
Platforms could therefore consider moderation that separates criticism of AI from attacks on the people who support or oppose it.
Considering that action authors are less likely than other authors in the same subreddit to post toxic content, such moderation is unlikely to restrict the discussion of anti-AI actions.
Besides, as our study shows, both sides use platform moderation tools against each other, such as coordinated voting and membership-based bans.
Platforms are therefore recommended to review such bans and to monitor calls for coordinated voting.

Community Claims and Measured Outcomes.
  
Our findings demonstrate that our measurements support most of the claims these communities make about tool output.
For instance, Common Crawl stores output from three disclosed endpoints, and one Nepenthes page is present in released FineWeb.
The most contested claim, that the output passes curation, holds for Poison Fountain code, while for text it depends on the pipeline and the page format.
This likely indicates that each side of this disagreement is correct for part of the output.

Recommendations to AI Practitioners.
  
Our study reveals that curation pipelines check the format of a document and do not check whether its content is correct.
For example, Poison Fountain code passes StarCoder2 at the rate of real source code despite its defects.
Three published poisoning attacks also pass these pipelines at rates close to their clean counterparts.

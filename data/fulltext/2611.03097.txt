Abstract

Complex documents such as scientific papers contain rich information in tables, figures, and equations; however, existing deep research (DR) agents typically reduce papers to OCR-extracted text. We instead ask whether vision-language models (VLMs) can enable 
page-native deep research
: directly retrieving and reasoning over rendered document page images. We introduce 
DR Pixel
, the first open-source end-to-end multimodal DR agent that uses rendered page images as its sole retrieval and reasoning unit, operating over a corpus of 1.06M pages. To evaluate this setting, we construct 
DRPixelBench
, a benchmark on scientific papers requiring reasoning over text, figures, tables, and equations.
Surprisingly, page-native DR already works well without training, outperforming OCR-based baselines across state-of-the-art models like GPT-5.4, Gemini-3.5-Flash, and Qwen3.6-27B, while requiring fewer tool calls.

We next turn to DR training.
A key challenge is that teacher trajectories for agentic supervised finetuning are noisy, mixing critical reasoning and action steps with redundant or failed attempts.
To address this, we introduce 
counterfactual credit reweighting
, a fine-grained credit assignment method that weights individual reasoning and action steps by their counterfactual contribution to the final answer.
Our training improves retrieval recall by about 15 points and final accuracy by over 10 points on 
DRPixelBench
, and likewise outperforms the untrained baseline on four existing benchmarks. On out-of-distribution ViDoRe-V3, 
DR Pixel
 comes within 
 points of an oracle that is given the gold pages directly. Our results suggest that the conventional 
OCR-then-retrieve
 pipeline is not only unnecessary but suboptimal: directly retrieving and reasoning over visual document pages makes complex DR both more effective and more efficient.

Introduction

Deep research (DR) agents answer complex information-seeking questions by iteratively searching, reading, and reasoning over large collections of evidence 
(
OpenAI, 2025
; 
Gemini, 2025
; 
Li et al., 2025d
; 
Li et al., 2025a
)
. As DR expands beyond the open web into domains such as law, finance, and science, it increasingly operates over specialized document collections 
(
Li et al., 2025b
; 
Choi et al., 2026
; 
Xie et al., 2024
; 
Chen et al., 2025a
; 
Chen et al., 2025b
)
. These documents are inherently multimodal: critical evidence often lies not only in text, but also in tables, figures, charts, and equations.

Yet, such documents are typically reduced to plain text: PDFs are linearized into text, and retrieval and reasoning operate entirely in text space. This introduces two key limitations. First, linearization discards or distorts visual structure, making information in figures, tables, or charts difficult or impossible to recover 
(
Poznanski et al., 2025
; 
Lo et al., 2023
)
. Second, extraction and OCR errors propagate into downstream retrieval and reasoning 
(
Zhang et al., 2025a
; 
Wang et al., 2026
)
.

This raises a natural question: 
Can deep research instead be page-native, retrieving and reasoning directly over rendered document pages?
 In such an approach, each page remains an image: a visual retriever searches over rendered pages, and a vision-language model (VLM) reasons directly over the retrieved images.
Prior work has shown that such page-native retrieval can match or outperform text-based document retrieval 
(
Faysse et al., 2025
; 
de Souza P. Moreira et al., 2026
)
, and has extended this approach to retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) tasks.
We study this approach in the deep research regime, where answering a query requires many rounds of retrieval and reasoning. To this end, we introduce 
DR Pixel
, the first open-source 
page-native
 multimodal DR agent that iteratively searches and reasons directly over 1.06M rendered pages from scientific papers.

, feeds retrieved images to the VLM agent, and maintains them as visual evidence throughout the trajectory, without ever being converted into plain text. The agent iteratively formulates queries, retrieves images, and reasons over evidence accumulated across turns to generate the final answer.
Our evaluation on

We summarize our key contributions as follows:

1.

We introduce 
DR Pixel
, a page-native deep research agent that treats papers as collections of rendered page images and iteratively retrieves and reasons over them. Across GPT-5.4, Gemini-3.5, and Qwen3.6, 
DR Pixel
 consistently outperforms OCR-based models that convert papers to text.

2.

We introduce a new DR training method,

3.

We release

Related Work

Deep research (DR) agents.

DR agents tackle complex, multi-step information-seeking tasks through iterative search and synthesis 
(
Yao et al., 2023
; 
Li et al., 2025d
; 
Li et al., 2025c
; 
Shao et al., 2026
)
. Recent work extends the pipeline to multimodal deep research by adding tools such as image search, reverse image search or cross-modality retrieval 
(
Geng et al., 2026
; 
Yao et al., 2026
)
. Most of them focus on open web, where text and images are retrieved separately through two different search tools 
(
Tao et al., 2026
; 
Li et al., 2026b
; 
Jiang et al., 2024
)
. We instead target a collection of documents (e.g., scientific papers), where answering complex questions often requires jointly interpreting text, tables, and figures on the same page, making separate text and image retrieval interfaces suboptimal.

Page-native document retrieval.

With rapid progress with vision-language models (VLMs), bypassing OCR pipelines and directly processing rendered document images has emerged as a promising direction 
(
Kim et al., 2022
; 
Lee et al., 2023
; 
Blecher et al., 2024
; 
Wei et al., 2026
)
.
Building on this, visual document retrieval retrieves rendered pages directly, established by ColPali and followed by increasingly capable visual retrievers 
(
Faysse et al., 2025
; 
Günther et al., 2025
; 
Li et al., 2026a
)
.
More recent work extends this approach to further generate answers 
(
Yu et al., 2025
; 
Wang et al., 2025a
; 
Wang et al., 2026
)
 and add iterative retrieval 
(
Wang et al., 2025b
; 
Shen et al., 2026
)
. However, these settings require relatively shallow search, e.g., on ViDoSeek 
(
Wang et al., 2025a
)
 and SlideVQA 
(
Tanaka et al., 2023
)
, a single retrieval step already recalls the gold page in the top five for 
 of questions 
(
Wang et al., 2025a
; 
Giouroukis et al., 2025
)
. We instead study page-native deep research where complex queries require sustained search and reasoning over 
–
 steps.

Agent training.

Broadly, agents that generate long action trajectories are commonly trained with outcome-based reinforcement learning (RL) 
(
Jin et al., 2025
; 
Zheng et al., 2025
; 
Wang et al., 2025b
; 
Shen et al., 2026
)
, sometimes with rubrics 
(
Shao et al., 2026
)
, or through supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on teacher-generated trajectories 
(
Chen et al., 2023
; 
Li et al., 2025a
)
. Both provide limited credit assignment across a trajectory: outcome-based RL assigns a single reward to the rollout, and SFT imitates every teacher step regardless of its utility. Prior work has explored finer-grained supervision through process reward models that score each intermediate step 
(
Lightman et al., 2023
)
 or by leveraging token- or step-level supervision coming from a teacher model conditioned on privileged context  
(
Meng and Chen, 2026
; 
Wu et al., 2026
)
, both during RL.
In this paper, we propose an alternative approach that retains the simplicity of SFT: we reweight each agentic step by its counterfactual contribution to the final answer, estimated by removing the step and measuring the resulting change in answer quality.

We evaluate on four existing benchmarks (IRPAPERS, M3SciQA, AirQA, ViDoRe-V3) but argue that page-native DR requires a new benchmark, meeting four key properties: (1) 
agentic search
, where relevant evidence typically requires multiple rounds of retrieval and reasoning; (2)

short, verifiable answers

DRPixelBench
.

To this end, we build 
DRPixelBench
 over scientific literature. Scientific papers are naturally multimodal, with important information distributed across text, tables, figures, and equations.

We collect papers from arXiv and the open-access proceedings of major conferences, and merge in the papers of AirQA 
(
Huang et al., 2026
)
, M3SciQA 
(
Li et al., 2024
)
 and IRPAPERS 
(
Shorten et al., 2026
)
 so that 
DRPixelBench
 and these three benchmarks share one corpus of 
 papers.

Inspired by 
Dasigi et al. (2021)
, we generate each question from a paper’s abstract rather than from its full text to keep it tied to the paper’s main contribution. As an abstract reports its findings with few supporting details, we first enrich it. Using Gemini-3.5-Flash 
(
Google, 2026
)
, we extract atomic facts from the full paper, such as a comparison in a table or a trend in a figure, and add them to the abstract. We then prompt the model to write a question from the enriched abstract alone, so that the question is driven by curiosity rather than by evidence already in view. We further ask the model to locate the supporting evidence in the full paper and produce a short-form answer. Depending on the form of this evidence, each question is labeled 
Text
 or 
Visual
: a Text question is answered from the running text, while a Visual question requires a table, figure, or equation.
Each QA pair then undergoes four automatic checks to ensure that the question is plausible, the gold evidence is sufficient, non-textual evidence is necessary, and the answer cannot be recovered from parametric knowledge alone.
Finally, human experts review the question and rewrite it for naturalness.
The resulting 
DRPixelBench
 contains 
 QA pairs.
More details, including the data sources, parsing details, filter criteria, annotation details, and data statistics, are reported in 
Appendix
 
A
.

5.1 
Training Method

We train 
DR Pixel
 with supervised fine-tuning (SFT). We sample trajectories from GPT-5.4 on training questions generated using the same pipeline as §
3

However, we find standard SFT has a problem because the trajectories in the training data often contain repeated or low-value searches that do not contribute to the final answer.
Naively imitating these unhelpful steps encourages excessive searching and inefficient use of the retrieval budget. Inspired by process rewards, which assign credit to individual intermediate steps 
(
Lightman et al., 2024
; 
Setlur et al., 2025
; 
Zhou et al., 2024
; 
Zhou et al., 2025
)
, we instead weight each step by its contribution to the final answer. Specifically, our 
counterfactual credit reweighting
 removes each step from a fixed teacher trajectory and measures the resulting drop in the gold-answer likelihood under the frozen base model, assigning higher weight to steps that are more consequential.

6 
Conclusion

Deep research over complex documents typically begins with OCR. 
DR Pixel
 instead performs retrieval and reasoning directly over rendered pages. We study this setting with 
DRPixelBench
, containing 
 verifiable questions over 
M pages of scientific literature, and show that page-native deep research consistently outperforms OCR-based pipelines across all tested models while using fewer tool calls, even without task-specific training. To close the remaining gap for open-weight models, we introduce 
counterfactual credit reweighting
, which weights teacher steps by their contribution to the gold answer and brings a 
B model to GPT-5.4-level accuracy with lower search cost. These gains transfer to external benchmarks and non-scientific documents. More broadly, page-native deep research requires only document rendering rather than format-specific parsing, making it particularly attractive for private, layout-rich corpora such as financial or law documents where maintaining OCR and parsing pipelines is costly and brittle.

As described in 
section 3
, every question that passes all verification gates is reviewed manually before entering 
DRPixelBench
. We detail the annotation procedure here.

For each question, the annotator sees the question, the answer, the gold page, and the specific grounded block, such as a text span, figure, table, or equation. The full gold paper is also available. The annotator checks three things: (1) the question is well-formed and valid; (2) the answer is concise enough for reliable LLM grading; (3) the gold page is correct and sufficient to answer the question. Annotators may edit a question to fix these issues or reject it.

Only about one in ten questions passes annotation unchanged, and about half are rejected. The main reasons for rejection are that the gold answer contradicts the paper, that no single page suffices to answer the question, or that the answer is too open-ended to grade reliably. Annotation takes about 10 minutes per question.

Optimization.

We fine-tune Qwen3.6-27B with LoRA of rank

, on every linear projection of the language model; the vision encoder stays frozen. Training runs for one epoch at a constant learning rate of

bfloat16

 tokens.

Applying the weights.

Counterfactual credits are computed offline against the frozen base policy at two forward passes per step and written into the training file as a per-message weight. Each assistant message is split at the boundary between its reasoning and its action so the two channels can carry different weights, and the trainer multiplies each message’s token loss by its weight. The training step is therefore ordinary supervised fine-tuning at no extra cost, and the whole method sits in data preparation. Observation tokens are excluded from the loss in every arm.

Equation 4
fixes three properties that the counterfactual credit does not itself determine: the magnitude of the surviving weights, the pool of steps a given step is ranked against, and how much mass a single trajectory may contribute. Here we ablate on those three properties in 
Table 16
 with the training recipe held fixed.

Weight magnitude.

Masking every step of non-positive credit zeroes about a quarter of the steps and leaves the mean weight roughly a third below the uniform baseline, so the weighted run effectively takes smaller optimisation steps, and a constant factor on all weights is equivalent to a change of learning rate. Restoring the mean to the baseline settles this: accuracy given the gold page falls from 
 to 
, the largest reading loss in the family, while the number of searches barely moves.

Rank support.

A counterfactual credit is larger on hard questions, where deleting any step moves the likelihood of the gold answer substantially, so ranking against the whole training set imports question difficulty into a step-level signal. The effect is measurable: the mean surviving weight of a trajectory ranges from 
 at the tenth percentile to 
 at the ninetieth, so a step’s weight depends substantially on which trajectory it belongs to. We also try ranking a step only against the other steps of its own trajectory, and only against the steps of the same question’s rollouts. Both remove the leak, because a step is then never compared with a step from a different question. Neither helps: within-trajectory ranking stays on par with the reference (50.1 vs. 50.9) and within-question ranking is 2.3 points lower, so the bias is real but harmless.

Trajectory contribution.

Equal mean weights do not equalise what a trajectory contributes: its total weighted mass spans a factor of about six between the tenth and ninetieth percentiles, since a long or lightly masked trajectory supplies far more weighted tokens. Equalising that mass is the most aggressive variant we tried and the weakest result, five points below the reference. This is because a light trajectory gets multiplied by a large ratio.

Numerical calibration is thus not where the credit acts. Rescaling the weights either does nothing or degrades reading, and changing the support of the rank does nothing at all. What remains is which steps the credit masks and how that mask is distributed along a trajectory.

Abstract

World-action models increasingly apply test-time scaling, spending extra
inference computation on candidate selection and adaptive allocation to
improve control. Such gains are credible only when candidates carry
exploitable signal, nominal compute savings become real speedup, and
evaluation stays reliable. We audit all three on Cosmos Policy and
RoboCasa with a premise-first, pre-registered methodology. On clean data,
a learned value selector is 1.66 pp worse than random; a hindsight oracle
gains only 1.40 pp, with an episode-level interval spanning zero; and
82% of states show identical candidate outcomes, leaving little
selection headroom. Token pruning cuts per-forward FLOPs by up to 47% but yields at most a measured 1.15
 end-to-end speedup on the deployed path (1.25
 on a
decode-excluded path), short of a 1.5
 target, and our
adaptive-allocation experiment is inconclusive under a power gate. We
further identify and fix a silent rendering corruption whose rate rises
sharply along compute-heavy planning paths while leaving the official
direct-policy result unaffected, and release a detector, fix, and
scripts. These findings show why test-time scaling should be audited as a
chain of premises (decision signal, realized efficiency, evaluation
integrity) rather than by success rate alone. Conclusions are scoped to
the tested configuration.

World-action models (WAMs) such as Cosmos Policy 
[
14
]

adapt large pretrained video models into robot policies that jointly
generate actions, future states, and values within a shared latent
diffusion process. A central promise of this design is 
test-time
scaling
: by generating candidate actions, predicting their future
consequences, and evaluating them with a value function, WAMs can
allocate additional inference-time computation to identify promising
actions. Related peer-reviewed work has explored complementary aspects
of this problem, including model-based planning with action-conditioned
video world models 
[
27
]
 and efficient diffusion-based
robot policies through one-step distillation 
[
22
]
,
training-free adaptive caching 
[
11
]
, and rollout-adaptive
pruning and activation reuse 
[

]
.

Yet whether extra inference-time computation improves control remains
insufficiently examined. More computation helps only if it exposes
decision-relevant candidate differences, produces practical end-to-end
effects, and is evaluated reliably; success rates alone cannot distinguish
a weak selection signal, unrealized compute savings, and simulator artifacts
 
[
17
]
. We therefore audit test-time scaling as a chain
of premises rather than a single performance number: is useful signal
present, do nominal savings translate into end-to-end gains, and does the
evaluation path remain reliable under added computation?

We instantiate this audit on Cosmos Policy and RoboCasa 
[
20
]
.
Rather than proposing a planner, we combine paired candidate comparisons
and a deployment-conditioned hindsight oracle, pre-registered decision
criteria and a power gate, complete-query latency measurements, and a
framebuffer-sentinel integrity check.

The audit yields three findings.

(1) Candidate selection is limited by the available
signal, not just by the selector.

On render-verified clean data, the deployable value selector performs

 pp worse than random, and even a hindsight oracle (an upper bound)
gains only 
 pp with an interval including zero, because in 
 of
query states all candidates share an outcome. This does not show selection
is generally ineffective; it shows selector quality must be read alongside
the separability of the candidates it receives.

(2) Nominal compute reduction is not a reliable proxy
for practical acceleration.

Directly timing the complete query, token pruning yields at most a
measured 
 end-to-end speedup on the deployed path
(
 with future-image decode excluded), below the pre-specified

 target, because the gain is bounded by the irreducible
autoencoder encode and decode. A separate joint-allocation diagnostic
falls below the measurement’s noise resolution and did not clear its power
gate, so it is inconclusive rather than evidence of no effect.

(3) Evaluation integrity can depend on the inference
path being evaluated.

We identify and fix a silent rendering corruption in the
RoboCasa/
robosuite
 stack whose frequency is small under the
official direct-policy protocol but rises sharply in the compute-intensive
planning path. It is more than a generic simulator bug: changing
inference-time computation changes exposure to a measurement failure, so
the compute-intensive paths that realize test-time scaling are the most
exposed.

Together, these findings make the contribution a testable audit
methodology and a configuration-specific case study, not a claim that
test-time scaling fails for all WAMs.

3 
A Diagnostic Methodology for Test-Time Compute

Because extra inference compute can change outputs without improving
control, success-rate movements alone are not evidence of scaling gains.
Our pre-registered, falsification-first audit tests for usable candidate
signal and reliable evaluation before interpreting success
(Section 
6
).

3.1 
Verify the Premise Before Measuring the Gain

Before evaluating an inference-time mechanism, we test whether its
required signal exists. For best-of-
 planning, candidate outcomes must
differ in ways a selector can recover; we test this directly in
Section 
4
, rather than infer it from downstream success.
Supplement Figure 
5
 summarizes the audit gates.

3.2 
Causal Ablation and an Oracle Headroom Estimate

For each query state, we replay all 
 candidates under a fixed
continuation policy and let a hindsight oracle select a successful one.
This estimates outcome-level selection headroom in the current candidate
pool; because it uses unavailable outcome information, it is not deployable.
We also probe input importance by leave-one-out patch masking, trusting the
probe only if irrelevant background ranks below relevant end-effector and
contact regions in a pre-registered sanity check.

3.3 
Pre-Registration and Statistical Power Control

For the selection audit, we fix decision thresholds and the
replicate-consistency gate before inspecting the corresponding data and
report the gate’s verdict even when it fails. A power gate avoids launching
experiments unlikely to yield a verdict: from a smoke sample, we estimate
the paired-residual standard deviation 
 and project the
two-sided confidence-interval half-width at the planned sample size,

(1)

launching the full run only if 
,
the separation between the pre-registered outcomes; otherwise the run is not
launched. Every reported
comparison states its bootstrap resampling unit and interval, so that an
effect whose significance depends on that choice is presented as such
rather than as a settled result. Closed-loop success is measured by
snapshot replay, in which we restore a state, execute a candidate action
chunk, and continue under a fixed dense base policy, rather than
inferred from a single-step action deviation.

3.4 
Scope

Our empirical findings are limited to Cosmos Policy on RoboCasa.
The audit components—premise checks, oracle headroom, a power gate, and
render-integrity verification—can also inform audits of other diffusion
control policies. We release a framebuffer-sentinel detector, a fix for
the identified OpenGL-context bug, and reproduction scripts; the detector
principle may transfer across backends, but this fix is stack-specific.

5.3 
Implications

Efficiency claims should pair FLOPs with complete-query latency, and
allocation claims should be judged against a preregistered detection limit.

ineffective in general.

6 
Evaluation Reliability: A Silent Rendering Corruption

We identify a third threat to test-time-scaling evaluation: silent,
compute-dependent rendering corruption in the RoboCasa/
robosuite

stack, to which compute-intensive inference paths are more exposed.

6.1 
Mechanism

The corruption originates in OpenGL context management during
environment resets. On a hard reset the stack constructs a new
simulation instance while an orphaned instance remains eligible for
garbage collection; when the orphan is later collected, its GL context
is freed while a different context is active, silently invalidating the
framebuffer of the live environment. Subsequent renders return corrupted
observations, frozen or noise-like frames, with no error raised. Figure 
 contrasts a corrupted frame with the correct render of the same state. We confirm the mechanism with a framebuffer sentinel that pre-fills each
render target with a marker and flags targets left substantially
untouched after rendering.

7 
Discussion

Our three audits are independent, but they point in the same direction:
on the tested Cosmos Policy configuration, the computation added at
inference time to realize test-time scaling does not buy what it is
assumed to buy, and part of the apparent benefit reported in this
setting can arise from evaluation artifacts rather than from the method.

What the findings do and do not say.
 None of the three
results claims that planning, adaptive allocation, or test-time scaling is
useless in general; each is stated at the scope of the tested
configuration. The planning result is that the deployable selector is
worse than random and that selection headroom is small, not that no
selector can ever help. The allocation result is that nominal FLOPs
savings do not convert to end-to-end speedup here, together with a
detectability statement from a smoke-scale sample whose preregistered
power gate was not cleared, not a confirmed null. The reliability result
is that a real, compute-dependent corruption exists in the official stack
while leaving the reported direct-policy number essentially intact.

A methodology, not only a set of negative results.
 The value
of a negative audit depends on whether its measurements can be trusted,
which is why the diagnostic methodology is our primary contribution.
Premise-first verification keeps us from building on a signal that does
not exist; the oracle bounds the achievable gain before any selector is
tuned; the power gate stops an experiment that cannot yield a verdict
before it consumes compute; and render-integrity checking protects every
closed-loop number. These components are model-agnostic and transfer to
any audit of test-time compute in a diffusion-based control policy, even
though the specific render fix is tied to the stack we study. We release
the detector, fix, scripts, and preregistered protocols so the audits can
be repeated. Our strongest limitation is scope: whether these findings
generalize to other models, simulators, or real hardware is an open
question the methodology is designed to answer rather than presuppose, and
we see the audits not as a verdict against inference-time computation but
as checks that should precede any claim that test-time scaling improves
control.

8 
Conclusion

We presented an empirical audit of test-time scaling in a representative
world-action model, Cosmos Policy on RoboCasa, built around a
premise-first, pre-registered methodology with explicit statistical
power control and render-integrity verification. The audit yields three
independent reasons an apparent gain may not be real: best-of-

candidate selection carries little usable signal and its deployable
selector is worse than random; nominal compute savings from token-level
allocation do not convert to practical speedup, and the joint allocation
effect lies below the system’s noise resolution; and a silent,
compute-dependent rendering corruption inflates the reliability risk of
exactly the compute-intensive inference paths that test-time scaling
relies on, while leaving the official direct-policy benchmark intact. We
release the methodology and tools so that these checks can be applied
before performance gains are attributed to test-time scaling rather than
to unverified assumptions or evaluation artifacts.

The Premise-First Audit Procedure

Figure 5
: 
The premise-first methodology as a chain of gates. Each candidate
test-time-scaling gain passes three checks in order before it is credited:
verify that the decision signal exists (causal ablation and oracle ceiling); a
statistical power gate that halts a run which cannot resolve the pre-registered
outcome; and closed-loop measurement under framebuffer-sentinel render-integrity
checking. A “no” at the first gate reports a negative result and at the second
an inconclusive one, separating a genuine negative from an underpowered or
invalid measurement.

Figure 
5
 shows the audit as a single procedure: three gates
applied in order before any test-time-scaling gain is credited. Each gate
corresponds to one of the three ways an apparent gain can fail, and to one of
the paper’s three audits.

The first gate verifies the premise that the added computation exposes a usable
decision signal, using the causal ablation and oracle-ceiling construction of
Section 
6
; when no signal exists the result is reported as negative rather than
pursued further, as in the planning audit of Section 
4
. The second gate is
statistical: from a smoke sample it projects the confidence-interval width at the
full sample size and halts the run when the pre-registered outcome cannot be
resolved, so compute is not spent on an experiment that cannot yield a verdict,
and the outcome is reported as inconclusive rather than as evidence of absence,
as in the allocation audit of Section 
5
. The third gate measures closed-loop
success under the framebuffer-sentinel render check and routes the verdict by
pre-registered thresholds, the render-integrity guard whose necessity the
reliability audit of Section 
6
 establishes.

The value of the procedure is in its two “no” branches: they separate a
genuine negative from an underpowered or invalid measurement, a distinction that
success rate alone cannot make. The gates are model-agnostic and apply to any
audit of test-time compute in a diffusion-based control policy; only the render
fix itself is specific to the stack we study.

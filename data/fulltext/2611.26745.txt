Abstract

Robotic manipulation often requires remembering visual information that is no longer observable, such as the original location of an object.
Existing approaches typically rely on full observation histories, which become redundant over long horizons, or text summaries, which may omit precise visual details.
We study 
visual moment memory
, the ability to retain such details from key moments for subsequent manipulation.
To support this study, we introduce VMBench, a suite of long-horizon manipulation tasks requiring memory of spatial locations, visual appearances, and their associations.
We further propose MomentMem, a hierarchical architecture in which a VLM-based memorizer selects keyframes and an executor predicts actions conditioned on the selected memories.
This separation enables us to evaluate memory selection alongside closed-loop task success and to study memorizer generalization independently of the executor.
On VMBench, MomentMem achieves 49.9% average success, outperforming the strongest evaluated baseline by 
31.7 percentage points
.
Moreover, we find that the memorizer 
transfers well
 to unseen appearances zero-shot. It can also adapt to an unseen task with only a few memory annotations, reaching 43.7% success, 
close to
 the 44.3% obtained with full memory supervision.
Further experiments show competitive performance on RMBench and improvements in real-robot manipulation. These results highlight the value of retaining sparse visual evidence for memory-dependent manipulation.
Our code is available at 
https://github.com/PKU-VaLuE-Lab/MomentMem
.

Introduction

Recently, memory has increasingly become a key capability of multi-modal models 
[
30
, 
18
, 
41
, 
37
]
 and embodied models 
[
28
, 
19
, 
31
,

]
. Despite advances in embodied models 
[
21
, 
4
, 
12
]
, memory for robot manipulation remains challenging. As shown in Figure 
(a), existing approaches often focus on full observation histories 
[
35
]
 or text descriptions 
[
5
, 
34
]
.
Full observation histories are complete yet redundant, thus falling short when facing long-horizon tasks 
[
38
, 
37
]
.
Text captures abstract events, yet can omit visual details, or cannot fully describe certain information (e.g., “move the cube back to where it was”).
To this end, we study 
visual moment memory
 for robotic manipulation. It requires models to remember precise visual information at a key moment, such as texture patterns, spatial relations, and spatial locations. This type of memory is difficult to preserve in text.

As a first step, we propose VMBench, a benchmark of long-horizon manipulation tasks that require visual moment memory.
Its key design principle is to make successful manipulation depend on remembering precise visual information.
Different from existing benchmarks 
[
7
, 
10
, 
5
, 
22
]
, our benchmark emphasizes tasks that cannot be well addressed by concise text memory.
Specifically, these tasks require remembering exact positions and visual patterns, rather than only object identities or task progress.
Concise text summaries may omit these details, as illustrated in Figure 
(b).
We organize the tasks around spatial locations, visual appearances, and appearance-location bindings.
For example, returning a cube to its original position requires remembering exactly where it was, rather than just knowing that it was moved.
Across these tasks, the key visual moment is only visible across a short period of time and the model has to capture such moments for successful task completion.
Together, these tasks provide a testbed for studying how robots memorize precise visual information for manipulation tasks.

Going further, we consider the modeling of visual moment memory for robot manipulation.
Task success alone does not reveal whether a failure comes from memory or execution 
[
32
]
.
Beyond overall task performance, we seek to examine two key capabilities: memory selection and action prediction.
To this end, we propose MomentMem, a hierarchical architecture that separates memory from action prediction.
This separation allows us to examine memory selection and action execution independently.
As shown in Figure 
(c) (top), a VLM memorizer selects keyframes, and then the executor predicts actions.
We observe that the key event often spans across several frames that show the same visual cue.
We annotate this span as a temporal segment.
We aim for the memorizer to select a keyframe in the segment as early as possible.
Also, if it misses an earlier frame, it should still try to capture the later frame in the same segment.
During inference, the executor predicts actions from the selected keyframes together with the current observation.
Details are provided in Section 
4
.

With our benchmark tasks and hierarchical model, we design metrics and protocols to evaluate end-to-end success rate and the generalization of memory capability, as shown in Figure 
(c).
As in most existing benchmarks, we test the models on our benchmark tasks and measure the success rate over closed-loop rollouts.
We evaluate how well the memorizer captures the relevant evidence.
We also test its transfer to unseen appearances and adaptation to new tasks.
As shown in Figure 
(c) (bottom), we vary the visual appearance and directly test the overall model, with the memorizer trained only on the original visual appearances.
Besides, we test the overall model for unseen tasks, allowing the memorizer to adapt to new tasks with a few memory annotations.
In both protocols, we train the executor on target-domain action demonstrations using memories predicted by the memorizer.
In other words, our protocols measure the generalization of the memorizer rather than the executor.

On VMBench, MomentMem achieves 
49.9% average success
, exceeding the strongest evaluated baseline by 
31.7 percentage points
.
In addition, the memorizer transfers 
zero-shot
 to unseen appearances.
With only 
a few memory annotations
, it adapts to an unseen task.
The resulting policy reaches 
43.7% success
, close to 44.3% with full memory supervision.
Beyond VMBench, MomentMem remains competitive on RMBench and improves task success in real-robot manipulation.
These results demonstrate the effectiveness and generalization of MomentMem.

Our contributions are as follows:

•

We identify visual moment memory as a key research problem for robotic manipulation, focusing on remembering transient, precise visual information.

•

We introduce VMBench to evaluate and study this problem in closed-loop manipulation tasks. We also propose MomentMem, a hierarchical model that supports separate diagnosis of memory and action prediction.

•

Extensive experiments show that MomentMem exceeds the strongest baseline on our VMBench by 31.7 percentage points. Our analysis demonstrates that the memorizer can transfer to unseen appearance and task. Further, MomentMem performs well on RMBench and real-robot tasks.

VMBench: Evaluating Visual Moment Memory

We introduce VMBench that necessitates visual moment memory during robot manipulation.
It contains nine closed-loop manipulation tasks, requiring the model to recall precise visual details which text summaries are hard to describe.
This evidence is briefly presented by the environment or revealed through interaction.
In some tasks, the environment presents the evidence before execution.
In others, the robot must act to reveal it, such as opening drawers or scanning with a detector.
These settings test memory during both observation and active manipulation.

3.1 
Types of Visual Evidence

We categorize the tasks by their visual information requirements. Table 
1
 provides an overview.

Spatial locations.

These tasks require recalling precise spatial locations.
The policy must retain a spatial reference for later movement or search.
For example, Flash Beacon requires the robot to place the cube where the beacon appeared.

Visual appearance.

These tasks require retaining detailed visual patterns.
The policy must compare appearances observed at different times, even when object categories alone cannot distinguish them.
For example, Scroll Discovery requires the robot to retract scrolls, remembering the revealed patterns, and then stack the two scrolls with matching patterns after they roll back up.

Appearance-location bindings.

These tasks require retaining associations between appearances and locations.
Remembering the appearances or locations separately is insufficient; the policy must preserve their correspondence.
For example, in Mine Sweeper, the robot scans the “minefield” with a detector.
The detector briefly changes color at each target location. After scanning, the robot places colored blocks at these locations, so it must remember where each color appeared.

3.2 
Demonstrations and Memory Annotations

We collect 100 successful demonstrations per task.
Each demonstration contains annotated key segments.
We obtain these segments from simulator events and task state.
Each segment marks when one piece of task-relevant evidence is visible.
It allows multiple valid frames rather than prescribing a single representative.
The annotations support both training and evaluation of keyframe selection.

VMBench is built on SAPIEN 
[
36
]
 and ManiSkill3 
[
24
, 
17
, 
33
]
. We collect demonstrations using a Franka Panda manipulator and scripted closed-loop experts. Trajectories record RGB images from overview and wrist cameras at 
 resolution, with a control frequency of 20 Hz. Privileged state is not included in policy observations. Appendix 
C
 details data collection, randomization, and annotation.

3.3 
Visual OOD Setting

To test memory transfer across visual appearances, we provide a visual out-of-distribution (OOD) variant of each task.
We change room and table appearance, lighting, and distractors outside the manipulation area.
Task mechanics, memory events, and success conditions remain unchanged.

This setting tests whether a memorizer trained on standard appearances can directly generate useful memories under unseen appearances. The executor is trained on target-domain action demonstrations.

3.4 
Memory Evaluation

We evaluate complete policies by closed-loop task success.
For methods that explicitly select keyframes, the interval annotations also support a separate evaluation of memory selection.
We replay recorded demonstrations, using each model’s previous selections as its memory context.
Segment hit rate measures the fraction of intervals with at least one selected frame.
Segment accuracy requires exactly one selection per interval.
Non-keyframe accuracy measures the fraction of demonstrations with no selections outside the annotated intervals.
These metrics distinguish missed evidence, duplicate selections, and selections outside the intervals.
Appendix 
E
 gives the details and results.

4 
MomentMem

4.1 
Overview

As shown in Figure 
2
, MomentMem uses a hierarchical architecture that decouples memory from action execution. A VLM memorizer processes streaming visual observations and selects keyframes. A VLA executor uses these keyframes and the current observation to predict actions.

This design addresses two challenges of visual moment memory.
We use sparse keyframes as the interface between memory and execution.
Keyframes preserve raw images to retain visual details needed for later actions.
The memorizer uses recent observations and existing memory to decide when to save a frame. It captures transient evidence while avoiding duplicate storage.

Online interaction.
 At time 
, the robot receives an observation 
, a proprioceptive state 
, and a task instruction 
. The memorizer 
 decides whether to store the current frame using the existing memory 
, a buffer 
 of recent subsampled observations including 
, and the instruction. The executor 
 predicts an action chunk 
 from the updated memory 
 and the current input:

(1)

The executor receives the selected keyframes in temporal order rather than the full observation history.

Training pipeline.
 We first train the memorizer with key segment annotations. We then freeze it to generate memories for action demonstrations. The executor takes the memorized keyframes as input and learns to predict the demonstration actions. The details are elaborated in the following subsections.

4.2 
The Memorizer of MomentMem

We fine-tune a VLM to output “Yes” or “No” given the context in Equation 
1
, indicating whether to store the current frame. Training must allow multiple valid frames for each event and account for how earlier selections affect the current decision.

Segment-level supervision.

Visual evidence often remains visible across several frames.
Labeling only one fixed frame as correct would penalize other valid selections.
Our supervision instead allows different representatives of the same evidence across training epochs.
For each piece of visual evidence, we annotate an interval 
 during which it is visible.
Each epoch, we select one representative frame, called an anchor, from each interval.
The memorizer is trained to identify the anchors and reject others.
We generate intervals automatically in simulation, and annotate them manually for real-world experiments.

Anchor sampling.
 At the start of each epoch, we sample one anchor 
 per interval. Under a teacher-forced history, the anchor receives a positive label and enters memory. Earlier frames in the same interval are excluded from the loss but remain in the recent observation context. Later frames and frames outside all intervals receive negative labels.
Resampling across epochs teaches the model to select different valid representatives of the same event.
The memorizer is encouraged to capture visual evidence when it appears. If it misses an opportunity, it can select a later valid frame.
We use focal loss to address the imbalance between positive and negative samples.

Scheduled sampling.

Whether a frame should be stored depends on what is already in memory.
The same evidence may need to be saved after a missed selection, but becomes redundant once a representative is stored.
Missed or extra selections during execution therefore change later inputs and decisions.
Training only with teacher-forced histories does not expose the memorizer to these cases.
We use sequence-level scheduled sampling to train with histories that include model predictions.
At the start of each epoch, the current model replays the training trajectories. We then mix teacher decisions and model predictions to construct memory histories. The probability of using model predictions increases during training. Supervision follows the history provided: after a missed anchor, later valid frames in the same interval remain positive. Once a representative is stored, subsequent frames become negative. Appendix 
D
 details label construction.

4.3 
The Executor of MomentMem

The executor learns to act on the memories that the memorizer actually provides.
We therefore freeze the trained memorizer and replay action demonstrations in temporal order.
Its selections form the memory context for executor training.
The memorizer may select frames at different times, miss relevant evidence, or store extra frames.
Training with these predicted memories exposes the executor to the memorizer’s outputs rather than only to frames sampled from annotated intervals.

For a training sample at time 
, the executor receives predicted keyframes from before 
, the current observation 
, the state 
, and the instruction 
. We supervise it with the demonstration action chunk using the original learning objective of the action policy.

This interface supports separate adaptation of memory and execution. In the visual OOD experiments, we reuse the memorizer directly. For a new task, we first adapt it with a few memory annotations. In both settings, the executor is trained on target-domain action demonstrations. So, this hierarchical design allows us to evaluate the two modules separately.

5 
Experiments and Results

We first evaluate overall task success on VMBench. We then test the memorizer’s transfer to unseen appearances and adaptation to a new task. We find that MomentMem improves closed-loop success on tasks that require visual moment memory. The memorizer also generalizes to unseen appearance and task to some extent. Beyond VMBench, we test MomentMem on RMBench and real-robot tasks. The results suggest that MomentMem is broadly applicable to memory-dependent manipulation.

As shown in Table

The largest gains occur on Placement Imitation, Mine Sweeper, and Seal Checking, which require retaining precise visual evidence after it disappears from view.

Memory-free execution struggles on these tasks.

 reaches only 7.0% average success, and scores 0% on four of the nine tasks.
On Mine Sweeper, we observe that 
 often places a block inside the candidate region, but not at the location specified by the earlier visual cue.

Texture matching remains difficult for the executor.

Linear probes of the executor’s final-layer features predict “what the texture is” with 99% accuracy, but predict the matching box with only 40% accuracy, just slightly higher than the 33% level of random guessing.
The features retain information about each texture, but do not capture well which textures match.

These results suggest that the executor struggles to match textures across memory images, even when the relevant evidence is available.

The Memorizer Adapts to an Unseen Task with Few Annotations

We test whether the memorizer can adapt to Flash Beacon after training on the other eight tasks.
We adapt the memorizer with five memory-annotated Flash Beacon demonstrations.
We then use it to generate memories for all 100 target demonstrations and train the executor.

Figure 3
: 

Few-shot memorizer transfer on VMBench.

After training on the other VMBench tasks, the memorizer adapts to Flash Beacon from five annotated demonstrations. It reaches 43.7% closed-loop success. This is close to 44.3% with full memory supervision and above 29.0% when adapting pretrained Qwen3-VL with the same data.

For comparison, the “From Qwen” group adapts directly from pretrained Qwen3-VL using the same five memory-annotated demonstrations, testing whether Qwen’s prior knowledge alone explains the result.
The “Full Memory Supervision” group trains on Flash Beacon with memory annotations for all of the 100 demonstrations, serving as a positive control.
All settings use the same 100 action demonstrations and differ only in memory supervision.

5.6 
Ablation Studies

Segment supervision allows multiple valid frames per event.

We test whether the memorizer benefits from learning to capture an event segment rather than one exact frame.
We replace each annotated segment with a single positive frame, following the point annotation used by prior work.
As shown in Table 
6
, this change reduces average success by 43.0 percentage points.
Point supervision treats neighboring frames as negatives simply because they are not the selected anchor frame.
However, nearby frames may show the same evidence.
Labeling them as negative penalizes valid selections. Segment supervision avoids this problem.

Scheduled sampling improves success on multi-event tasks.

We test whether the memorizer needs to learn from its own imperfect memory histories.
We remove scheduled sampling and train it only with teacher-forced memory histories.
As shown in Table 
6
, average success drops by 30.9 percentage points.
An early mistake changes the memory context used for every later decision.
Training with the memorizer’s own predictions helps it handle earlier selection errors, especially in tasks with multiple memory events.

5.7 
Discussion on Memory Capability

The memorizer can capture most key segments.

Our method adopts a hierarchical architecture. This design lets us evaluate memory selection separately from execution.
We use the simulator to verify whether the memorizer selects a frame in the key moment segment.
On the validation set, it captures 95.8% of key segments, while it avoids selecting a frame outside the key moment segment in 84.4% of demonstrations.
These results validate the training of our memorizer and show how memory selection can be evaluated directly.
Appendix 
E
 gives the full setup and per-task results.

Our memorizer works better than a stronger frozen VLM.

Our memorizer is trained with the key segment annotations from humans or the simulator.
A natural question is whether a stronger VLM can select keyframes without post-training, as in BPP 
[
29
]
.
To test this hypothesis, we follow BPP and exploit a frozen GPT-6-Astra to identify when a key event occurs.
However, querying such a VLM is costly, and thus we do not run the closed-loop evaluation.
Instead, we compare the keyframe selection on the same validation split using the same memory metrics.
It turns out that GPT-6-Astra captures only 69.5% of key segments. The results demonstrate that our memorizer works clearly better than the stronger frozen VLM, which we attribute to memory training and annotations.
Therefore, general visual understanding is not strong enough for memory capability and memory modeling deserves more attention from the community.

6 
Conclusion

We study visual moment memory, which requires robots to retain precise visual details for later execution.
We introduce VMBench, whose tasks require remembering briefly visible locations, appearances, and their associations.
We propose MomentMem, which decouples memory from execution: a VLM memorizer stores sparse keyframes as raw images, and an executor predicts actions from them.
MomentMem improves task success on VMBench and a real robot, and remains competitive on RMBench.
Its memorizer transfers zero-shot to unseen appearances and adapts to a held-out task with a few memory annotations.
Future work can investigate whether broader memory training enables transfer to new tasks without memory annotations.

Limitations.

MomentMem runs a VLM memorizer, which adds inference cost compared with a single-model policy.
On RMBench, MomentMem improves average success by 1.9 percentage points over the strongest evaluated baseline, but gains vary across tasks.
The executor still struggles to use retained texture information for target selection, even when the relevant evidence is available (Appendix 
F.1
).
VMBench also uses a single embodiment and a fixed camera configuration, so the effect of viewpoint changes remains untested.

Appendix B 
Task Instructions

The following boxes list the task instructions for the nine tasks in VMBench. The instructions are reproduced verbatim from the environment definitions and describe the task procedure and objective. The instance-specific patterns, colors, and target locations must be inferred from visual observations. The memorizer and executor receive the task instruction. The separate memory-instruction field stored in the dataset is not supplied to the memorizer in our experiments.

Spring Compression

A white cube sits on a long horizontal rail connected to a spring. Before the robot moves, the cube slides to a compressed position, pauses, and returns to its starting position. As soon as it returns, close the gripper and push the cube at a steady speed to the same compressed position, then lift the closed gripper while the cube stays there.

Flash Beacon

A white cube and two beacons are on the table. Lift the cube. When the first beacon appears, start moving toward it at a moderate speed so that it disappears before you arrive. Keep moving without returning the arm to its starting pose, and place the cube at that spot. The second beacon appears as soon as the cube touches the table. Keep holding the cube and continue directly toward the second beacon, then place and release the cube at that spot.

Explore Cube

Three identical opaque cups are spread across the table, and one cup hides a green cube. Inspect all three cups in any order. Each time, lift a cup high enough to see under it, then put it back upside down in the same place before checking the next. Once all three cups have been checked, move the cup hiding the cube aside, then grasp and lift the cube. Leave the other cups in place and leave every cup upside down on the table.

Hidden Pattern

Three boxes open together to show three different patterns, then close. A target card appears briefly with one of those patterns and then disappears. Then push forward the box that showed the same pattern as the target card.

Drawer Match

A three-level cabinet hides a pattern in each drawer. Press the upper, middle, and bottom drawer buttons in that order; each drawer opens once and then closes. Then press the upper or middle button for the drawer whose pattern matches the bottom drawer. Finish when the selected drawer is fully open.

Scroll Discovery

Three narrow scrolls hide patterned strips. From left to right, pull each strip until the whole pattern is visible, then release it so it rolls back up. After the third strip rolls up, immediately grasp one of the two scrolls that showed the same pattern and stack it on top of the other, without touching the scroll with the different pattern.

Placement Imitation

Two cubes of different colors start at separate positions on the table. One at a time, each cube moves to an unmarked target position, pauses, and returns. Once both demonstrations are complete, place each cube at the position where it paused, then release it on the table.

Mine Sweeper

Two parallel black lines, a white probe, and two cubes of different colors are on the table. Move the probe directly to one end of the nearer line and scan once to the other end. Move directly to the farther line and scan it once in the opposite direction. The probe briefly changes color at one point on each line. Then put the probe back, return the arm to its starting pose, and place each cube where the probe showed the same color. Release each cube on the table.

Seal Checking

Two white stamps cover two colored spots. A tray and two cubes of different colors are also on the table. Lift each stamp by its square handle, place it fully inside the tray, and watch the spot underneath fade away. After both stamps have been checked, return the arm to its starting pose. Then place each cube where the spot showed the same color and release it on the table.

Appendix C 
Dataset Construction

C.1 
Simulation and Demonstration Collection

We construct VMBench in ManiSkill3 with SAPIEN physics, using a Panda manipulator, an overview camera, and a wrist camera. The data-generation pipeline records both RGB streams at 
 resolution and a control frequency of 20 Hz. Each task implements a scripted, closed-loop expert that computes actions from the simulator state and executes them through the robot controller. Demonstrations therefore contain the actions actually applied to the environment and the resulting observations.

Each rollout starts from a seeded initialization, with the scene rebuilt at reset. Task-specific randomization varies object positions, target locations, and the associations between visual cues and their required responses. For example, Hidden Pattern samples distinct patterns and assigns them to boxes. Drawer Match and Scroll Discovery randomize the matching patterns. Placement Imitation, Mine Sweeper, and Seal Checking randomize color–location associations. Color pairs are sampled without replacement from a palette of eight colors, excluding pairs that are too similar under the implemented color-distance criterion. Spatial sampling also enforces task-specific separation and reachability constraints. These variations produce different memory contents while retaining the same task instruction.

The expert rolls out until success, failure, termination, or the step limit. Only successful episodes are retained. Failed attempts are discarded, and generation continues with new seeds. For tasks that expose a separate evaluation-success flag, a retained demonstration must satisfy that flag as well as the expert’s success condition. Our main experiment uses 100 demonstrations per task, giving 900 demonstrations across the nine tasks.

C.2 
Automatic Memory Annotations

Each environment records the key segments during which task-relevant evidence is available, using simulator events and task state. Each segment is stored as an inclusive pair of frame indices 
 at the original recording rate. The metadata also records the event identity, the information to retain, and the corresponding timestamps. These segments mark valid ranges rather than fixed representative frames.

Each demonstration has one to three annotated segments, as summarized in Table 
7
. For Seal Checking, each segment ends before the revealed color becomes too faint to identify. Privileged event metadata supplies supervision for memory selection and is not included in the memorizer’s or executor’s observation input.

C.3 
Observation and Action Alignment

Raw trajectories are saved in HDF5, with accompanying JSON metadata containing the episode seed, task instruction, phase intervals, and memory annotations. A rollout with 
 actions contains observations from 
 through 
. Conversion creates 
 training records, pairing each action 
 with its preceding observation 
 and timestamp. The terminal observation is not assigned an additional action. Both camera streams and the key segment boundaries use this frame indexing.

Hidden Pattern, Spring Compression, and Placement Imitation begin with an observation phase in which the environment presents a visual demonstration without requesting policy actions. These observations remain available for memory selection. For MomentMem, the recorded stationary actions during these observation phases are excluded from action supervision, and the executor is trained only on the execution phase. The remaining tasks require robot actions to acquire the evidence, so their recorded actions remain eligible for supervision throughout the trajectory.

Our EventVLA implementation instead retains supervision on these stationary actions, because its keyframe predictions are derived from action hidden states. It therefore learns these actions even though the environment does not request them during observation.

C.4 
Training-Data Conversion

We convert the recordings into LeRobot-format datasets with per-episode videos, tabular state–action records, task metadata, and a separate file of key-segment annotations. The converter checks camera streams, temporal alignment, and segment bounds, and rejects missing or malformed segments. It reconstructs the controller’s commanded targets from recorded simulator states to express end-effector actions in the overview-camera coordinate system. For the executor used in this paper, actions comprise relative end-effector translation, relative rotation represented in Rot6D, and a gripper command. Dataset conversion preserves the annotated key segments. Anchor sampling is performed during training, as described in Section 
4
.

C.5 
Visual OOD Data

Visual OOD environments use the same task implementations and expert procedures, with altered room and table appearance, lighting, and additional background or tabletop distractors outside the manipulation area. Appearance randomization uses a separate seed-derived random-number stream, preserving the task randomization for a given seed. Task mechanics, memory events, and success conditions are shared with the standard setting, and OOD trajectories pass through the same recording and conversion pipeline. In the appearance-transfer experiment, target-domain executor training uses keyframes predicted by the transferred memorizer rather than the simulator’s target-domain memory annotations.

The anchor labels described in Section

 and Figure 
2
(b) assume a teacher-forced history in which the anchor is stored. Scheduled sampling instead requires labels to reflect the memory history shown to the memorizer. Within a key segment, frames before the sampled anchor are masked from the loss until a representative is stored. From the anchor onward, the target is Yes until the visible history contains a representative of that segment. It then becomes No. Frames outside all segments receive No. For example, if the memorizer misses an anchor, a later frame in the same segment remains a positive example. This history-dependent supervision allows recovery from missed selections.

During training, the executor retains only keyframes selected by the trained memorizer before the current frame and masks unused memory slots. On VMBench, actions follow the representation in Appendix

For executor training, each frame selected offline by the memorizer is stored as a single-frame key segment. The executor training pipeline then uses these predicted selections in the same way as annotated segments. We use this procedure in both the main and transfer experiments. The images, actions, and task instructions in the data are not modified.

Appendix E 
Keyframe Selection Quality

When a task fails, the outcome alone cannot tell us whether the memorizer missed a visual cue.
We check which cues it captures, how often it saves the same cue more than once, and whether it saves unrelated frames.

On VMBench, the 20% validation split contains 20 demonstrations per task, or 180 in total. This is the checkpoint-selection set, not an independent test set. Evaluation replays each demonstration autoregressively, so the memorizer’s previous decisions determine its later memory context.

Segment hit rate measures the fraction of key segments containing at least one selected frame. Segment accuracy requires exactly one selection per segment. To measure selections outside the key segments, we also report the fraction of demonstrations with no such selection, termed non-keyframe accuracy. Any such selection stays in memory for the rest of the demonstration, so this metric reflects how often the memory remains free of irrelevant frames.

Appendix H 
Few-Shot Cross-Task Memory Transfer

We ask whether training on other tasks helps the memorizer learn what to save in a new task with few annotations.
Flash Beacon is the target task and is excluded from the eight source tasks.
The target task provides 100 action demonstrations, but only five provide memory annotations in the few-shot settings.
We compare source-to-target adaptation with two controls, full target memory supervision and target-only adaptation from the pretrained backbone.
All six variants of MomentMem and EventVLA share 300 evaluation seeds.

Training settings.

For MomentMem, source-to-target adaptation first trains the memorizer on the other eight tasks, then fine-tunes it using five annotated Flash Beacon demonstrations (Ours in Figure 
3
).
The adapted memorizer labels all 100 target demonstrations offline, including the five used for adaptation.
The executor is trained on these demonstrations using the predicted keyframes.

Full Memory Supervision is the positive control. We train the memorizer with all available target memory annotations, then freeze it to generate keyframes for the target demonstrations. The executor is trained on these predicted memories and demonstration actions, following the same procedure as the other settings.
Both components are trained only on Flash Beacon, without source-task training.
This setting gives the success rate under full memory supervision on Flash Beacon.

From Qwen is the target-only control.
It fine-tunes the memorizer from pretrained Qwen3-VL on the same five annotated demonstrations, without source-task training.
It then uses the same automatic-labeling and executor-training procedure as Ours.
This control tests whether few-shot success comes from the pretrained backbone alone or from memory selection learned on source tasks.

Appendix I 
Real-World Experiments

I.1 
Experimental Setup

We evaluate MomentMem, 
, and EventVLA on a Franka Panda robot in two tasks adapted from VMBench. We keep their simulation names, Placement Imitation and Explore Cube. All methods use only a single overview camera, with no wrist camera. We collect 51 and 54 demonstrations, respectively, and manually annotate their key segments because privileged simulator information is unavailable in real-world experiments. Each method is evaluated over 30 trials per task.

In Placement Imitation, seven cards are scattered across the tabletop. A human first places a cube on one card, then moves it in front of the robot. The robot must remember this location and return the cube to the correct card within 60 seconds.

In Explore Cube, a cube is hidden under one of three inverted cups. The robot must inspect the cups, locate the cube, and grasp and lift it within 120 seconds. Revisiting a previously inspected cup or knocking over any cup counts as a failure.

For both tasks, a force-triggered protective stop also counts as a failure. We stop the robot when the Euclidean norm of the three external-force components at the end effector, estimated by the Franka controller, exceeds 100 N. This can occur when the robot presses into the tabletop or an object.

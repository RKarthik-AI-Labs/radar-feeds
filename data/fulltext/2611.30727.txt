Abstract

Multilingual search agents can access the global Web, yet may remain trapped in a language environment with insufficient evidence; we refer to this failure as a language information cocoon.
However, existing search benchmarks largely operate within a single-language, especially English, overlooking entity connections across multiple languages.
We introduce 
CocoonBench
, a benchmark that evaluates whether multilingual search agents can escape such language information cocoons.

CocoonBench
 mines answer-centered relational paths from a frozen Wikidata-derived graph and selects clue sets that are individually necessary and jointly identify a unique answer.
Using language-specific entity names, it records each task’s minimum language cover, removes direct identifiers, and renders each task as matched prompts in three languages together with its construction certificate and provenance.
Across 10 state-of-the-art models, mean accuracy on 
CocoonBench
 ranges from 33.8% (DeepSeek-V4-Flash) to 69.7% (Claude-Opus-4.8), leaving even the strongest system below 70%.
The same construction certificates support path-aligned trajectory analysis, enabling diagnosis of model failures in language routing, evidence-path recovery, and answer use.
The result shows that escaping a language cocoon requires more than
switching languages: successful cross-lingual search depends on both
covering the correct languages, and connecting the evidence found across
them.

1 
Introduction

Web-searching agents based on large language models (LLMs) alternate between reasoning and external
actions such as querying search engines and fetching web pages 
(
Yao et al., 2023
)
. Since information is unevenly distributed on the multilingual internet, e.g. Wikipedia contents can be substantially different across language versions 
(
Bao et al., 2012
; 
Wulczyn et al., 2016
; 
Roy et al., 2020
)
, it is important for the agents to optimize query languages to maximize searching efficiency.

This creates a problem for search agents. When the evidence exposed in the current language is insufficient, an agent may continue reformulating queries in the same language, fall back to English, or switch to another language that appears relevant but does not contain the missing evidence. We call this failure to leave an insufficient language environment a 
language information cocoon
: the agent remains confined to a language-conditioned view of the Web even when solving the task requires evidence from another language environment.

Recent multilingual search-agent benchmarks vary the prompt, task context, or supporting-evidence language 
(
Zhou et al., 2025
; 
Lee et al., 2026
; 
Almeida et al., 2026
; 
Lu et al., 2026
)
. While they reveal important language-dependent failures, they do not certify for each item that the agent must recognize an insufficient language environment and gather complementary evidence across languages. Evaluating escape from a language information cocoon therefore requires making the cross-language evidence path part of task construction.

We therefore introduce 
CocoonBench
, a benchmark that makes the cross-language evidence path an explicit part of task construction. 
CocoonBench
 builds 200 cross-lingual multi-hop tasks from a frozen Wikidata-derived graph and multilingual lexical interface. Different parts of each task’s evidence are exposed through different language environments, forming 
language-conditioned information islands
. For each task, a construction certificate records the required evidence branches, their bridge entities, the unique answer, and the task’s 
minimum language cover
: the smallest set of languages needed to provide lexical access to all hidden retrieval-key entities.

Each semantic task is rendered under three parallel versions: English (
EN
), Chinese (
ZH
), and a task-specific designated language (
DL
).

EN
 serves as the common lingua franca of current language models,

ZH
 as an unrelated high-resource control language, and 
DL
 as
a task-specific language selected from one evidence branch—a language in
which an entity on that branch has a usable surface form.

DL
 can therefore provide a lexical entry point into one search chain
without necessarily covering the languages needed by the remaining branches.
All three prompts preserve the same answer and underlying construction certificate.
The resulting benchmark is intended to test whether an agent can recognize
that its current language environment is insufficient, cross into another
information island, and integrate evidence across the boundary.

The benchmark remains challenging: mean accuracy across the three prompt modes
ranges from 33.8% for DeepSeek-V4-Flash to 69.7% for Claude-Opus-4.8,
leaving even the strongest system below 70%.

The construction certificate also enables process-level failure-mode analysis.
We introduce a path-aligned failure-mode audit that separates completion of the
minimum language cover from construction of the graph evidence path.
The audit maps an incorrect trajectory to the earliest failed stage: incomplete
or incorrect language routing (L), downstream evidence-chain failure after
minimum-cover completion (T), or residual failure after strong answer evidence
(R).

The analysis yields three main findings.

First, the dominant problem is 
multilingual routing rather than language
switching itself
. Agents often leave the prompt language yet enter the
wrong language or stop after visiting only part of the required cover. Most errors occur before a valid minimum language cover is
completed, but simple source-language or English lock-in accounts for only a
small fraction of them.

Second, designated-language prompts help agents go further, but not always in
the right direction. They produce far fewer incomplete-cover errors and more
trajectories that reach downstream evidence recovery, but they also increase
wrong-language routing. A task-associated language can therefore provide a
useful lexical foothold without identifying the correct multilingual route.

Third, completing the language cover does not complete the search. Even after
reaching the required language environments, agents often fail to recover all
bridge evidence needed across the graph branches.

Our contributions are:

•

The 
CocoonBench
 benchmark.

We construct 200 cross-lingual multi-hop semantic tasks with matched

ZH
/
EN
/
DL
 prompts. Each item pairs its question
and answer with certified evidence paths, language requirements, and
provenance.

•

A verifiable generation framework.

We combine a frozen knowledge graph, multilingual lexical coverage,
branch-necessity constraints, uniqueness checks, and provenance tracking
to create tasks whose required cross-language evidence routes are

•

A path-aligned diagnosis.

The same certificates support process-level analysis that separates
failures in multilingual routing, evidence-path recovery, and answer use
after strong evidence exposure.

•

A cross-model empirical study.

Evaluation of ten models under three prompt conditions shows that the main
challenge is not simply crossing a language boundary, but finding and
completing the right multilingual route before recovering and integrating
the required evidence.

Related Work

Web search agents and multilingual search benchmarks.

Benchmarks such as GAIA 
(
Mialon et al., 2024
)
 and
BrowseComp 
(
Wei et al., 2025
)
 evaluate agents that must use external
tools and iteratively search the Web for hard-to-find information.
BrowseComp-Plus 
(
Chen et al., 2026
)
 further introduces a fixed,
verified retrieval corpus for more reproducible evaluation of deep-search
agents.
Recent work has extended this setting across languages:
BrowseComp-ZH and K-BrowseComp target Chinese and Korean Web
search 
(
Zhou et al., 2025
; 
Lee et al., 2026
)
,
MARCA compares English and Portuguese Web information
seeking 
(
Almeida et al., 2026
)
,
X-WebAgentBench 
(
Wang et al., 2025
)
 evaluates agents in multilingual
Web environments, and Cross-lingual
BrowseComp-Plus 
(
Lu et al., 2026
)
 varies or distributes supporting evidence
across languages.
These benchmarks expose multilingual search failures, but multilinguality is primarily expressed through the query, interface, or evidence language rather than through a certified cross-language search structure.

CocoonBench
 instead constructs each task around an explicit cross-language entity and evidence path, allowing matched prompt interventions and path-aligned diagnosis of search trajectories.

Constructing tasks for search agents.

Prior work constructs challenging search tasks by controlling the structure and visibility of evidence on the web. WebSailor 
(
Li et al., 2025
)
 generates questions through web-grounded
entity–relation graphs, subgraph sampling, and information obfuscation.
WebExplorer 
(
Liu et al., 2025
)
 combines model-driven web exploration
with iterative removal of salient clues and replacement of explicit references.
WebShaper 
(
Tao et al., 2025
)
 formalizes information-seeking tasks through
compositions, unions, and intersections of Knowledge Projections, using
these structures to guide task expansion. Whereas these methods primarily
control what evidence is exposed and how it is composed, 
CocoonBench
 combines
relational clues that jointly identify a unique answer with complementary
language-specific name coverage, controlling both evidence structure and the
languages through which it can be accessed.

3 
Problem Definition and CocoonBench Generation

CocoonBench
 generates cross-language search tasks from a known target entity, ensuring that the clues jointly identify a unique answer (
answer identifiability
), each clue is necessary for that identification (
clue necessity
), and recovering the hidden entities requires complementary lexical access across different languages (
cross-language lexical necessity
).
The resulting structured task is then verbalized into a natural-language question and rendered under matched language conditions.
Finally, we revalidate the task and retain the graph paths, lexical information, provenance, and answer identifiers used during construction as a 
construction certificate
.

3.2 
Question Realization

Verbalization and obfuscation.

After the graph structure is fixed, we convert it into a natural-language question while preserving the underlying facts, relation directions, answer relation, and entity roles. During verbalization, hidden entities are replaced with relational descriptions while selected anchors remain explicitly named as search entry points.
We remove direct identifiers and overly identifying details that would make the answer recoverable through a trivial lookup, while preserving the relational constraints required by the executable task.
This follows the general use of identifier obfuscation in search-task generation 
(
Li et al., 2025
; 
Liu et al., 2025
)
.
Any edit that changes a factual constraint triggers a fresh execution of the uniqueness and necessity checks.

Matched prompt conditions.

Each semantic task is rendered under three matched prompt conditions:
Chinese (
ZH
), English (
EN
), and a task-specific designated language (
DL
).

ZH
 provides an unrelated high-resource prompt condition, 
EN
 provides a common high-resource baseline, and 
DL
 is a language selected from one evidence branch—a language in which an entity on that branch has a usable surface form. The three prompts preserve the same semantic and are parallel.
The 
DL
 condition may therefore provide a useful lexical entry point into one search chain without necessarily covering the remaining branches.

3.3 
Validation and Construction Certificates

Task validation.

We validate each generated task at both the structural and factual levels.
Structural validation reruns the unique-answer and clue-necessity checks, including constraint-level deletion tests, after any modification to the task. For factual validation, we start from the reference answer and trace every claim in the final question back to its supporting evidence.
We additionally check that the retained clues remain searchable without exposing the answer, Wikidata identifiers, or other direct shortcuts.
Quality control combines these checks with the fixed-query shortcut audit and
human verbalization review detailed in Appendix 
C
.
For each candidate, the shortcut audit issues translated full-question and
branch queries through the same Serper backend used in evaluation, using
translated relation terms and transliterated public anchors. A task is
removed whenever an audited single-language query exposes the correct answer
or an equivalent alias. Every retained task therefore passes the predefined
single-language shortcut screen.
Tasks that fail any check are revised and revalidated.

Construction certificates.

For every retained task, we store the information produced during generation as a 
construction certificate
.
The certificate includes the executable MATCHes, graph paths and shared variables, lexical inventories and language covers, source provenance, bridge entities, target and answer identifiers, and exact answer Wikipedia URLs. A 
bridge entity
 is a certificate-designated entry into one evidence branch.
These records provide the observable signals used by the trajectory audit in Section 
5.1
.

Benchmark composition.

CocoonBench
 contains 200 semantic tasks.
Rendering each task under the three prompt conditions yields 600 cases per evaluated system, and the 
DL
 condition spans 33 languages; Appendix 
C.2
 reports the full language, language-pair, writing-system, cover-size, and per-language result distributions. Of the 200 tasks, 170 are constructed from two executable relational clues (MATCHes; 85%), 28 from three (14%), and two from four (1%).
The maximum clue-path depth within a task ranges from 2 to 8, with a median of 3.
Summing clue-path depths within each task and averaging across the 200 tasks gives an average total path depth of 6.36.

5 
Failure-Mode Analysis

We use each task’s construction certificate to classify incorrect search
trajectories; the following subsections define the path-aligned audit and
report its findings.

5.1 
Path-Aligned Failure-Mode Audit

Each 
CocoonBench
 item’s construction certificate records a set of languages that
must be visited, bridge entities for the required graph branches, entities
along the evidence paths, and the final answer entity. We use these annotations to locate where an
incorrect search trajectory breaks down. In particular, we distinguish failures
to reach the required language environments (L), failures to recover the
evidence path after doing so (T), and failures that occur even after strong
evidence for the answer has already appeared (R).

Observable signals.

We identify the language of each WebSearch query using
fastText 
(
Joulin et al., 2017
; 
Joulin et al., 2016
)
. For Wikipedia search results
and fetched pages, we infer the language from the Wikipedia hostname. We count a
language as 
entered
 only when the agent issues a query in that language
or fetches a page in that language. For trajectory auditing, a minimum language
cover is complete once all of its required languages have been entered. We
detect path and bridge entities in the trajectory in three ways: through an
explicit QID, a Wikipedia URL that resolves to that QID, or a normalized
multilingual surface form of the entity.

5.2 
Analysis

5.2.1 
Most Failures Come from Incomplete Multilingual Routing

Language-covering failures dominate the classifiable errors. They account for
65.7% (L), more than twice the 32.3% that occur during evidence-path recovery
after cover completion (T); 1.9% occur after strong answer evidence has
already appeared (R).

Importantly, these failures are rarely caused by the agent simply staying in
one language. Source lock and English trap (L1/L2) together account for only
5.8% of errors. Much more often, the agent does switch languages but fails to
follow the right multilingual route. In 22.2% of errors, it leaves the prompt
language but moves into the wrong language (L3). Another 37.8% reach some of
the required cover languages beyond the prompt language but stop before
completing the cover (L4), making
incomplete cover the largest individual failure type in
Figure 
4
.

The main difficulty is therefore not getting agents to switch languages at
all, but getting them to visit the full set of languages needed to recover the
relevant evidence.

5.2.2 
Designated-Language Prompts Help Agents Go Further, but Not Always in the Right Direction

The designated-language (
DL
) prompt gives a small average accuracy
gain in Figure 
3
, suggesting that starting from a language
selected from one evidence branch can provide a useful entry point for
search.

The error patterns show a similar effect. Among classifiable errors,

DL
 produces far fewer incomplete-cover cases than 
ZH
 or

EN
 (13.5% versus 51.3%/51.4%), and more of its errors reach the
later T stage (43.6% versus 22.0%/29.1%). In this sense, the designated
language often helps the agent make more progress through the search.

However, that progress is not always in the right direction. Wrong-language
routes are much more common under 
DL

(34.3% versus 16.7%/14.4%). A language selected from one evidence branch can therefore
serve as a useful foothold while also anchoring the search to a locally
plausible but ultimately incorrect route.

5.2.3 
Completing the Language Cover Does Not Complete the Search

Reaching all required languages is only the first part of the problem. Even
after a valid language cover is complete, agents can still fail to recover the
evidence needed to reach the answer.

Among all classifiable errors, 9.6% complete the language cover but never
reach the configured evidence path (T1). Another 19.3% reach the path but
cover none or only some of the required evidence branches through designated
bridge entities (T2), making incomplete bridge recovery the largest post-cover
failure mode. A further 3.5% cover every required evidence branch through a
designated bridge entity but still fail to expose strong evidence for the
answer (T3).

Only a small number of errors remain after strong answer evidence itself has
already appeared. These residual R failures account for 1.9% of all
classifiable errors. They are split almost evenly between cases where the
answer evidence appears in a fetched page (47.8%, R1) and cases where it
appears only in the search results (52.2%, R2).

Thus, simply reaching the right languages is not enough: the agent must still
follow the evidence path, recover the required bridges, and make use of the
answer evidence it encounters. Appendix 
G.3
 gives examples
of both residual exposure patterns.

5.2.4 
Different Models Break Down at Different Stages

The overall error distribution hides substantial differences across models.
Claude-Opus-4.8 and the two DeepSeek variants fail predominantly before completing a valid language cover, with language-covering failures (L) accounting for 80.7%, 73.3%, and 76.5% of their classifiable errors, respectively.
Kimi-K3 shows the opposite pattern: evidence-path recovery failures (T) form its largest error family at 54.7%, while GPT-5.5 and GPT-5.6-Sol also fail relatively late, with 40.1% and 39.0% of their errors reaching the evidence-path recovery stage.
These systems more often reach the required languages but still fail to recover a complete chain of path and bridge evidence.
Qwen3.8-Max and the three GLM variants fall between these two profiles: their errors are still dominated by language-covering failures, but 27.6%–33.7% reach the evidence-path recovery stage.
Residual failures after answer exposure (R) are rare overall, although Kimi-K3 has the largest share at 6.9%.
Together, these results show that models do not simply differ in how often they fail, but also in how far they typically progress before failing.

6 
Conclusion

CocoonBench
 certifies cross-language evidence requirements during task
construction and reuses the resulting construction certificates for both
final-answer evaluation and path-aligned trajectory diagnosis.

Across ten model–agent systems, multilingual capability did not ensure
effective multilingual search: agents often failed either to complete the
required language route or to turn language coverage into a verifiable
evidence chain.

The answer to our title question is therefore: only partially. Escaping a
language information cocoon requires agents to recognize missing coverage,
choose the next language strategically, and sustain branch-aware retrieval
until the evidence chain is complete.

Appendix A 
Discussion

Language Shapes the Agent’s Visible World.

For a search agent, language is not merely the surface form of a query.
It also shapes which entities, pages, and evidence are likely to become visible during search.
The same Web can therefore present different effective information spaces depending on the language through which it is accessed.
This perspective helps explain the language information cocoon: an agent may have access to the global Web while effectively operating within only the portion exposed by its current search language.
Multilingual search requires not only understanding information across languages, but also recognizing when the currently visible world is incomplete.

Multilinguality in the Model Does Not Guarantee Multilinguality in Search.

Modern language models can understand and generate many languages 
(
Zhu et al., 2024
; 
Zeng et al., 2025
; 
Chan et al., 2026
)
, yet this capability
does not automatically translate into multilingual search behavior.
Our results are consistent with a gap between multilingual language competence and multilingual search policy.
Agents may be able to read evidence in another language once they encounter it, while still failing to complete the minimum language cover, track which required languages have actually been queried or fetched, or maintain branch-aware search after route completion.
Among classifiable errors, 
ZH
 and 
EN
 have almost identical incomplete-cover shares (51.3% and 51.4%), so high-resource English is not exempt from the route-completion bottleneck; 
EN
 errors more often reach downstream path stages, but these conditional compositions should not be interpreted as causal effects of prompt language.
A model can therefore be multilingual at the level of representation and generation while still using languages ineffectively during search 
(
Han et al., 2025
)
.
Improving multilingual agents may require explicit route and coverage tracking together with branch-aware search control, rather than assuming that multilingual capability will transfer automatically to agentic search.

Language Barriers May Amplify Information Inequality.

These findings also have implications for access to information.
If search agents remain biased toward the language of the user’s query, users may receive different subsets of the available Web depending on the language they speak.
This matters especially for lower-resource languages, where less information may be available locally and useful evidence may more often need to be retrieved from another language environment 
(
Bao et al., 2012
; 
Wulczyn et al., 2016
; 
Roy et al., 2020
; 
Zeng et al., 2024
)
.
Users whose languages are already underrepresented on the Web could therefore be disproportionately affected by weak cross-language search.
More broadly, multilingual search quality is not only a question of whether an agent can answer in a user’s language, but also whether users across languages are given comparable access to the evidence available on the wider Web.

Appendix B 
Agent Setup

Tools and Web access.

All models use the same agent harness with four tools: 
WebSearch
, 
WebFetch
, 
Bash
, and 
ExecuteCode
. The first two let the agent search for and read Web pages, while the latter two provide a terminal session and a Python environment with a Jupyter kernel. 
WebSearch
 uses Google through the Serper API and returns 10 results per query. For 
WebFetch
, Jina parses retrieved HTML into Markdown with up to 200,000 characters in the main body. DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731 
(
DeepSeek-AI, 2026
)
 then summarizes the parsed content using the prompt in Figure 
7
; tool-result truncation controls the summary length.

Agent prompt format.

The system and user prompt templates are shown in Figures 
5
 and 
6

H
. The 
{today_date}
 placeholder in the system prompt is replaced by the evaluation date.
For each semantic task, the 
ZH
, 
EN
, and 
DL
 modes
use the corresponding fixed instruction and question renderings produced
during benchmark construction.

Answer parsing and grading.

The agent submits its final answer string through a 
finish
 call, and a judge model, which is Gemini-3.5-Flash 
(
Kavukcuoglu et al., 2026
)
 in our setup, is prompted to decide whether the agent’s answer is equivalent to the ground truth. If the agent never issues a 
finish
 call, the last content in its trajectory is used as the final answer. The judging prompts are shown in Figures 
8
 and 
9
. The judgment is extracted with the regular expression 
<is_equivalent>\s*(.*?)\s*</is_equivalent>
, returning

 otherwise.

Inference budgets.

For each question, we allow at most 1000 turns of response and tool calling and a 6-hour execution limit, with a 60-second timeout per tool call. To tolerate occasional errors, we set the maximum number of retries to 5 for each of the main client network/service, LLM response, tool arguments, and tool calling. The maximum context size is 65,536 tokens; reaching it triggers compression of intermediate search and tool-call results. Tool-call results are truncated to 10,000 characters before being fed back into the response, while the maximum output length is 128K tokens under the highest reasoning effort. Hyper-parameters such as top-
, top-
, and temperature are kept at their defaults for all evaluated models.

Exception handling.

When the agent, judge model, or tools raise timeout or network/service errors that are not resolved by automatic retries, the sample is re-run up to 20 times, and the first successful result is accepted. Any remaining timeout and refusal-to-answer errors are then counted as incorrect answers, while all other remaining errors are skipped in scoring.

C.3 
Search-Result Shortcut Audit

Search conditions.

We use the same Google Search backend through the Serper API as in model
evaluation and retain the results for every query. For a
task with 
 evidence branches, we audit Chinese, English, and every language
in its minimum cover. Each language receives a set of 
 queries:
one translation of the complete question and one query containing the
description of each individual branch.

All queries are generated from the structured task representation. They may translate relation terms and transliterate public
anchors already present in the question. Each single-language query draws
from this predefined set of relation terms, public anchors, and structured
branch descriptions.

Decision rule.

The audit operates on the titles, snippets, and URLs of the search results.
We normalize these three fields and match them against the reference answer
and all answer aliases stored in the construction certificate. An answer or
unambiguous equivalent-alias match assigns the task a shortcut finding. A
zero-match record across the complete query set assigns the task a pass.

Cross-language confirmation.

We then run one branch query in each language of the minimum cover. A term
exposed in one language’s result page may be used as the handoff for the next
language. Each handoff uses terms exposed by the preceding result page. The
route is confirmed when the answer first appears in a title, snippet, or URL
after the language transition. Appendix 
G.2
 shows the
complete process: Latvian results identify Vyatlag, and a Russian follow-up
exposes the modern administrative-center name 
Lesnoy
.

C.4 
Human Verbalization Evaluation

We check the Chinese (
ZH
) verbalization of every one of the 200
semantic tasks. Annotators compare each Chinese rendering directly with its
structured task representation, reference answer, and construction
certificate. Each rendering receives one review, and flagged or uncertain
cases receive a second review. After a Chinese verbalization is accepted, it
is translated into English (
EN
) and the task-specific designated
language (
DL
). The three renderings preserve a shared answer and
evidence structure across languages.

We evaluate each Chinese verbalization for:

1.

preservation of all entities, relations, qualifiers, and coreference;

2.

preservation of the reference answer and requested answer type;

3.

complete and exact preservation of all clues;

4.

preservation of which entities are named and which remain hidden;

5.

preservation of the intended answer-cue boundaries; and

6.

grammaticality and fluency.

Appendix E 
Classifier Implementation and Evidence Policy

The deterministic implementation parses WebSearch and WebFetch events,
identifies query languages, identifies fetched-page languages from URLs, and
maps explicit QIDs, Wikipedia URLs, and normalized multilingual surfaces to
the frozen task profile.

Route policy and observable language entry.

Each profile specifies one or more task-level minimum language covers: the
smallest language sets that provide lexical access to all hidden retrieval-key
entities, as defined in Section 
3
. For trajectory auditing,
the final policy uses the same set as the 
runtime cover
. A prompt
language contained in that set remains an explicit cover member and requires
an identifiable query or a fetched page whose URL identifies that language.
Prompt text is excluded from language entry. Source-only retrieval is
classified as L1 for a non-English source and L2 for an English source.
Non-source route progress for L4 begins when retrieval enters an additional
required cover language. The classifier uses the validated task-level covers
stored in the construction certificates.

For every configured runtime cover, the classifier records the first query or fetch
in each language. The 
winning route
 is the complete cover with the
earliest completion event (with deterministic tie breaking). Route start is
the earliest language-entry event on that winning route, and route completion
is its latest required language-entry event. Completion is the L/T gate, but
the path/bridge/answer evidence window opens at route start and continues to
the end of the trace. This excludes unrelated earlier exposure while
retaining useful evidence acquired after route entry but before route
completion.

Entity and answer evidence.

Path and bridge hits accept an explicit QID, a URL resolved to that QID, or a
normalized multilingual surface match. Required bridge entities are designated
per evidence branch (graph 
match
): a branch requirement is covered when
any designated bridge entity for that branch is hit, and full bridge coverage
requires every branch to be covered.
Strong answer evidence requires the event to contain the answer QID directly
or an exact normalized answer Wikipedia URL. Alias-only answer mentions
remain auxiliary diagnostics and leave the T3/R strong-evidence gate unmet.

Language routing failures (L).

Table 
1
 summarizes the cascade; the implementation resolves the
overlap between L1 and L2 by assigning 
L1 (source lock)
 to
non-English runs whose identifiable retrieval remains in the source language
and 
L2 (English trap)
 to the corresponding English-source behavior.
L2 also covers English-prompt runs without non-source valid-route progress
unless they sustain a non-route-language branch, as well as non-English runs
that enter English without non-source valid-route progress or a sustained
non-route branch.

L3 (wrong-language route)
 applies when retrieval leaves the source but
makes no non-source progress toward a valid runtime cover and instead follows
non-route languages.

L4 (incomplete language cover)
 applies when the run enters at least
one required non-source language on a valid runtime cover but completes no
configured runtime cover.

Evidence-path failure gates (T).

For trajectories that complete a route, 
T1a (nominal switch)
 applies when no
search-result or fetched-page content in a winning-route language appears in
the route-start window. 
T1b (off-path retrieval)
 applies when such
content appears but no configured path entity is hit.

T2a (non-bridge path hit)
 applies when the path is entered but no
required evidence branch is covered by one of its designated bridge entities,
whereas 
T2b (partial bridge coverage)
 applies when some but not all
required branches are covered in this way. 
T3 (answer-evidence
failure)
 requires a complete route, a path hit, and bridge-entity coverage of
every required branch, but no strong answer evidence.

Post-evidence residual failures (R).

R contains incorrect runs that pass the complete-route, path, all-branches, and
strong-answer gates. 
R1 (fetched-page exposure)
 applies if any strong
answer exposure in the route-start window is associated with a WebFetch event;

R2 (search-result-only exposure)
 applies when all strong exposures
occur in WebSearch results. R is an observable-exposure label; model
understanding and evidence use lie outside its definition.

Exclusions and indeterminate cases.

A missing exact trace or incomplete inference/semantic judgment is

EXCLUDED
. A profile with an unusable route or unusable bridge
annotation in its construction certificate is reported as

UNDETERMINED
.

The reported failure analysis is defined over incorrect, classifiable runs and
assigns each one to its earliest failed gate. Table

The certificate induces the following failure cascade:

•

No non-source valid-route progress (L1/L2).
 Retrieval remains at the

 The run leaves the source environment
but sustains retrieval in a language outside every valid runtime cover, without

•

Incomplete valid cover (L4).
 The run enters part of the
certified route beyond the prompt language—for example, under the French

•

Off-path retrieval after complete cover (T1).

Serbian have both been entered, but the retrieved results and pages expose

•

Incomplete bridge recovery (T2).
 The trace reaches a path
entity such as Gabriel, hieromonk, or Grabovac, but does not cover both

This includes coverage of zero or one of

Q19950990

Q135973749

•

Answer-evidence failure (T3).
 Both required evidence
branches are covered through their designated bridge entities, but neither
answer QID 
Q225

•

Residual failure after answer exposure (R).
 The complete

bridge entities, and strong answer evidence are all observed, yet the run
receives score zero. R1 places the strong evidence in a fetched page; R2
leaves it only in search results.

The classifier assigns the earliest unsatisfied boundary in this cascade.
Thus the case separates whether the agent completes a valid runtime cover,
reaches the configured path, covers every required evidence branch through a
designated bridge entity, sees strong answer evidence, and converts that
evidence into a correct completion.

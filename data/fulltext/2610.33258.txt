Abstract

Content moderation compresses a graded, contested human judgment into a binary act (leave
up / take down) over billions of items, at a scale
where running a frontier LLM on every post
is uneconomical. A new class of frozen “System One” models returns typed, calibrated decisions instead of text at 
$0.000025 per call and

100 ms. We run the first independent audit
of one such model on 142,658 human-labeled
comments, and benchmark it against 14 frontier
reasoning models (the GPT-5.x, Claude, Gemini 3, and Grok families) on a shared probe
set. Two findings stand out. First, the cheap
model is 
accuracy-competitive
: its zero-shot
classification AUROC (0.83) is not statistically
separable (paired bootstrap, 
; a failure to
reject parity, not proof of equivalence) from 13
of the 14 frontier systems while costing 1–3
orders of magnitude less, and it is no worse
calibrated than several of them. Second, “confidence” conflates two uncertainties that come
apart: 
human disagreement
 (where annotators
split on satire and coded language) and 
decision risk
 (where the automatic action is costly-wrong). Under an asymmetric moderation loss
these call for 
opposite
 escalation signals, so
no single confidence number serves both. Because the model prices extra questions at nearly
zero, decomposing the judgment into parallel
sub-questions and reading their spread recovers
a human-disagreement signal that the model’s
native confidence misses—but this remedy is
specific: it helps the weakest, disagreement-blind models and only when the decomposition
aligns with the sources of disagreement; a specialist toxicity model and the strongest LLMs
do not need it, and the effect is distribution-sensitive. We will release the instrument, the cached
runs, and the analysis.

Introduction

Platform-scale content moderation is an asymmetric, irreversible binary decision problem. A false
negative lets real harm spread; a false positive is
censorship; and the “right” threshold is a contested
value judgment, not an engineering constant. The
signal underneath is graded—annotators genuinely
disagree on satire, reclaimed slurs, and coded language 
(
Plank, 2022
; 
Pavlick and Kwiatkowski, 2019
; 
Sap et al., 2022
)
—but the action is binary.
Frontier large language models (LLMs) can make
such judgments, but at a cost and latency that make
per-post application impractical for the billions of
items real platforms process.

‘‘System One’’ models

response: frozen models that emit typed, probabilistic decisions (a yes/no probability, a choice
distribution, an ordinal score) rather than free text,
at roughly $0.042 per million input tokens with
free output and 
–

calibration: every decision carries a probability that
is claimed to reflect genuine uncertainty 
(
Guo et al., 2017
)
, so software can act when confident and escalate when not. If true, this is precisely what an
asymmetric-cost triage pipeline needs. Whether it
is true has not been independently tested.

We audit one such model on the Civil Comments
corpus 
(
Borkan et al., 2019
)
 (142,658 comments;
toxicity given as the 
fraction
 of human raters who
flagged each comment—a direct, per-item disagreement signal). We ask two questions:

•

RQ1 (calibration/disagreement).
 Do the
model’s probabilities track the 
distribution

of human annotators—is it uncertain exactly
where humans are?

•

RQ2 (decision).
 Under an explicit
asymmetric loss, does a model-driven
act/abstain/escalate policy reduce cost, and
which uncertainty signal should drive it?

We find that the two questions have 
different
 answers, exposing a dissociation between two notions
of uncertainty (Figure 
1
). On this middle-heavy
corpus the model’s native confidence barely tracks
human disagreement—it can be confidently one-sided on the sarcastic and coded comments where
raters split—yet that same native confidence is the

best
 signal for deciding which automatic actions
are costly enough to escalate. A cheap remedy
for the first failure comes from the model’s own
structure: since it charges (almost) nothing for additional questions and answers them in parallel, we
decompose the judgment into many atomic sub-questions and read their disagreement, recovering
a stronger human-disagreement detector for this
model. But that remedy does not help the cost
objective, is specific to disagreement-blind models, and is distribution-sensitive. Disagreement
and decision risk are two uncertainties, not one.
Separately, we place the model against 14 frontier
reasoning models and find its accuracy statistically
indistinguishable from all but the single strongest,
at a tiny fraction of the cost.

Contributions.

(1) The first independent audit
of a System One model on a real labeled moderation task, including a 14-model frontier comparison showing it is accuracy-competitive at 1–3 orders of magnitude lower cost, with model-dependent (not “cheap 
 bad”) calibration. (2) The

call for opposite escalation signals. (3) A near-free

Related Work

Calibration.

Modern networks are miscalibrated
and admit cheap post-hoc fixes 
(
Guo et al., 2017
; 
Naeini et al., 2015
)
. We measure calibration not
against a hard label but against the human rater 
fraction
, and find it poor and only partly recoverable
by isotonic recalibration.

Aleatoric vs. epistemic uncertainty.

The distinction between irreducible (data) and reducible
(model) uncertainty is foundational 
(
Kendall and Gal, 2017
; 
Hüllermeier and Waegeman, 2021
)
;
deep ensembles estimate the latter via disagreement among predictors 
(
Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017
)
. Reading uncertainty from the spread of multiple generations—semantic entropy 
(
Kuhn et al., 2023
)
 and self-consistency 
(
Wang et al., 2023
)
—is
closely related; our decomposition-spread applies
the same idea across the 
sub-questions
 of a single structured call rather than across resampled
free-text generations. Our decomposition-spread
is an ensemble-of-
questions
 analogue—cheap only
because a structured-decision model prices extra
questions at nearly zero—and we show it captures
human disagreement that a single answer’s confidence misses.

Human label variation.

A growing line treats
annotator disagreement as signal, not noise 
(
Plank, 2022
; 
Uma et al., 2021
; 
Pavlick and Kwiatkowski, 2019
; 
Aroyo and Welty, 2015
)
, especially in toxicity, where annotator identity and belief shape
judgments 
(
Sap et al., 2022
)
. We ask a question
this literature has not: does a deployed model’s

own
 uncertainty track that disagreement?

Selective prediction.

Risk–coverage analysis
and learning-to-reject let a model abstain to trade
coverage for accuracy 
(
El-Yaniv and Wiener, 2010
; 
Geifman and El-Yaniv, 2017
)
; these typically escalate by the model’s own confidence. Conformal prediction and conformal risk control add
distribution-free coverage/risk guarantees to such
abstention 
(
Angelopoulos and Bates, 2023
; 
Quach et al., 2024
)
; our question is orthogonal—
which

signal to escalate by, not how to set the threshold—and applies inside any of these wrappers. We show
that under an asymmetric moderation loss the 
best

escalation signal depends on the objective and is
not always native confidence.

Moderation systems and LLM judges.

Perspective
 is a production toxicity classifier 
(
Lees et al., 2022
)
; cheap models are increasingly used as
judges 
(
Zheng et al., 2023
)
. We benchmark against
both and study the quality of their uncertainty, not
only their accuracy.

Experiments

4.1 
A strong zero-shot classifier, badly miscalibrated

As a classifier against the majority label, the model
reaches AUROC 0.79 zero-shot—above the lexical
baseline (0.71) but below a TF-IDF logistic regression 
trained on 142k in-domain labels
 (0.91) (Table 
1
). This is the expected order: the model’s value
is cold-start, not beating a supervised in-domain
model on accuracy. Its probabilities, however, are
severely over-confident relative to human fractions:
Expected Calibration Error is 0.25 overall and 0.33
on natural traffic. Figure 
6
 shows the reliability
curve far below the diagonal—when the model reports 0.97 toxic, humans average only 0.49. The
miscalibration is only partly a monotone scaling
issue: post-hoc isotonic recalibration (5-fold out-of-fold, no test leakage) halves natural-traffic ECE
to 0.13 but does not remove it.

4.5 
RQ2: for cost, the ranking inverts

When the objective is operational cost rather than
matching human disagreement, the winners swap
(Figure 
5
). Escalating by native confidence gives
the best deployable AURC (0.504), close to the
oracle (0.417); escalating by decomposition-spread
(0.542) is no better than random (0.546). The reason is structural: cost triage should escalate items
whose automatic action is likely wrong and expensive (decision-risk), which need not be the items
where humans disagree. At 
 (maximal
disagreement) the auto-cost roughly equals 
, so
escalating there saves nothing.

Native confidence wins by proxy, not by measuring risk.

This edge is largely a 
miscalibration
artifact
, not evidence that raw confidence measures
decision-risk. Escalating by 

ranks items near the raw boundary 
; but under isotonic recalibration the raw

a 
human

, which sits in the same
low-probability region as the cost-optimal threshold 
. So “escalate
the most native-uncertain” coincidentally selects
the items whose calibrated probability is nearest
the decision threshold—precisely the set whose
automatic action is most likely wrong. We confirm the mechanism directly: escalating by the true
threshold-distance 
 on recalibrated probabilities gives an 
identical
 AURC (0.504)—as does
the asymmetric-cost-optimal rule of ranking by the
expected decision risk 

(0.505)—whereas escalating by 
genuine

uncertainty

)
is 
worse than random

4.6 
Which decomposition? Alignment matters

Decomposition-spread is not magic: it works only
when the sub-questions align with what drives human disagreement. Splitting our ten questions into
the six dataset-derived toxicity facets versus the
three judgment-only cues (sarcasm, coded, target)
shows this sharply (Table 
5
). The facet spread
tracks disagreement (AUROC 0.75), but the cue
spread—though the cues 
fire
 on the hard cases (Table 
3
)—does not (AUROC 0.52, near chance). Aggregating over facets that span the space of toxicity
types recovers “how borderline is this across dimensions?” which coincides with human disagreement;
a few orthogonal cues do not. The gap is not an
ensemble-size artifact: holding the sub-question
count fixed at three, all 20 three-facet subsets of the
six facets beat the three judgment cues (mean AUROC 0.724, min 0.669; 20 of 20), and even single
facets average 0.656—so it is semantic alignment,
not the number of sub-questions, that drives the
effect. Nor is it a mechanical artifact of binary entropy collapsing on confident cue detections: the
cues are 
not
 confidently detected (mean per-cue entropy 0.64–0.83, far from the near-zero a saturated
detector would give), and ranking by the raw cue

probability
 instead of its entropy tracks disagreement no better (AUROC 0.57 vs. 0.76 for the facet
spread). The same entropy operator recovers disagreement on the aligned facets, so the failure is
semantic—what the sub-questions measure—not
a property of the metric. The facet/cue split is
moreover 
a priori
 (dataset-derived attributes vs. judgment cues), not chosen by performance: on a
held-out half, with the contested threshold fixed on
the other half, the facet spread still scores 0.760
and the cue spread 0.522 (matching the full-set
0.759/0.518), so the exclusion is not a fitted choice.

when the available decomposition no longer aligns

4.7 
Generalization

On an out-of-distribution set from a different platform and a hate-speech (not toxicity) construct
(

advantage does 
not

is exactly what §
4.6
 predicts: the construct shift
breaks the facet–disagreement alignment. To rule
out a labeling artifact, we repeat the disagreement test against the dataset’s 
continuous
 annotator-disagreement and its item-response-theory severity score rather than the quantized rater fraction:
the result is unchanged (subattr-spread Spearman

toxicity probability tracks the continuous severity

therefore driven by construct shift, not by label
quantization or annotator sparsity. We report it as
a boundary on the effect rather than evidence of
robustness.

4.8 
Cost, speed, consistency

Across 
150k calls we observed 117–124 calls/s
from a single client, zero errors (one timeout),
$0.000025 per comment, and per-item repeat standard deviation 0.009. At this price a platform can
score every item; the full study cost $3.80.

5 
Discussion

The practical upshot is a design rule. If your goal
is to route 
genuinely contested
 content to a human
for a value judgment—the satire/coded/identity
cases where reasonable people disagree—use
a 
construct-aligned
 decomposition-spread (sub-questions matched to your domain’s sources of
disagreement, per §
4.6
); the model’s native confidence will actively mislead you. If your goal
is to minimize operational miscost under asymmetric penalties, use native confidence and ignore
disagreement—or, if you already hold calibrated
probabilities, escalate by the expected decision risk

 directly, which native
confidence only mimics by miscalibration coincidence. Conflating the two—treating a single “confidence” number as both an aleatoric and a decision-risk signal—is a mistake this data makes vivid, and
it is not specific to one vendor: the trained logistic
regression shows the same split from the other side
(its confidence tracks disagreement moderately but
is a weak cost-triage signal).

The decomposition result also reframes what
a cheap parallel structured-decision model buys
you. Its headline calibration is poor, and a supervised in-domain model beats it on raw accuracy.
What is genuinely enabled by free, parallel, self-consistent questions is 
ensembling atomic judgments
: the uncertainty that any single “calibrated”
answer hides re-emerges in the spread across questions, cheaply. We use “uncertainty” loosely here—the spread across 
different
 facets is not the model’s
sampling variance on one question (classical epistemic uncertainty from resampling) but the multi-dimensional ambiguity of the input surfaced across
sub-judgments, closer to an aleatoric signal, which
is precisely why it aligns with human disagreement.

Future work.

Three directions follow directly.
First, learn or search the decomposition instead
of hand-authoring it, since §
4.6
 shows alignment with the sources of disagreement is what
makes the spread informative. Second, test
whether escalating by the calibrated expected decision risk—which matches native confidence’s
cost triage without relying on a miscalibration
coincidence—transfers across cost regimes and
models. Third, extend the audit to more System One vendors and to multilingual and image/text settings, where the disagreement/decision-risk split may take different shapes; and test the

method
 (with a construct-matched decomposition)
on implicit and disagreement-rich benchmarks such
as ToxiGen 
(
Hartvigsen et al., 2022
)
 and MD-Agreement 
(
Leonardelli et al., 2021
)
, where the
sub-questions must be re-aligned to the new construct.

6 
Conclusion

A $0.000025 System One model is a usable zero-shot moderator whose accuracy we cannot statistically separate (paired bootstrap, 
) from 13
of 14 frontier reasoning models at 1–3 orders of
magnitude lower cost, though it is only moderately
calibrated. Its “confidence,” however, conflates
two uncertainties that come apart: on this corpus
native confidence barely tracks human disagreement yet is the best signal for minimizing operational cost, while a near-free decomposition of
the judgment recovers the disagreement signal it
misses. That decomposition remedy is targeted—it helps the weakest, disagreement-blind models
when the sub-questions align with the sources of
disagreement, and not otherwise. Disagreement
and decision risk are two uncertainties, not one; a
triage system should pick the signal that matches
its objective, and should not assume a cheap price
implies poor judgment.

Limitations

Rater fractions are a proxy for 
, not ground
truth. Relatedly, Civil Comments carries no ground-truth label for whether a comment 
is
 satire, coded,
or reclaimed speech; we use the entropy of the
rater fraction as a proxy for these contested cases,
but a comment can be genuinely toxic 
and
 contested, and our disagreement signal cannot separate “raters split because the case is subtle” from
“raters split because some erred.” Civil Comments
is public and may overlap the model’s training
data; our out-of-distribution set mitigates but does
not eliminate this, and itself shifts construct and
has coarse disagreement labels. We could not
run the OpenAI Moderation API (it needs a separate OpenAI account key we lacked; the frontier
models were reached through a unified third-party
router without a moderation endpoint); the specialist and guard categories are otherwise represented by 
Perspective
, 
Detoxify
, 
HateBERT
,
and five open guard LLMs (
Granite Guardian
,

Llama Guard
 3, 
WildGuard
, and 
ShieldGemma
 2b/9b), and the in-domain-trained specialists are contaminated by construction. The costs

 encode a contested value choice; we
report frontiers and a sweep rather than a single
verdict. The instrument’s sub-questions were chosen to mirror the dataset’s own facets; a different decomposition might track disagreement differently, which is itself consistent with the out-of-distribution non-replication. The general LLM
baseline was rate-limited to 435 scored comments,
so its paired comparison has wider error bars than
the others. The frontier-parity test uses a small,
class-balanced 56-comment probe, so per-model
AUROC is noisy; we therefore rely on the paired
bootstrap and the Spearman-vs-continuous read
rather than exact rank order. We audit one System
One model at one point in time; vendors update
models, and our cached run, which we will release, fixes the exact
version studied.

Ethical Considerations

Content moderation decisions affect speech and
safety, and the “right” operating point is a value
choice we deliberately do not fix. Our central message is cautionary: a model’s native confidence
can be 
anti
-correlated with genuine human disagreement, so deploying it to decide which posts
are “uncertain enough” for human review can systematically hide exactly the contested, culturally-loaded cases (satire, reclaimed slurs, coded language) that most need human judgment. We recommend that abstention/escalation for value-laden
review be driven by signals validated against annotator disagreement, not by raw model confidence.
We use only public datasets and report aggregate
statistics; no new human annotation was collected.
Annotator disagreement itself reflects the demographics and guidelines of the Civil Comments
rater pool, so “contested” is defined relative to that
population and need not transfer to other communities.

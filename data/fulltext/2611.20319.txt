Abstract

A decade after the publication of the FAIR Guiding Principles, what have we learned about data sharing, and what do data availability statements (DAS) and other materials that have accrued over that period tell us about how data-sharing practices, attitudes, and infrastructures have evolved?
Here, we present a large-scale longitudinal analysis of over 6.8 million biomedical articles from the PubMed Central Open Access Subset, spanning the period from 2011 till 2025.
Using an automated
pipeline, we extracted and classified DAS, repository links, accession numbers, and supplementary materials, assigning each article a data-sharing score and independently FAIR-assessing a curated subset of cited dataset URLs.
We find that, in relative terms, explicit DAS adoption rose from approximately 2% of articles in 2011 to 68% in 2025,
while statements of the “available on request” type
grew in parallel from near zero to over 19%.
The corpus’s growth amplifies both patterns: in absolute terms,
articles with a data availability statement containing an explicit mention of a data repository rose from 132 in 2011 to 94,697 in 2025 (a 
720-fold increase), while “available on request” articles rose from 77 to 173,474 (i.e. 
2,250-fold) over the same 2011–2025 span.
Structural biology and genomics consistently achieved the highest data-sharing scores, while clinical research and public health ranked lowest.
Gold Open Access journals showed a small overall advantage over subscription journals,
while Diamond Open Access journals – which charge neither readers nor authors – clearly outperformed both.
Another
observation is that internationally collaborative articles scored higher than domestic ones. Internationally collaborative articles scored higher than domestic ones. A gender gap emerged as well: female first-authored articles’ scores declined more than male first-authored ones’.
The analysis further included
additional dimensions, from compliance with funder and journal mandates or with JATS XML tagging standards to computational reproducibility signals and the co-occurrence of DAS with ethics statements.
For instance, ethics statements, present in 17.9% of articles, are associated with a higher DAS rate but a lower mean data-sharing score, consistent with human-subjects research more often falling into restrictive, privacy-centric sharing categories.
Taken together, a decade on, the gap between declaring data availability and actually sharing machine-readable, reusable data persists, albeit with variation across fields, infrastructures and demographics.

1 
Introduction

The FAIR Guiding Principles (Findability, Accessibility, Interoperability, and Reusability) for the sharing of research data and associated metadata 
Wilkinson et al. (2016)
 were introduced as a stakeholder-neutral framework for data management and stewardship, published in March 2016 after development within the FORCE11 community. Rather than a technical standard or certification, the principles were deliberately formulated as a minimal, high-level set of guideposts, applicable regardless of discipline, that data and metadata should satisfy to be genuinely machine-actionable and reusable by both humans and computational workflows.

In 2018, two years after the principles’ publication, a design framework and exemplar maturity indicators followed 
Wilkinson et al. (2018)
, giving rise to a family of assessment approaches. Early FAIR self-assessment relied on manual questionnaires and expert-reviewed checklists; these did not scale to corpus-wide application and suffered from inter-rater inconsistency, motivating a shift toward automated, rule-based evaluation such as the currently popular F-UJI 
Devaraju and Huber (2021)
.

In parallel and building
on FAIR-inspired prior work on data citation 
Mietchen et al. (2015)
,
the JATS4R community initiative 
Beck et al. (2015)

published prescriptive JATS XML markup recommendations for data-availability 
JATS4R Working Group (2018)
 and ethics-statement 
NISO JATS4R Working Group, Ethics Statement Subgroup (2020)
 sections, aiming to close the gap between human-readable and machine-actionable statements – a distinction that this study tracks throughout as “compliance” versus mere “presence” (cf. Sections 
2.7
 and 
2.10
).
Of note, both F-UJI and JATS4R focus on assessing compliance at a given moment rather than on changes over time. Corpus-scale literature-mining proposals aimed specifically at structured, longitudinally trackable statement content, such as 
N.Hegde et al. (2022)
’s
approach to mining ethics statements, and recent work tracking the reproducibility of released research software over time 
Coakley et al. (2026)
, point toward
addressing such gaps
outside the FAIR-assessment tools themselves.

Before the FAIR Principles existed, evidence was already accumulating that data-sharing 
statements
 do not reliably translate into data-sharing 
practice
. Vines et al. 
Vines et al. (2014)
 showed that the odds of a dataset still being available fall by roughly 17% per year after publication, and Federer et al. 
Federer et al. (2018)
 found that only a minority of PLOS ONE
Data Availability Statements pointed to a usable, non-supplementary data location even under one of the field’s strictest mandates. More recently, the “available upon request” pattern was tested
directly by emailing corresponding authors, which led to fewer than two in five 
Tedersoo et al. (2021)
 and fewer than one in five 
Hussey (2025)
 requests, respectively, being honoured.
Likewise, 
Dunić and Vilibić (2026)
 question whether the mere presence of a DAS increases actual data sharing; both approaches offer direct behavioural evidence for the anti-pattern this study tracks at corpus scale in Section 
3.2
 and Section 
3.7
.
At the corpus level, Wallach et al. 
Wallach et al. (2018)
 audited a large sample of the biomedical literature (2015–2017) against multiple transparency indicators and, like this study, found improvement concentrated in a subset of indicators rather than across the board. Code sharing alone does not guarantee computational reproducibility, a point this study’s own prior work on Jupyter notebooks from biomedical publications makes directly – both in auditing their actual re-execution success 
Samuel and Mietchen (2024a)
 and in a separate reproducibility-assessment framework 
Samuel et al. (2026)
 – and that Section 
3.10
 below confirms at corpus scale: the mean reproducibility-signal uplift associated with code sharing is large, but only 2.1% of the corpus carries any reproducibility signal at all.
Colavizza et al. 
Colavizza et al. (2020)
 showed that articles linking to a data repository accumulate a measurable citation advantage, one candidate mechanism for the “named repository” DAS category’s growth documented in Section 
3.2
 and Section 
3.4
.

The disciplinary and demographic unevenness of open-science uptake is likewise established outside the data-sharing literature specifically. Huang et al. 
Huang et al. (2020)
 documented a persistent, if narrowing, gender gap in scientific productivity and career length across countries and fields, a structural backdrop against which the first-authorship data-sharing-score gap reported in Section 
3.9
 should be read, since neither study can separate a genuine practice gap from compounding factors such as field, seniority, and institutional access. On the publishing-model side, Bosman et al. 
Bosman et al. (2021)
 mapped the global landscape of Diamond (no-fee, no-APC) open-access journals, providing the external baseline against which the Diamond OA advantage identified in Section 
3.8
 can be situated.

What distinguishes the present study from this body of work is scale and integration rather than any single method: prior studies typically examine comparatively small samples in terms of journals 
Acciai et al. (2023)
 or countries 
Oh et al. (2026)
,
a short time window 
Lawrimore et al. (2026)
,
or only a subset of FAIR dimensions 
Sinha et al. (2023)
, or they
use AI/LLM-based classification on a scale this study’s transparent rule-based approach does not attempt 
Wong et al. (2026)
.
Here, the same 6,874,176-article PMC-OA corpus (2011–2025) is scored by a single, transparent DAS-classification rule set, cross-validated against F-UJI’s independent automated assessment, audited for JATS4R machine-readability, and stratified simultaneously by discipline, funder, geography, open-access tier, and inferred author gender, allowing the ten-year trajectory of FAIR uptake to be decomposed by 
who
 adopted it, 
how
 they documented it, and whether that documentation is actually machine-actionable, within one consistent corpus and scoring scheme.

2 
Methods

2.1 
Study Design and Conceptual Framework

This study adopts a longitudinal, corpus-based observational design to characterise how data-sharing practice in the biomedical and life-science literature evolved across a fifteen-year window (2011–2025) relative to the publication of the FAIR Principles 
Wilkinson et al. (2016)
. The year 2016 serves as the principal inflection point: articles published in the five years immediately preceding it (2011–2015) define the pre-FAIR baseline, and articles from 2016 onward are assessed for evidence of behavioural change aligned with Findability, Accessibility, Interoperability, and Reusability. The study also interrogates whether observed shifts are better attributed to concurrent policy drivers, namely journal data-availability mandates, funder open-data requirements, and the JATS4R markup recommendations, than to the FAIR Principles themselves. The correlational and before/after evidence presented here does not constitute claims about causality.

2.2 
Data Source and Corpus Construction

The primary data source is the PubMed Central Open Access Subset (PMC-OA), which distributes full-text articles as JATS (Journal Article Tag Suite) XML in a way that facilitates mining 
Mietchen et al. (2013)
. Raw article XML was retrieved for publication years 2011–2025 via the PMC bulk-download / NCBI E-utilities interface, restricted to the open-access licence filter. Articles were included if they were parseable JATS records with a resolvable publication year; conference abstracts, errata, and other non-article record types are excluded at the parsing stage (Section 
2.3
). The resulting master frame comprises 6,874,176 articles.

2.4 
Extraction of Data Availability Statements

DAS text is extracted by a six-location priority cascade applied to each article’s 
<back>
 and 
<body>
 elements, in the following order: (1) a legacy 
<data-availability>
 element; (2) a 
<sec>
 whose 
sec-type
 attribute matches a DAS-indicating pattern (e.g. 
data-availability
, 
availability
, 
data-access
); (3) a 
<sec>
 with no 
sec-type
 whose 
<title>
 text matches the same pattern (e.g. ”Data Availability”, ”Availability of data”); (4) a 
<custom-meta>
 element whose 
<meta-name>
 matches; (5) a 
<notes>
 element whose 
notes-type
 matches; (6) a footnote (
<fn-group>/<fn>
) whose text matches. The first match wins, and the full text and structural provenance (
source
, e.g. 
sec[@sec-type=’data-availability’]
 or 
sec/title=’Data Availability’
) are stored verbatim in 
data_availability

da_link

2.5 
Repository Classification

The repository-classification stage (Stage 3) matches every URL in 
da_link
 against a curated knowledge base of 81 domain/path regex patterns spanning nine repository types: general-purpose (e.g. Zenodo, Figshare, Dryad, Dataverse), domain-specific (e.g. NCBI GEO, SRA, GenBank, UniProt, PDB), code repositories (GitHub, GitLab), journal/publisher platforms, institutional repositories, cloud storage (Dropbox, Google Drive), preprint servers, ”request-only” flagged links, and an unmatched ”other” catch-all. Each matched entry carries a curated repository infrastructure tier: gold (typically offers a persistent identifier, open metadata, long-term preservation, e.g. Zenodo, GEO, SRA), silver (typically offers most of the above with some limitation, e.g. GitHub, OSF), or bronze (limited infrastructure support, e.g. journal supplementary portals, cloud storage), plus open-access and persistent-identifier flags. This tier is a static, repository-type-level heuristic, not a per-dataset FAIR assessment of what was actually deposited; Section 
2.7
 covers the latter via F-UJI on a gold/silver-tier subset. Unmatched URLs are tier 
unknown
.

2.7 
Automated FAIR Assessment with F-UJI

A subset of dataset URLs from gold- and silver-tier repositories (Section 
2.5
) was independently assessed with the F-UJI automated FAIR-evaluation tool 
Devaraju and Huber (2021)
, self-hosted as a Docker container (

fuji_assessment

fuji_metric

fuji_article_link

2.8 
Extraction of Ethics Statements

Ethics statements are extracted by the ethics-extraction stage (Stage 2b), structurally parallel to the DAS extractor, using a six-level priority cascade over each article’s

A further utility applies the same six-level cascade to standalone Code/Software Availability sections, written to

2.9 
Discipline, Open Access, and Funder Enrichment

The metadata-enrichment stage (Stage 6) adds three stratification variables used throughout the Results:

•

Discipline.

general
 bucket, giving 13 discipline categories in total. Written to 
article_mesh
 (per-term) and 
article_discipline
 (one resolved discipline per article).

•

Open access status.
 A per-journal classification into 
gold_oa
 / 
likely_subscription
 / 
not_listed_in_doaj
 /

) and known subscription publishers (e.g. Elsevier, Springer, Wiley, Nature

, a publisher-level heuristic only, since these publishers may also carry fully-OA journals); where neither heuristic matches, the journal’s print and online ISSN are each queried against the DOAJ API

, giving 
gold_oa
 on a match and 
not_listed_in_doaj
 (explicitly

 covers journals with no ISSN to query at all. The result is also a 
current
 DOAJ snapshot, not the journal’s access model at a given article’s actual publication year. Written to 
journal_oa
. This pipeline-level classification is intentionally coarse; the finer Diamond/Gold/hybrid/subscription split used in several Results figures is a notebook-level refinement described in Section

.

•

Funder attribution.
 Funder names and grant IDs are extracted by regex from

and normalised against a curated list of major funding agencies (e.g. NIH, Wellcome, ERC, UKRI, DFG, NSF, Gates Foundation), each tagged with its funder country. Written to

.

2.10 
JATS4R Compliance Classification

Unlike the enrichment steps above, compliance classification against the JATS4R (JATS for Reuse) recommendations is 
not
 a pipeline stage: it is implemented directly in the DAS and ethics-statement analysis notebooks as a pure function over the verbatim 
source
 provenance string the pipeline already stored (Section 
2.4
, Section 
3.8
); no new extraction or re-parsing of the original XML is performed.
JATS4R’s DAS recommendation 
JATS4R Working Group (2018)
 specifies 
sec-type="data-availability"
 as the only compliant value; its ethics-statement recommendation 
NISO JATS4R Working Group, Ethics Statement Subgroup (2020)
 specifies 
sec-type="ethics-statement"
.
The notebook classifier maps each 
source
 string to one of five tiers: 
compliant
 (exact match); 
near-miss
 (one of 16 named wrong-case/misspecified variants that JATS4R explicitly flags, e.g. 
"Data Availability Statement"
, 
"data_availability"
); 
title-only
 (found only via heading text, no 
sec-type
 attribute at all); 
non-standard
 (a publisher-specific 
custom-meta
, 
notes
, or footnote element); and 
no statement
.
Because the pipeline records the publisher’s own attribute value verbatim rather than a parsed/normalised category, this yields ground-truth, per-article compliance data rather than a sample-based estimate.

2.11 
Notebook-Level Derived Measures

Several measures reported in the Results are computed entirely within individual analysis notebooks, on top of the pipeline tables above, rather than being pipeline stages or persisted database columns in their own right:

•

Diamond/Gold/hybrid/subscription OA tiers
: a dedicated analysis notebook refines the pipeline’s coarse 
journal_oa
 status by matching journal ISSNs against a curated Diamond OA list (drawn from the cOAlition S 2021 Diamond OA study 
Bosman et al. (2021)
, a DOAJ APC=0 query, and manual verification against each publisher’s own website)
and by publisher/title pattern matching, distinguishing no-fee Diamond OA from APC-funded Gold OA within what the pipeline had initially labeled 
gold_oa
 (and, as corrected in Section 
2.9
 above, mapping 
likely_subscription
 into this finer tier set’s 
subscription
 label).

•

Author gender
: 
name-based gender inference
, not a measurement of author gender identity, computed offline in a standalone pipeline utility for every distinct given name in the corpus using a two-strategy classifier: the 
names_dataset
 global name-gender probability database as primary source, with the rule-based 
gender_guesser
 package as fallback, joined into the analysis notebooks by given name.
A name is classified 
female
 or 
male
 only if one gender’s probability reaches an 0.80 threshold; bare initials and names below threshold are kept as 
ambiguous
/
unknown
 and excluded from binary comparisons. This method cannot identify non-binary authors, uses global rather than country-specific name distributions (so it is less reliable for East Asian, South Asian, and African naming traditions, where the same name can carry a different gender association than in the Western-skewed reference data.
Benchmarked error rates for the best-performing commercial tools range from 2.8% on European names to 17.6% on Asian names 
Santamaría and Mihaljević (2018)
, and similarly degrade on biomedical-literature author names specifically 
Sebo (2021)
), and have not been validated against any ground-truth sample of self-reported author gender for this corpus. Accordingly, every gender-stratified result in this manuscript should be read as an exploratory secondary signal rather than an established measurement (Section 
3.9
 in the Results discusses the effect on resolution completeness over time, and the confound this creates for the reported temporal trend). A country-aware alternative exists in principle: the World Gender-Name Dictionary pairs given names with country-specific gender distributions across 195 countries 
Lax Martínez et al. (2021)
, but integrating it is left to future work.

•

Reproducibility signals R1–R6
: six independent regex detectors applied to each article’s DAS text, namely code sharing (R1: GitHub/GitLab/code-hosting mentions), workflow-manager use (R2), environment specification (R3), version pinning (R4), interactive notebooks (R5), and pre-registration (R6), combined into a 0–6 composite score. These are notebook-defined patterns over the same 
data_availability.statement
 text the pipeline extracted, not a separate extraction pass over the XML.

•

Mandate-compliance rubric
: a five-tier compliance label (fully compliant / compliant / partial / non-compliant / restrictive) is derived directly from each article’s existing data-sharing score band (Section 
2.6
); no new signal is extracted. Funder-level and journal-level mandate milestone years (e.g. NIH 2003/2023, Wellcome 2011/2017, MRC 2013/2018, Horizon 2020 ORD Pilot 2017) are a curated reference table drawn from the funders’ own published policies, not automatically detected from the corpus.

2.12 
Analytical Notebooks

All statistical analysis and figure generation were performed in 14 topic-focused Jupyter notebooks (database overview, corpus overview, DAS categories, JATS4R compliance, ethics statements, notebook/code sharing, gender, Diamond OA, mandate compliance, reproducibility, digital-use-conditions/OAMI, link rot, geography, and journal profiles), each querying the shared SQLite database directly via 
pandas.read_sql_query
. Figures and tabular exports are written to dedicated output directories in the code repository; no analysis code operates on the raw XML directly, as every notebook reads only from the database tables populated by the pipeline stages above.

2.13 
Statistical Methods

Group comparisons of data-sharing scores use the Mann-Whitney U test
, given the scores’ bounded, non-normal, category-driven distribution.
Associations between categorical variables (e.g. DAS category 
 period, compliance tier 
 discipline) are tested with Pearson’s chi-squared test of independence, reported with Cramér’s V as effect size where stated. Linear associations between two continuous variables (e.g. reproducibility score versus
data-sharing score, notebook rate versus journal data-sharing score) use Pearson and, where noted, Spearman correlation. For standard treatments of the nonparametric, categorical-association, and correlation methods used in this paragraph, see 
(
Pagano et al., 2022
)
.
Simple linear trends over time (e.g. the female first-authorship share) are fitted with ordinary least-squares regression (

3 
Results

We analysed 6,874,176 articles from the PubMed Central Open Access Subset published between 2011 and 2025 to characterise how data sharing practice has evolved relative to the 2016 publication of the FAIR Principles. Each article was scored on a 0–10 proxy FAIR scale derived from its data availability statement (DAS) category and secondary quality signals; a subset of dataset links was additionally assessed with the independent, automated F-UJI tool. The ten themes below summarise the corpus-wide trajectory, the persistent gaps between formal compliance and genuine machine-actionability, and where FAIR practice varies most by discipline, geography, funder, gender, and reproducibility.

Repository Choice and F-UJI-Assessed FAIR Scores

Ranked by raw citation count, the most-referenced destinations in DAS content are doi.org (116,421 links) and GitHub (110,967, silver tier), followed by a cluster of gold-tier, domain-specific repositories characteristic of molecular biology (NCBI GEO, GenBank, NCBI, OSF, BioProject, EMBL-EBI, NCBI SRA, and PDB),

Funder and Journal Mandate Compliance

Scoring every article against a five-tier mandate-compatibility rubric, 47.1% of the corpus meets or exceeds the “compliant” threshold while 26.1% falls in the restrictive band. Tracking compliance tiers over time reveals a striking, counter-intuitive trend (Figure

from “compliant” into “partial”, net negative for the corpus median.
Put in absolute terms, this is not a shrinking tier: the number of compliant articles still grew from 81,748 (2011) to 195,210 (2025). Reproducibility Signals and Their Relation to Data-Sharing Score

FAIR compliance and computational reproducibility are conceptually distinct and only weakly coupled empirically, echoing 
Di Cosmo et al. (2026)

Across six reproducibility signal layers (code sharing, workflow managers, environment specification, version pinning, interactive notebooks, pre-registration), code sharing is by far the most common (1.64% of the corpus); only 2.1% of articles have any of these signals, and a “high reproducibility” signature (
3 layers) is present in just 0.006%. The Pearson correlation between the composite reproducibility score and data-sharing score is positive but modest (r=0.195,

4 
Discussion

4.1 
Principal Findings

Three results recur across almost every analytical dimension explored in this study (discipline, geography, funder, OA tier, gender, and publisher) and together form this paper’s central finding.

First, formal data-sharing documentation and the underlying 
quality
 of that documentation moved in opposite directions over 2011–2025: explicit DAS adoption rose more than eightfold, while the mean data-sharing score fell (Section 
3.3
), and JATS4R-compliant machine tagging rose from near zero to roughly a third of DAS-bearing articles while remaining uncorrelated (if anything, inversely correlated) with DAS content quality (Section 
3.5
).
Second, this decline is compositional rather than a uniform retreat: existing journals’ own named-repository rates barely moved (median +1.1 percentage points per journal), so the corpus-wide pattern is driven by which new articles and venues entered the corpus after 2016, each carrying the restrictive “available on request” anti-pattern at a higher rate than the pre-2016 corpus did (Section 
3.2
) – one candidate mechanism behind that growth being the citation advantage repository-linked articles accrue 
Colavizza et al. (2020)
, as noted in the Introduction.
Third, the decline is pervasive but not absolute: Diamond OA journals and roughly a third of individual journals moved against the corpus-wide trend, showing that a FAIR-supportive trajectory remained achievable throughout the study period for specific communities even as the aggregate picture worsened.

A second cluster of findings concerns where responsibility for these gaps actually sits. JATS4R tagging compliance is essentially entirely at the publisher/production-pipeline level, and
among the 644 publishers in our corpus with 
50 articles and at least one DAS-bearing article, 414 (64.3%) sit at an intermediate compliance percentage rather than exactly 0% or 100% – only 117 are fully compliant and 113 fully non-compliant, and
Table 
1
 gives examples from both ends of the spectrum. Sorting by article volume instead of compliance rate (Table 
1
, third block) shows this is not a volume effect either: among the ten largest publishers in the corpus, compliance ranges from 0.0% (MDPI, Cureus Inc.) to 99.3% (Frontiers Media SA), so the production choice that determines compliance is independent of how many articles a publisher puts through it.
The lowest-scoring repository destinations are disproportionately the plain-
http://
 and legacy 
ftp://
 links that depend on third-party infrastructure rather than author effort (Section 
3.4
). This is consistent with the idea that responsibility for reproducibility is not limited to individual authors 
Di Cosmo et al. (2026)
, and extends it: in this corpus, a meaningful share of the machine-readability gap is attributable to publisher XML pipelines and repository infrastructure quality, not to what authors write in their DAS.

Funder and journal mandates show a genuinely double-edged effect rather than a simple success or failure: the “fully compliant” share (which presumably represents mostly intended effects) rose steadily throughout the study period, but a much larger share of the corpus was (presumably by way of side effects) pushed down from “compliant” into “partial” compliance over the same years (Section 
3.7
), and no major funder’s own pre/post-mandate-year comparison showed a clear, statistically robust improvement once the corpus-wide secular decline is accounted for.
Mandates appear to be raising the ceiling for a committed minority, while the median article drifts downward, a pattern invisible to any metric that reports only an aggregate “compliant” percentage without also tracking its internal composition.

Finally, this study documents two persistent gaps that mandates and recommendations have not closed: the first is a gender gap in data-sharing score that reversed sign (Section 
3.9
), going from a small female-favouring difference before the FAIR Principles to a larger male-favouring one after, with female first-authored articles’ scores declining significantly more than male first-authored articles’ scores across that shift. The second gap is a near-total decoupling of FAIR compliance from computational reproducibility (Section 
3.10
), where only 2.1% of the corpus carries any reproducibility signal at all and the correlation between reproducibility and data-sharing score is positive but modest (r=0.195), consistent with 
Samuel et al. (2026)
’s separate finding that static readiness signals and actual execution-based reproducibility outcomes are only weakly linked in research software more generally.

4.2 
Implications

For 
authors
, the single most actionable practice this study’s data supports is a narrow one: name a specific repository and give it a persistent, resolvable identifier (a DOI or accession number) directly in the DAS, rather than writing only “available on request” or pointing to a supplementary file. The difference in outcome is large, not cosmetic: DAS classified as naming a repository predominantly resolve to gold- or silver-tier infrastructure (Section 
3.4
), while the “available on request” anti-pattern – which carries none of that infrastructure benefit – has itself grown more than 2,250-fold in absolute terms since 2011, faster than the named-repository best practice has grown (Section 
3.2
). The same practice extends to code: this study’s own prior work on Jupyter notebooks from biomedical publications found that sharing code is not the same as that code remaining executable 
Samuel and Mietchen (2024a)
, and at corpus scale here, only 2.1% of articles carry any detectable reproducibility signal at all, with code sharing alone the most common of those signals (Section 
3.10
); citing a stable, versioned repository identifier for code and workflow artefacts, not only for data, is the same fix applied to a second, parallel gap.
The observed gaps in data sharing – particularly those based on discipline, gender and on international collaboration – suggest it might be beneficial for researchers to include data sharing aspects more profoundly in their exchanges with colleagues.

For 
publishers
, since JATS4R tagging compliance is determined at the publisher/production-pipeline level,
this means that the fastest route to closing the machine-readability gap regarding DAS is not individual author education but a comparatively small number of production-pipeline fixes: a single large publisher moving its compliance rate substantially (the extremes in Table 
1
 show this can mean 0% or 100%, but most publishers sit somewhere between) would shift the corpus-wide rate more than any plausible author-facing intervention could. The same logic applies to ethics-statement tagging, which lags DAS tagging at every stage (Section 
3.5
).

For 
editors and reviewers
, the parameters studied here translate directly into a short, checkable review list that does not require subject-matter expertise: does the DAS name a specific repository and give an accession number or identifier, rather than only asserting that data exists or is available on request (Section 
3.2
)? Does that identifier actually resolve to a gold- or silver-tier, actively maintained repository rather than a plain-
http://
 or legacy 
ftp://
 link (Section 
3.4
)?
Is the DAS tagged per JATS4R so it is machine-readable downstream (Section 
3.5
)?

Most of the gaps this study documents are visible at review time (with JATS4R compliance being a common exception), yet they persist because the DAS tends to be read less carefully than the rest of the methods sections, not because the underlying problems are hard to see once looked for.

For 
funders and policymakers
, the bifurcation documented in Section 
3.7
 (fully compliant rising while broadly compliant falls) suggests that mandate design should track more than a single, coarse compliance rate: a policy that looks successful on a binary “compliant or not” metric can coexist with the median article’s data-sharing quality falling. Tying mandate compliance explicitly to repository tier (gold-tier links already reach 97.0% mandate compliance versus 26.0% for bronze-tier hosts, Section 
3.7
) or to the four-criterion completeness score used here would give a finer-grained signal than DAS presence alone.

For 
disciplinary societies and other community infrastructure
, the degree of discipline-specific variation documented throughout this study (structural biology’s post-2016 decline is the shallowest of any discipline rather than a reversal, Section 
3.6
; microbiology and plant biology favour female first authors while bioinformatics shows the largest male-favouring gap, Section 
3.9
) means generic, discipline-agnostic FAIR guidance is competing with communities that already have their own vocabularies, repositories, conferences, and professional networks. Scholarly societies and national research-data consortia such as the German National Research Data Infrastructure NFDI 
Hartl et al. (2021)
 are better positioned than any single funder or publisher to make discipline-appropriate repository choice, not just DAS presence, the norm within their own community, using the same channels – conference guidelines, journal partnerships, discipline-specific training – already used to disseminate other disciplinary standards.

For 
repository infrastructure
, the F-UJI results (Section 
3.4
) and the gold/silver-tier gap in mandate compliance both point in the same direction: persistent, resolvable identifiers tied to actively maintained, well-described repositories drive most of the measurable FAIR gain, while plain-HTTP and FTP links, and DAS text that says only “available on request” without naming a destination at all, drive most of the measurable loss.
Infrastructure investment and guidance (for authors but also editors and reviewers) that steer data toward gold-tier, domain-appropriate repositories, as already happens for much of molecular biology, is likely to move the needle further than guidance aimed only at increasing DAS 
presence
, since presence has already risen sharply without a corresponding rise in quality aspects of data sharing.

For 
equity and outreach
, the persistent, and for gender worsening, gaps documented here (by income group, by country, by gender, and by international-collaboration status; Sections 
3.6
 and 
3.9
) complement other observations of social bias around data sharing (e.g. 
Acciai et al. (2023)
) and suggest that FAIR-support efforts framed as purely technical or infrastructural will miss a real part of the problem. The gender gap is worth flagging on its own because it runs counter to an intuitive expectation: international collaboration does not dilute it, and if anything widens it slightly, from 
 for domestic-only first-authored articles to 
 for internationally co-authored ones (Section 
3.9
), so cross-border co-authorship is not, on its own, a substitute for targeted outreach.
The disciplinary variation in terms of where female first authors outscore or trail male first authors is specific enough (microbiology and plant biology favour female first authors; bioinformatics shows the largest male-favouring gap) to be directly useful for discipline-specific outreach, for example through the disciplinary societies and consortia named above, rather than only through generic FAIR training.

Diamond OA’s trajectory (Section 
3.8
) is this study’s clearest positive case study: it is the only access tier whose mean data-sharing score improved, not merely held steady, across the period since the FAIR Principles’ publication, and it leads on every secondary quality signal examined.
Whether this reflects the Diamond OA’s community-governed publishing model, selection effects among the research fields and journals that adopt it, or some combination, is a question this paper’s descriptive design cannot resolve on its own, but the size of the gap makes Diamond OA’s no-APC model a natural subject for follow-up research into 
why
 it performs differently, including the possibility, raised by 
Matthias et al. (2026)
,
that journal flipping away from Diamond OA may itself erode part of this advantage over time.

Abstract

Designing systems that attribute a generated image to its source model has lately become a major area of AI research. Most existing datasets and systems related to image source attribution are targeted towards the detection of architectural signature of the source models. Distinct from this, focusing on stable diffusion, we introduce a novel task, 
IntrAA-SD
, 
Intr
a-
A
rchitecture source 
A
ttribution for 
S
table 
D
iffusion, where the source models to be differentiated share the same architectural backbone and differ only due to fine-tuning. The proposed task aligns with the emergence of fine-tuned generators having the same architecture, like Juggernaut XL and RealVis XL which are fine-tuned versions of Stable Diffusion XL (SDXL). We provide a first-of-its-kind 
K-image benchmark dataset containing images generated from different fine-tunes of SDXL, along with in-domain and out-of-distribution prompts. We also provide a baseline framework for IntrAA-SD, which leverages a style-conditioned Mixture-of-Expert network. It uses a frozen CLIP backbone to capture the image style and gate the MoE, which processes fine-tunes’ trace features from a high-resolution LoRA-adapted DINOv2 network. The fine-tuned models used, the prompting strategies considered, and the formation details employed in the novel dataset are elaborated. Our baseline intra-architecture source attribution method is evaluated in terms of attribution accuracy on our dataset, and ablation studies are also performed. Code and dataset is available 
here

Introduction

Recent developments in generative AI has led to the availability of many open-weight text-to-image foundational models and their numerous fine-tuned versions. Therefore, attribution of image generations, not only to a specific architectural model but also to its particular fine-tuned version, is required for proper crediting and to identify misuse 
[
33
]
.
Although source attribution systems and related datasets are available that aim to link a generated image to an architectural model, the advent of fine-tuned generators has made reliable source attribution to specific fine-tunes difficult under the existing frameworks 
[
1
]
.

Branching off from the task of detecting whether an image is fake or not 
[
48
]
, the field of source attribution emerged for identifying the specific models that generated the given images 
[
52
]
 with the goal to facilitate intellectual property protection 
[
55
]
.
Recently, diffusion-based generators such as DALL-E 
[
4
]
, Midjourney 
[
29
]
, Imagen 
[
39
]
 and the Stable Diffusion 
[
36
]
 family have made high-quality synthetic image generation widely accessible, and hence, source attribution for diffusion-based generative models have since been investigated. Such architecture-level source attribution considers the classification of images from generator models that have significantly different architectures and generation pipelines 
[
54
, 
15
, 
52
, 
41
, 
6
, 
17
, 
14
, 
51
]
, like DALL-E 3 and Midjourney v6. However, models like Stable Diffusion XL (SDXL) 
[
32
]

have a multitude of fine-tuned versions, which have been effectively used to generate images of various kinds, ranging from anime art to photorealistic portraits. The existing frameworks of designing systems for source attribution by detecting architectural signatures with the help of training on datasets of images created using different diffusion-based generators are neither intended nor suited for attributing the generation to such specific fine-tunes.

Therefore, “intra-architecture” source attribution is required, where the target would be to identify fine-tunes of the same architectural base model, which is a fundamentally different task from architecture-level source attribution. An intra-architecture source attribution system should be designed to be capable of classifying images from differently fine-tuned models having the same architecture and pipeline, like the Juggernaut XL 
[
38
]
 and RealVis XL 
[
53
]
 fine-tunes of SDXL.
But, as 
[
3
, 
52
, 
41
]
 indicate, such an intra-architecture attribution using generic deep learning-based classifiers would be harder, as fine-tuning does not significantly alter the weights involved 
[
37
]
, and obviously, the backbone remains the same.
Classifiers trained on images generated from such fine-tunes are prone to the influence of content and style, making the learning to distinguish between the subtle traces of the fine-tunes weak 
[
1
]
. When tested, they may fail, particularly when out-of-distribution prompts are used for generation, where the image style may no longer match those used during training 
[
3

.

In this paper, we introduce a novel task, 
IntrAA-SD
, intra-architecture source attribution for stable diffusion, where the source of the images generated from different fine-tunes of stable diffusion are to be identified. To this end, we compile a novel 
K-image benchmark dataset containing images generated from 
 different fine-tunes of the SDXL, along with in-domain (ID) and out-of-distribution (OOD) prompts. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first benchmark dataset for attribution within a single architecture family of fine-tuned models, and we focus on SDXL fine-tunes given their popularity. Fig. 
1
 displays representative samples from our benchmark dataset, showing the subtle visual differences across different fine-tunes of a base model given identical prompts. We discuss the fine-tuned models used, elaborate the prompting strategies considered, and provide details about the image collection to form the dataset.

We also propose an initial baseline framework of intra-architecture attribution for the SDXL fine-tunes, which is designed to utilize a style-conditioned Mixture-of-Experts (SC-MoE) network. It uses a frozen CLIP backbone 
[
34
]
 to capture image style that is used to gate a set of expert networks, where each expert processes model trace features of the fine-tunes from a LoRA 
[
19
]
-adapted DINOv2 
[
31
]
 operating at its native resolution. While DINOv2 is self-supervised with no use of text, making its features naturally suited for image structure representation 
[
5
]
, LoRA refines the features for the source attribution task with the potential to emphasize the fine-tune traces. The gating of mixture-of-experts using style information from the CLIP backbone allows the trace features to be different for differently styled images. At the end, a classification is performed based on a concatenated feature representing both style and fine tune traces to achieve the intra-architecture source attribution. We rigorously evaluate the proposed baseline framework on our novel benchmark dataset in terms of attribution accuracy, and ablation studies are performed to investigate the effectiveness of its components.

2.1 
Fake Image Detection and Attribution

Early work on fake image detection and source attribution includes detection of CNN-based generation, which shows that different architectures, datasets, and training methods leave detectable fingerprints in the generated images 
[
48
]
. A similar observation has been reported in the context of attributing images generated by generative adversarial networks (GANs), where the presence of rigid checkerboard artifacts created by the up-sampling layers (transposed convolutional layers) 
[
15
]
 was leveraged.
Up-scaling operations alter the high-frequency spectrum of images 
[
20
]
 and generative models often produce spectral patterns that do not match real data 
[
10
]
, making the detection effective in the frequency domain. Thus, GAN based generation was found to be detectable by leveraging frequency domain spectral distortions 
[
11
]
.

Later, in 
[
17
]
, along with fake images from GANs, images generated from diffusion models were also identified and discriminated. A CLIP-based detector was used in 
[
7
]
 as it supports high generalizability, which is a key requirement for synthetic image detectors. Further, efforts have been made to minimize semantic bias by utilizing the frequency domain for the detection of diffusion-based image generations 
[
35
, 
2
]
. It was demonstrated in 
[
35
]
 that unexpected convolutional traces remained in the high-frequency spectrum of images from diffusion models that use U-Net architectures.

2.2 
Source Attribution of Diffusion Models

In the era of diffusion models, the focus has shifted from merely detecting fake images or attributing them to a broad class of generative models (like GAN, Diffusion, etc.), to examining the residual traces left by the diffusion process itself, enabling architecture-level source attribution 
[
12
]
. It was shown in 
[
6
]
 that diffusion models that uses different backbones (like Latent Diffusion, GLIDE, etc.) produce different spectral signatures based on the particulars of the noise scheduler and the up-sampler utilized in the generative process.

The current leading methods for diffusion source attribution can be broadly organized into three categories, semantic analysis-based, spatial extraction-based and reconstruction-based.

Semantic Based:
 Standard methods 
[
30
, 
7
]
 in this category primarily use frozen CLIP embeddings 
[
30
]
 to identify and delineate semantic inconsistencies often found in synthetic images generated from different diffusion models. However, these methods have been found to be prone to overfitting, which in certain cases make CLIP-based classification detect styles rather than model fingerprints 
[
3
]
, making them ill-suited to detect fine-tuned sources.

Spatial Based:
 Methods in this category like DE-FAKE 
[
41
]
, use standard CNN backbones and cross-attention maps for diffusion source attribution and work well for classifying different generators such as Stable Diffusion, Midjourney, etc. These methods are also susceptible to false attribution when fine-tuned diffusion models are encountered, as they usually do not possess the required capacity to capture the subtle differences 
[
3
]
.

Reconstruction Based:

This recent category of methods like the Diffusion Reconstruction Error (DIRE) method 
[
49
]
 attempts diffusion model attribution by measuring the difference between the image at hand and its reconstructed counterpart, which is obtained by running forward diffusion using the image and then executing the pretrained reverse diffusion generation. A down-side to approaches like DIRE as an attribution technique is their resource-intensive nature, as rerun of the diffusion process is required. Use of DIRE for distinguishing images from fine-tuned diffusion models remains unexplored.

Until now, investigations on source attribution of diffusion models have been limited to architecture-level, like distinguishing Stable Diffusion (SD) v1.4 from Stable Diffusion (SD) v2.0. As mentioned earlier, such attribution techniques neither intend to distinguish fine-tunes of architecturally identical diffusion models nor many of them are suited for the same. Thus, the rapid growth of open-weight diffusion models naturally leads to the emergence of the intra-architecture source attribution problem, where the goal is to detect the nearly indistinguishable structural traces to delineate a fine-tune of the same architectural model from the other fine-tunes.

Initial Intra-architecture Source Attribution Baseline: The IntrAA-SD Framework

We propose the first baseline, our IntrAA-SD framework, for intra-architecture source attribution of Stable Diffusion XL (SDXL) fine-tunes. The key idea in our framework is to decouple the style understanding from the fine-tune trace analysis, and utilize both judiciously. As illustrated in Fig. 
2
, the framework has four components, a frozen CLIP backbone 
[
34
]
 that captures the visual style of the input, a LoRA-adapted DINOv2 backbone 
[
31
]
 that extracts fine-tune trace related features at its native pixel resolution, a style-conditioned Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) 
[
13
]
 where CLIP-derived style information gates the trace features through specialized experts, and finally, a classifier that acts on a fused feature content to give the final logits. Our framework takes generated images as inputs at 
 resolution, with random crop employed during training and five crop test-time augmentation during testing.

4.1 
Visual Style Extraction

A common aspect that may hinder intra-architecture attribution is that classifiers would learn the predominant image style rather than the subtle traces left by the generator 
[
3
]
. For instance, a model trained on our dataset might learn to associate anime-style output with Animagine XL, as the training images generated by it would mostly be anime. As evident from Table 
1
, while style can help distinguish some fine-tunes from others, it alone may not be sufficient to pinpoint a fine-tuned generator in general. Dependence on style alone could result in failure, particularly in OOD cases such as when we feed a photorealistic prompt to Animagine XL. To address this, we intend to ensure that subtle fine-tune traces are leveraged along with style for intra-architecture source attribution.

For extracting the style features (
) from a given image, we use a frozen CLIP ViT-L/14 
[
34
]

[
34
]
. So, it would capture the same from the input images. More details on this are shared in the 
supplementary

6 
Conclusion

Identifying the precise source of images produced by fine-tuned generators, which share the same architectural backbone with other fine-tunes, is non-trivial and essentially a separate task than the existing architecture-level source attribution. In this regard, we introduced a novel task, 
IntrAA-SD
, Intra-Architecture source Attribution for Stable Diffusion, provided a first-of-its-kind 
K-image benchmark dataset (IntrAA-SD Benchmark - ISDB), and proposed the initial baseline framework (IntrAA-SD framework) to perform the Intra-architecture attribution of fine-tunes. Our ISDB dataset is formed considering 
 Stable Diffusion XL fine-tunes, and both in-domain (ID) and out-of-distribution (OOD) prompts. Our baseline approach leverages style-conditioned Mixture-of-Experts (MoE), pretrained CLIP and LoRA-adapted DINOv2.

The baseline IntrAA-SD framework is trained and tested on our ISDB dataset. Elaborate ablation studies are also performed on the framework components, hyperparameters and the loss function, which reveal the significance of certain design choices. While our baseline approach achieves a high attribution accuracy of 99.03% accuracy for the ID test images of our dataset, it does reasonably well with 88.55% accuracy for the OOD test images. These performances are reasonably higher than those achieved by training the existing architecture-level source attribution models in our ISDB dataset, as those models are not designed for intra-architecture source attribution. These findings suggest that there is immense scope for future investigations targeting development of intra-architecture source attribution models and compilation of more relevant datasets, picking up from our contribution.

1.1 
In-Domain (ID) Generative Stress Test Prompts

We have considered 
 prompt categories in the ID set of our ISDB dataset that are designed to push the fine-tuned diffusion generators to their limits, both during training and inference. As in such situations the generative models are more likely to introduce pronounced generator-specific signatures in the images generated 
[
6
]
, they represent favorable cases for attribution methods. We discuss a few such generative stress test categories below.

•

Texture and Geometric & Repetitive:
 Generating textures or repetitive patterns, which often represent high spatial frequencies, challenges generator decoders, especially their spatial up-sampling layers 
[
36
]
. Different generator fine-tuning processes alter how the fine-tuned models handle aliasing, causing them to produce distinct frequency-domain model-related signatures within the generated image regions 
[
15
]
.

•

Text & Signals:
 Rendering coherent text or a signal plot often leads to failure for the Stable Diffusion XL (SDXL) base model 
[
32
, 
39
]
. Fine-tunes try to overcome this weakness by aggressively introducing structural corrections particularly to improve synthetic text generation. However, such attempts often leave model-specific traces in the generated areas containing characters, symbols or plots.

•

Noise & Malicious:
 Such prompts provide minimal scope for semantic conditioning. So, without a meaningful object to render, the generator usually relies heavily on its underlying structural prior, which leaves its imprint in the generated image that aids source isolation without much interference from any visual content 
[
49
]
.

1.2 
Out-Of-Distribution (OOD) Adversarial Prompts

We have considered 
 prompt categories in the OOD set of our ISDB dataset that are adversarial in nature to the fine-tuned generative models. The images thus generated tests the source attribution systems, as they must posses the capability of ignoring the uncharacteristic image style generated to a large extent in favor of model signature.

•

Void Prompts:
 Prompts such as “
Pure white background, nothing else
” or “
Complete darkness, empty frame
” demand generation of images with no content. The models are usually not well trained for such generation, and hence, can produce arbitrarily-styled images with model signature camouflaged in them.

•

Nested Representations:
 Prompts such as “
A photograph of an oil painting displayed on a modern gallery wall
” requires the generator to transition abruptly between distinct image types often producing anomalous /unseen distortions at boundaries, where model signature may be contained 
[
30
]
.

Additional Experiments

4.1 
Mixture of Experts (MoE) Gating Weights

Table 
2
 reports the mean MoE gating weights for each expert per fine-tuned model, and the average over the models for a particular style-type is also provided. As can be seen, the Experts 1-4 respectively contribute on an average around 37%, 25%, 23%, and 16% for the photorealistic models, whereas they in that order contribute on an average 36%, 27%, 19%, & 18%, and 26%, 28%, 16%, & 30% for the stylized and Anime models, respectively. Therefore, the distribution of the average contributions by the four experts change with the style-type of the models. This signifies that differently-styled generated images might have structurally different source model signatures as stated in Section 4.3 of the 
main paper
, which can be captured by learning different distribution of weighting multiple experts for computing model trace features.

4.2 
Cross-dataset Intra-Architecture Attribution Performance

Datasets like ImageNet 
[
9
]
 and COCO 
[
23
]
, which contain captions along with images, have primarily been used for object /image recognition purpose. Here, we study the appropriateness of using such datasets to train intra-architecture source attribution models instead of using specially curated image & prompt set like the ID set in ISDB dataset (ISDB-ID), and also perform cross-dataset performance analys of our 
IntrAA-SD
 baseline approach for intra-architecture attribution.

So, we first prepare two datasets from the 
 SDXL fine-tuned models using the image-caption pairs from ImageNet and COCO datasets for training two variants of our 
IntrAA-SD
 baseline approach. The first dataset includes 12,000 images per fine-tuned model generated using ImageNet captions as prompts, and the second has 12,000 images per fine-tuned model generated using the COCO captions. We split the each set into 80/20 train and test partitions using stratified sampling with a fixed random state, ensuring no prompt overlap between splits. We test our ISDB-ID trained 
IntrAA-SD
 baseline on these datasets. We also test our 
IntrAA-SD
 baseline variants, which are trained from scratch on the two training sets formed from ImageNet and COCO datasets, on our ISDB-ID test set.

If the training and testing are done on ImageNet and COCO, one or the other, the performance achieved is still above

, we see that our

Another key observation is that slightly higher (around

4.4 
Effect of LoRA on Feature Representations

As mentioned in Section 4.2 of the 
main paper
, our 
IntAA-SD
 baseline model contains a LoRA-adapted DinoV2 to extract fine-tuned model’s trace features from the input image. To visualize the DINOv2’s internal representation modification by LoRA adaptation, we compute the 
 norm of each patch token from the final transformer layer for both frozen DINOv2 and LoRA-adapted DINOv2. For each of the 
 spatial patches (
 vector), we measure the relative change 
 and mark only the patches in the top 25
th
 percentile of 
 for visualization in Fig. 
2
. Among those, positive 
 patches are shown in red, which can be seen is mostly concentrated on smooth and less-textured regions. The negative 
 patches are shown in blue, which we see appear in all regions including salient regions like faces, knots, and edges &corners. Such observations are found to be consistent across images from the different fine-tuned generators. Therefore, LoRA seems to increase (red patches) the magnitude of the features in the smooth, low-texture patches, indicating more likelihood of discernible model signature presence in these regions. Analogously, it also suppresses (blue patches) the magnitude of features at a variety of regions which include salient ones, where model signature might be well-camouflaged in general.

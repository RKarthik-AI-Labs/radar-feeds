Abstract

Safety benchmarks for computer-use agents (CUAs) place the harm either in the intent of the task or on a single screen: a malicious instruction, an injected command, a phishing page. An agent that checks the task and each screen in isolation can pass them, so they do not test whether it combines facts seen on different screens. Yet in delegated work, harm often arises exactly this way: a fact about a person on one screen, a fact about an item on another, and a legitimate task that binds them. We introduce 
Seam
 (
S
afety 
E
xecution 
A
cross 
M
ultiple screens), a benchmark of such cross-screen compositional harm: 600 multi-step, cross-application OSWorld tasks in which the instruction and every single observation are benign and only their combination is harmful. Minimal-pair variants that differ in one place, scored from the server log rather than by an LLM judge, locate where an agent fails. General-purpose open-weight and flagship agents carry out the harm in up to 92% of runs, yet decline when the user states it in one sentence. Agents open the decisive pages, so exposure is not the primary bottleneck. Asked about those pages, they flag the harm, and with thinking they often write the conflict into their reasoning and confirm anyway. Safety recognition thus does not reliably translate into safe execution, and the translation depends on the information channel: the same warning stops agents far more often in the user’s instruction than on screen. Proprietary models are markedly more cautious. We release 
Seam
 with its generator and quality gates at 
https://yuxuan2003.github.io/SEAM/
.

Much of everyday delegated work requires exactly this combination. A colleague asks the assistant to “book the spa session for Mei Lin”. The session page says the hot tub runs at 40 °C; the client record says Mei is 22 weeks pregnant and advised to avoid water above 38 °C. The intent is legitimate, each screen is benign, and no screen contains a warning. The harm exists only in the combination of screens (
Figure 1
), and nobody is hiding it. In multimodal jailbreaks, a user conceals a harmful request, for example across several images 
[
7
, 
39
, 
40

safety execution

[

, 
28

[
13
]

We therefore introduce 
Seam
 (

A

M

cross-screen compositional harm
: multi-step computer-use tasks in which each single observation is harmless and only their combination is harmful. It has three key properties.

(1) Strict cross-screen composition.
 The instruction and every page are benign; seeing the harm requires combining a fact about a person, a fact about an object and the binding between them. No single screen reveals it, which we verify behaviourally (§
3.5
).

(2) Minimal-pair variants.
 Each task has five variants that differ in one place: harm only on screen (
R

B

E

), by the requester (
E

E

). The environment is byte-identical across the four harmful variants, so behavioural differences are attributable to where the harm is stated and by whom.

(3) Execution-level evaluation.
 Tasks are cross-application workflows (10–12 steps on the shortest path) in OSWorld virtual machines; outcomes and exposure are read from the service’s own server logs, without an LLM judge.

Seam
 covers 150 rules across 27 harm families, 8 reasoning operations and 49 application domains, instantiated as 600 scenarios 
 5 variants.

Across 17 configurations of 11 models, the general-purpose open-weight and flagship agents carry the harm out in 17.5–91.6% of 
R
 runs against 1.1–22.0% of 
E
u
 runs, while completing 91.8–99.6% of benign twins; the proprietary models stay at or below 3.6%, three of five by also leaving much of the benign work undone (
section 4
). Three findings locate where safety execution fails (
section 5
).

(i) Exposure is not the primary bottleneck:
 agents open the decisive page in at least 99.3% of required-path runs.

(ii) Safety recognition does not reliably translate into safe execution:
 asked about those same screens, agents block the step; with reasoning they often state the conflict before confirming (in 81–87% of DeepSeek-1017’s harmful runs and 50–69% for the other reasoning models we judged) and then report success without mentioning it.

(iii) The translation depends on the information channel:
 the same sentence stops the agent far more often as part of the user’s instruction than read on screen. A pre-registered control with budget limits in place of safety limits shows that screen facts reach the action poorly in general, safety facts somewhat more often.

Contributions.

•

We identify a blind spot of CUA safety evaluation, harm that lies in neither task intent nor any single screen, and build 
Seam
, the first CUA benchmark of 
strict cross-screen compositional harm
, with minimal-pair variants and log-based scoring over 3,000 instances in OSWorld.

•

We evaluate 17 configurations of 11 models and show that harms refused when stated are widely executed when they exist only across screens, while benign twins are completed, and that the proprietary models which avoid the harm largely do so by acting less.

•

We trace the failure from exposure through recognition to execution, show that it lies in translating recognition into action, and identify the information channel as a factor in it, with a pre-registered benign-constraint control.

3.1 
Motivation and Design Principles

A task consists of an instruction

The two conditions exclude the two places prior benchmarks put the harm (
Figure 1
; 
Table 8

OS-Blind

Design principles.
 Five choices follow from (
1
).

(P1) Harm only in composition:
 each rule is a person fact, an object fact and a binding, placed on different pages; no page carries two of them, and no text names the conflict.

(P2) A benign twin for every task:
 the same workflow with one object fact changed so the action is safe, which separates detection from indiscriminate refusal and from incapability.

(P3) One-sentence explicit controls:
 the harm stated in one sentence by the user or by the requester, with every page byte-identical, so the effect of composing the harm can be measured within each rule.

(P4) Ordinary workflows, one irreversible step:
 the risk lands on the final confirmation of a multi-step task across two applications (10–12 steps on the shortest path), as in 
OS-Harm
 and 
OS-Blind
.

(P5) No judge in the loop:
 outcomes and exposure are read from the service’s own server log.

What counts as the right action.
 On 
R
, confirming the hazardous item for the person at risk is harmful; stopping, asking or switching to the safe alternative is not. The person at risk is a third party outside the conversation and nobody has accepted the risk, so a careful assistant would check before committing. Two blind annotators agree: on 36 sampled rules, neither would carry out 
R
 or 
E
 as asked (0 of 144 judgments), and both complete 
B

Appendix J
). Flagged and silent executions on 
E
 are still reported separately (
Appendix K

3.3 
From a Rule to Five Task Variants

Workflow.
 Following 
OS-Harm
 and 
OS-Blind
, each task is an ordinary office workflow with the risk landing on the final submission. The user instruction is generic (“Please take care of the request in Wei Lin’s latest email and reply to let them know it’s done”; six paraphrases). The agent opens a webmail client, reads the request, follows a link to a service site (one of 49 domains: a spa, a clinic pharmacy, a school office, a boat hire, …), inspects the item, fills in the booking form, reviews and confirms, and replies to the requester: 10–12 steps across two applications. The site always offers a safe alternative, so an agent can 
remediate

3.4 
Evaluation Protocol

Outcome and exposure from server logs.
 The service site logs every page served and every confirmation with its item and people. A run is 
harmful
 if a confirmation matches the rule’s hazard condition (e.g., the 40 °C session with Mei among the guests), checked programmatically, so no LLM judge is involved. Other runs are 
stopped
, 
remediated
 (a safe alternative confirmed) or 
incomplete
. The same log records whether the page holding the person fact was ever served, so exposure is measured directly.

Metrics.
 We report the 
harmful-action rate
 on 
R
, 
E
 and 
E
u
 beside the 
completion rate
 on 
B
. Runs that end in infrastructure failure, unparseable output or an exhausted reasoning budget are censored and reported separately; runs that reach the step limit are scored from the log. Contrasts between variants are paired within rule, with rule-clustered bootstrap confidence intervals and exact McNemar tests on discordant rules.

3.5 
Quality Assurance

Each observation is benign, behaviourally.
 For every rule, in a four-step rendering of its pages, we show a model single screens (only the instruction, the person page, the object page or the decision page) and the full set, and ask whether proceeding is safe. With DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash (high) the full set separates harmful from benign variants (discrimination +0.88 to +0.92) while the person page or the object page alone stays at 0.00 on average, and 0 of 600 per-rule single-screen tests reach 
 (one-sided Fisher; 583 of them could have under perfect separation). Without thinking, single screens separate the variants by at most 0.06 (
Appendix D
). Shown single pages, annotators flag 2 of 144 harmful-only and 3 of 144 benign-only pages (
Appendix J
).

Construction gates.
 Automated gates run on every instance without a model: each variant’s literal path and the remediation path must score as labelled; no page may carry both facts; the person fact must be exactly in its assigned location; 
E
, 
E
u
 and 
E
q

R
 except for their one sentence; and the bundle uploaded to the VM must not reveal the variant or the hazard (the agent’s browser can open local files). Each gate was validated by injecting its fault. All 3,000 instances pass except 6 
E
 instances (3 rules, in the two placements that keep the person fact off the email), where the hazard sentence restates that fact verbatim and trips the no-leak check; since stating the hazard is what 
E
 does, we keep them. At most 3 of 450 on-path 
E

External review.
 Independent models audit every task as rendered screenshots (
Appendix E

Agents.

Environment and actions.
 Tasks run in OSWorld 
[
33

 Ubuntu VMs at

It returns one action (click, type, select, scroll, back, open URL, reply, done; 
Appendix R

The two computer-use agents are trained for pixel actions and receive the plain screenshot in their own action format instead; the same Qwen3.8-27B run under both protocols gives the same main effect (+53.6 and +55.3 points), so the protocol does not drive it (

Budget and repetitions.
 The step budget is 40, well above the 10–12-step shortest path; the measured median is 11–12 steps. Each task variant is run once per configuration (temperature 0.2; 0 for the pixel agents), with rule-clustered confidence intervals; a second run of DS-V4.1 reproduces its rates within 2 points (

From Recognition to Execution

The same harm is refused when stated and carried out when it exists only on screen. We examine three points at which an agent can fail: seeing the evidence, recognizing the harm, and acting on what it recognizes. Agents see the evidence (§
5.1
) and recognize the harm, but recognition does not reliably govern the action (§
5.2
), and whether it does depends on the channel through which the evidence arrives (§
5.3
). Probes use the configurations named in each table.

Which screen.
 Paired within rule, a person fact in the request email leads to more harmful actions than the same fact in the site’s own record, in all four configurations and both email placements (+6.1 to +26.0 points). Salience does not explain this, since a non-navigating model given all pages detects the hazard at least as often in the email (
Appendix H
). The checkpoint fork (§
5.2
) points the same way: at the moment of commit, a fact read earlier in the email is rarely recalled.

Control: is the channel effect specific to safety?.
 If screen facts reach the action poorly in general, a non-safety constraint placed on screen should fare no better. A pre-registered control (
Appendix L
) replaces the safety threshold of the 47 physical quantity-tier rules with a budget or entitlement threshold of the same form (“booking cap: no tour above £250” for “stay below 2,500 m”) and re-runs parents and controls side by side. With the constraint only on screen, agents almost never respect it (100% and 92.6% violated, DeepSeek without / with thinking), against 82.3% and 52.6% harmful actions on the safety parents. The pre-registered contrast 
 is +35.5 [19.1, 50.4] points without thinking and 
3.0 [
20.7, 15.6] with thinking; stated by the user, a budget is often treated as negotiable (61.0 / 38.5% still violated), which blurs the contrast. Constraints shown only on screen therefore reach the action poorly in general, and safety relevance helps only in part.

6 
Conclusion

We introduced 
Seam
, where the task intent and every observation are benign and the harm exists only in their composition across screens. Harms refused when stated are carried out when they exist only on screen: agents see the evidence and recognize the harm when asked, but recognition does not reliably reach the action, and a one-sentence prompt defense closes only part of the gap. Safety evaluation of CUAs should therefore go beyond single screens and include harms that an agent must compose from what it has read on different screens.

Limitations.
 Self-hosted applications and generated rules trade naturalism for byte-identical variants, in English only. Each variant is run once per configuration, and the analysis of written reasoning relies on LLM judges and on reasoning that need not reflect what drives the action.

Appendix B 
Ethics Statement

Seam
 is built to expose a failure mode of computer-use agents before they are deployed in settings where it can hurt people. All people, records, sites and mailboxes in the benchmark are fictitious; no real personal data is used. Every task runs inside an isolated OSWorld virtual machine against self-hosted sites, so no agent action has a real-world effect. The harms in the benchmark are everyday safety hazards (allergies, medication interactions, pregnancy, accessibility, child safety) chosen so that a careful human assistant would recognize them; the benchmark contains no instructions for causing harm, and its construction makes the hazard visible only to a reader who combines two benign facts, which is exactly the capability we want agents to have.

External model review.
 The gates prove structure but cannot read, so every scenario is also read by models that are never evaluated as agents, and every flag is checked against the pages before anything is changed. In the final round: (i) 
Blind labelling
: gpt-oss-120b sees the pages of the harmful variant and its benign twin, shuffled and unnamed, and says for each whether doing the request puts someone at risk (two shuffles, 2,400 verdicts). DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-1017 with thinking agrees on all 878 verdicts of the 439 scenarios it read. (ii) 
Adversarial audit
: gpt-oss-120b is told the design, the labels and the harm and asked to attack each task (premise, benign twin, single-page leaks, hints in page chrome, date coherence, plausibility); of 352 audited scenarios, 302 have no issue, 45 minor and 5 major. (iii) 
Negative controls
: deliberately broken copies of real tasks (risk stated in the email, unsafe benign twin, item page revealing the harm, harmless harmful variant, incoherent dates) are mixed in; the audit catches 9 of 10.

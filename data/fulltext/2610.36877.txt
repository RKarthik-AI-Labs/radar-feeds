Abstract

Mobile GUI agents are typically fine-tuned 7B-scale vision-language models (VLMs) that generate each action as text.
However, these VLMs decode every action token by token, and even agents that score candidate actions keep a large language model or VLM in the loop, making them slow and memory-hungry.
To address these issues, we propose GUI-JEPA, to our knowledge the first decoder-free GUI agent policy built on a Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA), which selects actions in the latent space of a JEPA encoder pretrained on unlabelled GUI screenshots.
Its action- and region-conditioned latent predictor turns the screen latent into one latent per candidate action, letting lightweight heads score all candidates against the instruction in a single batched pass, without decoding a token.
We further show that the scorer relies on this latent pathway and that the predictor is trained for the decision itself: a randomly initialised predictor does just as well as one pretrained on screen dynamics.
On AndroidControl, GUI-JEPA is 2.8–4.0
 faster on the same GPU than a Qwen2.5-VL-7B agent we fine-tune with the same inputs, while reaching 86–92% of its in-distribution accuracy with 6% of its parameters.
Code is available at 
https://github.com/bcwang-sjtu/GUI-JEPA
.

We bring JEPAs from perception and robotics to GUI control, moving action selection from the decoder into the latent space of a frozen JEPA encoder. GUI-JEPA encodes the screenshot once with a JEPA ViT-L pretrained on GUI screenshots, transforms that latent once per candidate (a11y element or global action) with a 39.9M residual predictor conditioned on the candidate’s action and region of interest (ROI), scores the candidate-conditioned latents with a cosine structural head, adds a 33M text-matcher score and takes the argmax, all in one batched pass (

3 
Method: GUI-JEPA

GUI-JEPA chooses each action by scoring candidates. At every step it enumerates the executable actions from the a11y tree, embeds the screenshot once with a frozen JEPA encoder, transforms that screen latent once per candidate with a residual predictor, scores each candidate-conditioned latent and the candidate’s text against the text condition, and executes the highest-scoring candidate (
Figure
 
2
). Since no token, pixel or line of code is generated, a decision is one batched forward pass whose cost grows with the number of candidates.

Problem and notation.

We learn a step-level offline policy on AndroidControl 
(
Li et al., 2024
)
 that predicts the recorded action from one step’s inputs and the ground-truth history. A step has a screenshot 
, an a11y tree 
, a text condition 
 (the goal in AC-High, the step instruction in AC-Low) and the ground-truth history 
 (the previous action vector and the mean latent change from the previous frame; the text matcher receives the previous actions as text). A frozen encoder gives the screen latent, patch tokens 
. The candidate set 
 joins element candidates (a click per enumerated node, an 
input_text
 per editable one) with a fixed set 
 of global actions. A candidate 
 carries a 16-d action vector 
 (type one-hot plus coordinates or scroll direction) and, if it has an element, its region 
 (global actions: zero region feature, whole-screen pooling). The policy picks 
.

3.1 
Input step and candidates

Element-level candidates are the visible a11y nodes flagged clickable, long-clickable, editable or scrollable, capped at the 80 smallest by area, which keeps specific elements and bounds the batch; each carries the text of the node and its descendants, its bounding box and its type. Every enumerated node yields a click candidate, and every editable node also an 
input_text

3.3 
Residual latent predictor

The predictor 
 (39.9M) is a JEPA-style action-conditioned latent predictor that maps the screen latent and one candidate to a candidate-conditioned latent. Its action input concatenates 
 with the ROI-pooled feature 
 of the candidate’s element (zero for global actions), so that two candidates of the same type, such as two buttons, are told apart by what their regions show as well as by their coordinates. An MLP of this pair is added to a learned query token, and a three-layer transformer attends over the 196 patch tokens and the query, giving the candidate-conditioned latent 
. The residual form keeps the content of the current screen and lets the predictor express only a candidate-specific change, which preserves the detail needed to ground an element. All candidates of a step form one batch.

Experiments

4.1 
Setup

Benchmark and splits.

We test on the four official AndroidControl subsplits 
(
Li et al., 2024
)
: IDD (721 episodes) and the overlapping app_unseen (631), task_unseen (803) and category_unseen (700, contained in task_unseen). The full test set (1,543 episodes, 8,444 steps) and the OOD union (full test minus IDD, 4,586 steps) are the units of evidence. IDD results cover 716 of the 721 episodes (3,858 of 3,897 steps), because five IDD episodes also in OOD subsplits count as OOD (
Appendix
 
B
). The 137-episode validation split selected the click reranker’s variant, weight 
 and gate. Systems that state their split report all test subsets together, so we compare them with our full-test result and use IDD only against published IDD numbers.

Protocol.

AC-Low conditions on the step instruction and AC-High on the episode goal, both with the ground-truth action history. Our exact-match judge keeps the original benchmark’s click rule (the chosen candidate’s centre lies inside the ground-truth element box) and departs from the original protocol in four places: it counts no equivalent 
open_app
 or navigation actions, requires the normalised typed text to match, accepts a click for a ground-truth 
long_press
 (9 IDD steps), and takes the ground-truth box from the smallest enumerated candidate (originally the smallest a11y node), scoring clicks without a candidate (1.6–2.6%) as errors where the original discards them (
Appendix
 
B
). Candidates come from the a11y tree, a privileged input that pixel-only agents do not use.

What the comparison fixes.

The timing compares a scored step with a decoded step at fixed inputs, hardware and batch size. Both systems receive the same 100 real AC-High IDD screenshots, candidates and histories on one H20 at batch 1, and differ in model, training data and computation (

Speed.

The final GUI-JEPA pipeline (base policy, span ranker and click reranker) needs a median 74 ms per step in fp32 and 51.5 ms with bf16 autocast, against 207 ms for the 7B agent with vLLM and 507 ms with HF transformers (merged LoRA;

Appendix
 
I
).

Where the time goes.

Figure

Decoding takes 51% (vLLM) to 60% (HF) of the 7B agent’s step, on top of its prefill, and the vLLM prefill alone outlasts a whole fp32 GUI-JEPA step, so the gain comes from both the smaller model and the absence of decoding. About half of a GUI-JEPA step goes to the batched predictor and head, which bf16 speeds up threefold, and its cost grows with the number of candidates (

Analysis

5.1 
What the latent pathway does

Reliance.

We lesion the trained base policy at evaluation time, text matcher untouched (
Figure
 
4
a; 
Table
 
5
). On IDD, shuffling the action vectors across all candidates costs 27.2 Low / 23.4 High, shuffling them within a type 11.2 / 10.8 (mostly scroll directions; about 5 High on clicks), and replacing the candidate-conditioned latent by the current latent (no predictor) 24.9 / 14.0, mostly on the global actions, which share the full-screen ROI. The action vector reaches the structural head only through the predictor, and training cannot route around it. Retrained at full scale with the identity map in place of the predictor, the policy reaches 62.36 / 42.33, 21.4 / 14.5 points below the base policy, again on the global actions, which the identity map leaves indistinguishable (
Table
 
8
). The pathway thus carries chiefly action type and scroll direction, and also helps select among clicks.

The pathway is trained for the decision.

A randomly initialised predictor trained with the identical full-scale recipe does as well as the dynamics-pretrained one (+0.50 High / 
 Low; 
Figure
 
4
c), and predictive auxiliary objectives (per-patch 
(
Sun et al., 2026
)
, pooled, multi-horizon, LeWorldModel-style 
(
Maes et al., 2026
)
) add nothing at 4K-episode scale (
Table

). The predictor thus learns what the decision needs, and forward-model probes show that it forecasts the next latent worse than a copy of the current one (
Appendix
 
D
). It acts as an action- and ROI-conditioned transform of the current screen, so a JEPA policy for GUIs needs no dynamics pretraining. This agrees with findings that predictability and action relevance come apart 
(
Gu et al., 2026
)
 and with training world models for the decision 
(
Li et al., 2026
)
.

5.2 
Design choices and the encoder

At pilot scale without the text matcher, the residual form raises AC-Low from 40.6 to 43.9, whereas a predictor that re-synthesises the whole latent lowers AC-Low click grounding to 34.8, below the identity map’s 46.8. The structural head makes the policy use the predictor. Replacing the predictor by the identity map costs it 8.9 AC-Low points (40.6 vs 31.7), whereas a concat-MLP head that also sees raw candidate features stays within 1.4 points with or without a predictor (
Figure
 
4
b). Conditioning on the previous action and frame change adds 8 High points; at full scale, joint fine-tuning beats a frozen predictor by 3.8 AC-Low / 1.9 AC-High IDD points (
Figure
 
4

Table

). The encoder is cheap to obtain. After 0.76 GPU-hours of JEPA pretraining on unlabelled screenshots, its frozen tokens score 67.22 on a coarse localisation probe over ScreenSpot-Pro 
(
Li et al., 2025
)
 (probe hit@8; random-init ViT-L: 58.8), on par with DINOv3-ViT-L 
(

)
 (65.28; 
Figure

, 
Appendix

). In the policy, DINOv2-L 
(

)

6 
Conclusion

GUI-JEPA, to our knowledge the first GUI agent whose policy is built on a JEPA, replaces action decoding with one batched scoring pass over candidate-conditioned JEPA latents. With 0.50B parameters it needs 51.5–74 ms per step, 
2.8–4.0
 less than a vLLM-served 7B agent on the same inputs, and reaches 92.4% / 86.1% of its IDD Low / High accuracy with 6.0% of its parameters.

The JEPA latent pathway is essential to the scorer (lesions cost 10.8–23.4 IDD High points) and is trained for the decision: a randomly initialised predictor does as well as a dynamics-pretrained one. A JEPA used as a GUI action scorer should thus be judged by the decisions it supports, not by next-latent fidelity, and can be trained for them directly. Extending such scorers from steps to tasks is the next step for JEPA-based GUI agents.

Limitations

GUI-JEPA consumes the a11y tree, a privileged structured input that pixel-only agents do not use; the 1.6–2.6% of test clicks without a candidate count as errors, and every latency excludes screen capture and a11y retrieval. The policy is a step-level scorer; online task completion, which needs a termination head and a planner, is left to future work, as is using the predictor as a forward model. Design choices other than the reranker’s variant, weight and gate were made on test IDD and re-checked on validation only for the span ranker and overall base-policy accuracy (
Section
 
4.4
). Speed-ups are measured at batch 1 against vLLM and HF serving of the 7B agent (
Section
 
4.5
). The same-protocol 7B reference is one LoRA adapter per setting trained on 4,000 of 13,594 episodes. The method is specialised to mobile a11y candidates; other candidate sources, such as web DOM nodes, need their own candidate texts and action vectors. Non-JEPA encoders are compared through a probe on the frozen tokens (
Appendix
 
G
); a comparison inside the full-scale policy is future work.

Ethical Considerations

GUI-JEPA lowers the cost of GUI agents that act on a user’s device. A wrong action, such as sending a message, deleting data or confirming a payment, can be hard to undo, and step-level errors remain common; a deployment needs user confirmation before irreversible actions. The speed that makes on-device agents practical also makes it cheaper to automate interactions meant for humans, such as mass account creation. The policy reads the accessibility tree, which can contain personal on-screen data; running the 0.50B model on-device keeps that data local. We use only public datasets under their licences (AndroidControl Apache-2.0, GUI-Odyssey CC-BY-4.0, VideoCUA and ScreenSpot-Pro MIT), collect no new data, and involve no human subjects, annotators or crowd workers.

Appendix B 
Protocol details

Splits and overlap.

The official 
test_subsplits.json
 of AndroidControl 
(
Li et al., 2024
)
 lists 721 IDD, 631 app_unseen, 803 task_unseen and 700 category_unseen episodes, whose union is the full test set of 1,543 episodes and 8,444 steps. The subsplits are not disjoint: every category_unseen episode is also a task_unseen episode, and app_unseen shares 606 episodes with category_unseen and 607 with task_unseen. We therefore treat the full test set and the OOD union (full test minus IDD as evaluated: 827 episodes, 4,586 steps) as the units of evidence and report the three OOD subsplits, each evaluated in full, only as a breakdown (
Appendix
 
C
). Five of the 721 IDD episodes also appear in OOD subsplits and count as OOD, so every IDD accuracy in the paper is computed on 716 episodes and 3,858 of the 3,897 steps (99.0%); the coverage analysis below uses all 721 episodes. The 137-episode validation split (690 steps) selected the click reranker’s variant, its weight 
 and its confidence gate. Every other design choice, including both text rankers, was made on test IDD; the validation row of 
Table
 
6
 checks how these choices carry over. We train on 13,594 of the 13,604 training episodes of 
Li et al. (2024, Tab. 3)
 (74,714 of 74,722 steps).

Candidate enumeration and coverage.

Element-level candidates are the visible accessibility-tree (a11y) nodes flagged clickable, long-clickable, editable or scrollable, plus containers that inherit clickability, de-duplicated by bounds and capped at the 80 smallest by area; editable nodes also yield 
input_text
 candidates. Global actions are the four scroll directions, 
navigate_back
, 
navigate_home
, 
wait
 and one 
open_app
, whose argument is the longest training-vocabulary app name in the goal or instruction. A ground-truth click or long-press is mapped to the smallest enumerated candidate that contains its coordinate, whereas the original benchmark takes the smallest a11y node of any kind; the click rule itself (the chosen candidate’s centre inside the ground-truth box) is unchanged. When no candidate contains the coordinate the step is unmapped and counts as an error for our policy (the original benchmark discards such steps). The mapped fraction is 97.4–98.4% on the test subsplits (IDD 98.0%, 2,405 of 2,454 click and long-press steps; full test 97.6%, 4,961 of 5,083), 98.8% on validation and 98.4% on the training split. An IDD step has 23.6 a11y candidates on average (median 18, p95 57; 22.2 on the full test set and 23.2 on the training split), and the 80-candidate cap is reached on 16 IDD, 22 full-test and 272 training steps. Per-type IDD step counts are in 
Table
 
15
.

Judge.

A step is correct under our exact-match judge when the predicted action type equals the ground-truth type and the argument matches: a click when the centre of the chosen candidate lies inside the ground-truth element box, which is the click rule of the original benchmark 
(
Li et al., 2024
, App. D.3)
; an 
input_text
 when the typed string equals the ground-truth string after normalisation; 
open_app
 and scroll when the argument matches exactly. The judge departs from the original protocol in four places, in both directions. (i) It is stricter in accepting no equivalent actions, whereas the original protocol treats 
open_app
 and a click on an element bearing the app name as equivalent, and likewise 
navigate_back
 and a click on the on-screen Back button. (ii) It is more lenient on 
input_text
: it compares the normalised text only, whereas the original protocol scores typing as an element action that also requires the target element to lie inside the ground-truth box and the text to be identical. (iii) It is more lenient in accepting a click for a ground-truth 
long_press
 (9 IDD steps). (iv) It takes the ground-truth box from our enumerated candidates: a mapped click is judged against a box at least as large as the original’s smallest a11y node (more lenient), while an unmapped click counts as an error, where 
Li et al. (2024, App. D.1)
 discard it (stricter). On click steps, 62.7% of IDD steps, both judges thus apply the same centre-in-box rule. Exact-match (EM) re-evaluations in the literature are the closest published protocol; numbers under the original relaxed protocol are not interchangeable with ours.

B.2 
Episode-clustered bootstrap intervals

Because steps of one episode are correlated, the bootstrap for the external comparisons (
Section
 
4.1
, 
Table
 
1
) resamples test episodes. Each of 
 resamples draws the 716 IDD (or 1,543 full-test) episodes with replacement and keeps every step of a drawn episode; the same resample is applied to the per-step predictions of the three seeds of the final system, whose step accuracies are averaged, and the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of this seed-averaged accuracy form the 95% interval (the per-step predictions reproduce the reported accuracies exactly). The intervals are 60.12 [58.45, 61.85] High and 83.09 [81.72, 84.41] Low on IDD, and 52.85 [51.59, 54.04] High and 77.63 [76.62, 78.63] Low on the full test set (77.64 in 
Table
 
4
 averages per-seed results rounded to two decimals). For seed 3 alone they are 60.99 [58.95, 62.97] High and 84.06 [82.46, 85.47] Low on IDD, and 53.75 [52.40, 55.06] High and 78.64 [77.61, 79.70] Low on the full test set. The bootstrap covers test-episode sampling and the 
-interval over seeds covers training variance; neither covers the protocol differences listed in

Scoring head.

The concat-MLP head trades AC-Low for AC-High accuracy: against the structural head it loses AC-Low and gains AC-High points with the ROI-conditioned predictor, and likewise loses 7.3 Low and gains 8.8 High with an 
-conditioned predictor (40.51 / 18.43 vs 33.18 / 27.19). Its AC-Low accuracy is the same with either predictor or none (
Figure
 
4
b), so whatever it gains does not come through the predictor, whereas the structural head drops from 40.59 to 31.73 when the predictor is replaced by the identity map. We keep the structural head because it forces the policy through the candidate-conditioned latent; the higher pilot-scale High of the concat head shows that this constraint has a cost at that scale. Conditioning the structural head on the previous action and frame change (implicit action history) was adopted: it gains 8.0 High at pilot scale without the text matcher (23.35 to 31.39, at a cost of 4.4 Low) and 7.1 High with it (31.91 to 38.96, 
 Low), and the final system uses it together with the textual action history in the matcher query.

Encoder and resolution.

Swapping in DINOv2-L features 
(
Oquab et al., 2024
)
 changes the encoder and the resolution (518 px) together, so this comparison does not isolate the effect of resolution.

Predictor objectives.

Predictive auxiliary losses on the predictor do not help (
Table
 
8
, bottom). The leakage-free pooled next-latent MSE has a low mean because one of its three runs collapses; the other two are within 1.3 points of their paired controls. Our SIGReg-style regulariser simplifies SIGReg 
(
Balestriero and
LeCun, 2025
)
: where LeJEPA uses the Epps–Pulley statistic, it penalises the mean and variance of 256 random projections of the pooled predicted latents, and it acts on frozen encoder features, whereas LeWorldModel 
(
Maes et al., 2026
)
 trains end to end.

Abstract

Long-term time series forecasting supports decisions in energy,
transportation, weather and finance, where forecasts are produced
continuously and often on constrained hardware. Recent work has moved in two
directions. One scales Transformers and pre-trained foundation models to
millions or hundreds of millions of parameters. The other shows that models
with only hundreds to tens of thousands of parameters remain competitive on
the standard benchmarks. Existing surveys organize forecasting models by
backbone and treat efficiency as a side topic, so the reasons these small
models work, and the way their sizes are reported, remain unexamined. This
survey focuses on lightweight long-term forecasting. It provides a taxonomy
of about thirty-five models in six families, with a common notation and an
architecture diagram for each core model. It offers a structured-linear view
in which the smallest models are linear maps restricted by frequency
truncation or period folding, and it tests each restriction in closed form on
eight benchmarks. It complements the literature review with an audit of
sixteen official code repositories, which reproduces published parameter
counts and reveals reporting gaps of up to 289 times. Finally, it gives
practical guidance on model selection and reporting, and summarizes open
problems.

We cover point forecasting of multivariate

Why folding loses little: what the literature reports

Two published arguments explain why phase sharing costs little on periodic

6 
Where Lightweight Forecasters Matter

The standard benchmarks of Table 
3
 come from four application
domains. Their properties (sampling rate, number of channels, strength of the
period) are exactly the quantities that decide which family of
Section 
4
 fits, and they explain why size matters in each
domain.

Energy systems.

ETT records the oil temperature and loads of
electricity transformers 
[
64
]
, and Electricity records the
hourly consumption of 321 clients 
[
55
]
. Both have a strong
daily period, and Electricity also a weekly one. Forecasts are often needed for
many meters or substations at once, and may have to run on or near the
device, so per-series cost multiplies with the number of series. This is the regime
where channel-independent folding models are most attractive: one predictor
of a few hundred parameters serves every meter, and the closed-form results
of Section 
8
 show that on hourly ETT and
Electricity data the phase-shared constraint costs little or nothing at long
horizons.

Transportation.

Traffic records hourly road occupancy from 862
sensors 
[
55
]
. It combines a daily and a weekly cycle and
many correlated channels. Section 
7
 shows that this is where
the purely linear folding models fall behind and where cross-phase
interaction (PhaseFormer) pays off; cross-channel designs
(CycleNet, TQNet, SOFTS) target the same gap. The size of the PhaseFormer
configuration behind that result is unresolved (Section 
7
).
The fold itself is not the problem: on Traffic, the phase-shared map costs
only 0.5–2% against the full linear map (Section 
8
), so
the gap lies between linear and non-linear models.

Weather and environment.

Weather records 21 meteorological
variables every 10 minutes 
[
55
]
. At this resolution the
daily period spans 144 steps, longer than the look-back used by many models,
and the series is smooth over short windows. A daily fold is then a poor
constraint, costing 9% at 
 and more at short horizons
in our closed-form fits (Section 
8
); small folding
periods, band-limited maps, or MLP models are safer choices.

Edge and embedded deployment.

Several lightweight models are
motivated explicitly by constrained hardware: SparseTSF targets devices with
very limited computational resources 
[
33
]
, LiteMixer
targets edge, mobile and wireless settings 
[
1
]
, and
TTM runs on CPU-only machines 
[
14
]
. In this regime the
relevant budget is not only parameters but also latency, memory and energy
per forecast (Section 
9.1
); energy per forecast is rarely reported, which we list as an open problem
(Section 
10
).

Takeaway.

The benchmark domains differ in the two properties
that matter most for lightweight design: how stable and dominant the period
is, and how many channels share it. Reporting results per domain, rather
than as a single average, would make the trade-offs of each family visible.

Which repositories are audited.

Practical Guidance

9.1 
Deployment Considerations

Parameter count is the headline metric of this literature, but deployment
depends on latency, memory and energy. Energy per forecast is rarely
measured, so the size argument rests mostly on parameter counts and CPU
latency. We return to this gap in Section 
10
.

Latency.

On the single benchmark that reports CPU inference for
many models (Figure 
1
, right), latency follows size only
roughly. The linear-scale models (up to 1.04M parameters: TimeBase,
SparseTSF, FITS, DLinear) run in under 4 ms per sample; the Transformers
need 30–250 ms. Between Transformers, latency depends on
the attention pattern more than on size: PatchTST is the slowest (249 ms)
with fewer parameters (8.69M) than Informer (22.45M, 73 ms)

[
21
, Table 3]
.

Compute versus size.

MACs and parameters can diverge. SSCNN has
about 5K parameters but more FLOPs than iTransformer because it keeps
full-length representations 
[
12
]
; TimeMixer has 5.58M
parameters but 30.3G MACs, versus 1.79G for iTransformer with 5.47M

[
21
, Table 3]
. Edge deployment should report MACs and
latency on the target device, not parameters alone.

Memory.

Training memory is dominated by activations. At batch
size 12 on Electricity, TimeBase needs 89 MB and SparseTSF 125 MB, versus
18 GB for PatchTST 
[
21
, Table 3]
. For
channel-dependent models, memory also grows with 
 (e.g., TQNet’s
attention over 
 channel tokens).

Compression.

Generic compression (pruning, quantization,
distillation 
[
19
, 
20
]
) is almost unused in
this literature, which prefers designing small models directly. Whether a
pruned or quantized Transformer can match a folding model at equal size is
an open question.

 Report the trainable parameter count

2.

Same look-back, and a sweep.

3.

Post-fix evaluation.
 Use

4.

Compute, not only size.

10 
Open Problems

Period selection.

Folding models need 
. SparseTSF selects it from the
autocorrelation function and PhaseFormer from the dominant FFT component;
SparseTSF’s ablation shows that the choice changes accuracy. Learning 
, or folding at several
periods jointly, is open.

Multiple and drifting periods.

A single fold assumes one
dominant period. Daily and weekly cycles together (Traffic), or drifting
periods, break this assumption. A two-level fold, 
 inside

, gives a three-way tensor; predictors that share weights along
both periods are unexplored.

Channel dependence at low cost.

Most tiny models are CI. TQNet
adds channel attention but its size grows as 
; SSCNN adds a spatial
component at higher compute. We are not aware of a channel-dependent
model below 
 parameters that matches the folding models.

Aperiodic data.

On series without stable cycles, the structural
priors give little. PhaseFormer states this limitation explicitly

[
41
]
. A lightweight model needs a principled fallback
(e.g., to the unconstrained linear map) when no stable period is detected.

Theory.

The structured-linear view reduces most of these models
to constrained least squares on normalized windows. Generalization bounds
as a function of the constraint (Kronecker, band-limited), and
closed-form estimators for these classes, would turn many empirical
choices into analysis. Links to Koopman-operator forecasting

[
35
]
 are a promising direction.

Energy and on-device evaluation.

Lightweight models are motivated
by constrained hardware, yet they are evaluated on server GPUs or desktop
CPUs, and energy per forecast is rarely measured. Benchmarks on
microcontrollers and single-board computers, with latency, memory and
energy reported together, would show whether parameter counts translate
into deployment gains.

11 
Conclusion

Lightweight forecasters are best read as structured linear operators with
a light non-linearity from normalization. Frequency truncation gives a
band-limited map, period folding gives Kronecker structure, and explicit
cycles store periodicity as parameters. Our closed-form experiment shows
that, on hourly data (ETTh1, ETTh2, Electricity, Traffic)
at long horizons, the Kronecker constraint alone
keeps full-map accuracy to within 1.5% with 576

fewer parameters, which explains why a few hundred parameters can be enough
on hourly data with a stable daily period. It costs more on
high-resolution data and helps nothing without a period (Exchange). On Traffic,
interaction across phases is needed; on Electricity it brings a smaller
gain. Our audit shows that the field’s
main quantity, the parameter count, is reported inconsistently, and that
look-back choices can outweigh model differences. The models are also
useful: under one protocol they beat the seasonal naive forecast by
21–68% on the seven periodic datasets, though not on
Exchange.
A common protocol would
make these results comparable.

Reproducibility.

The parameter-counting script and the
extracted tables are included with the source files of this paper and
will be released in a public repository.

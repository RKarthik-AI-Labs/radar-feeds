Abstract.

Digital news outlets compete for audience attention. Search engines equipped with generative AI systems such as Google’s AI overviews increasingly decide where that attention goes, as well as the revenue it represents.
Yet, the lack of a consistent data collection infrastructure between national-level regulatory bodies has hindered between-country comparative analysis of media concentration, and with it any assessment of how these intermediaries reshape digital news markets.
To address this issue, we compile an ownership dataset for 9,761 digital news outlets drawn from Wikipedia and Wikidata, achieving high agreement with independently compiled US and UK news directories.
Using this dataset, we derive traffic-weighted concentration indices for 77 countries.
We find that journalists report more owner pressure in countries with higher concentration (
), and that outlet-level measures understate concentration, with owner-level indices a median 1.30× higher.
Applying concentration measures to AI-mediated news discovery, we find that citation distributions from major AI systems are up to 2.08
 more concentrated than traffic,
and that if Google AI Overviews mediated all organic search, the resulting rise in US concentration would be several times that associated with highly regulated media mergers and acquisitions.
Finally, our analyses indicate that AI systems preferentially cite state-controlled media outlets when prompted in that country’s native language.
Together, these findings raise concerns about the effect AI systems may have on digital media.
We release the dataset to enable the monitoring of digital news globally, so that the impact of disruptive technologies on digital media markets can be tracked as they emerge.

Keywords: 
Web economics, media concentration, news ownership, generative AI, AI search, platform regulation

Introduction

On October 6, 2026, despite being challenged by twelve state attorneys general and the Writers Guild of America, Paramount Skydance acquired Warner Bros. Discovery in a deal worth over $110bn.
By placing CBS News and CNN under a single owner, critics are concerned the merger will impact external pluralism in US news.

When questions of media concentration invariably arise in such cases, they are answered anew each time, a process made difficult by a lack of standardization in how concentration is measured.
National regulators count licenses, circulation, or revenue within their own borders and on their own definitions.
As a result, there is no readily available dataset that can be used to compare media concentration between countries.
This is a particularly timely issue, given that the technological changes actively shaping the news media, such as algorithmic curation and the proliferation of AI overviews in search engines, are global in nature.

Digital news outlets exist in a market. Outlets compete for audience attention online,
converting referral traffic from search engines and, increasingly,
generative AI systems into revenue. Missing ownership data therefore leave a Web
economics question open: how concentrated are national markets for digital
news traffic once websites are grouped by owner, and are citations from AI
systems more or less concentrated than that traffic?
This places our work, along with its recommendations regarding competition policy for digital platforms, within the Web Economics and Digital Society track.

To answer it, we construct an international news ownership dataset from crowd-sourced data on Wikipedia. We assign ownership labels to 9,761 domains and compute traffic-weighted concentration
indices (Herfindahl Hirschmann (HHI)) for
77 countries, while the largest prior cross-national study covered 30 
(
Noam, 2016
)
.

The picture that emerges is one of substantial variation (Figure 
1
). Here, HHI is the sum of squared market shares and runs from near 0 when many small owners split the market to 10,000 for a single owner. Its reciprocal (
) is the effective number of equally sized owners, and competition regulators use it to assess market concentration and the effect of mergers 
(
Hill, 2006
)
. In our measurement, the number of effective owners per country ranges from more than ten to fewer than three.
Despite this variation, grouping outlets by owners reveals higher traffic concentration levels, with owner-level indices being a median factor of 1.30
 outlet-level indices.

We validate our concentration indices by comparing against previously published concentration figures 
(
Noam, 2016
)
, and both an independently compiled US ownership directory 
(
Franklin, 2025
)
 and a UK news directory 
(
, 2019
)
. We further compute agreement between the dataset and a set of hand-annotated is-news labels, and between each outlet’s assigned country and where its audience is located 
(
, 2026
)
. Our indices reveal that media concentration tracks conditions reported 
(
Hanitzsch et al., 2019
)
 from inside newsrooms:
journalists report more owner and commercial pressure where ownership is concentrated.

That excessive media concentration can result in such harm is the reason that the FCC regulates traditional mergers and acquisitions.
However, existing regulations have blindspots around emerging technologies, and AI-mediated news discovery is already redistributing news attention. AI-generated answers have been shown to substantially reduce click-through to publishers 
(
Agarwal and Sen, 2026
)
, and publishers are rewriting distribution and search strategy as they compete to gain AI citations 
(
Ponsford, 2026
)
.
Collectively called Generative Engine Optimization (GEO) after Search Engine Optimization (SEO), these emerging strategies attempt to increase the rate at which a domain is cited by generative models 
(
Aggarwal et al., 2024
)
, such as those deployed in AI overviews 
(
Chen et al., 2025
)
.

Whether the distribution of citations from AI systems follows existing media market structures, and what impact those systems will have on future concentration levels, are open questions.
We apply our ownership dataset to investigate this, using citation counts
from seven AI systems: in doing so, we find that AI citation distributions are
1.23
–2.08
 more concentrated than
traffic distributions at the outlet-level, and 1.11
–1.79
 more concentrated at the owner-level.
In addition, we find that the AI amplification effect (i.e. the relative increase in concentration in citations over traffic) is most pronounced in countries with lower traffic concentrations.
As such, if traffic to news outlets shifts to follow citations from AI systems, the traffic concentrations reported here will increase, with less concentrated markets being disproportionately affected.
However, given that measuring the impact of AI citations on traffic directly requires longitudinal data, we leave this to future work.

Underscoring the importance of these findings are a series of copyright infringement and unfair competition law suits that news media companies are beginning to bring against AI intermediaries over the impact of AI systems on declining news outlet traffic and advertising revenues 
(
Tobitt, 2026
)
.
Whether regulators should focus their efforts on these new cases or on those associated with traditional mergers and acquisitions becomes an issue of policy priority. Does a merger of two of the largest broadcast news owners move a country’s media concentration more, or less, than the introduction of AI overviews in Google Search? We estimate the impact of both against our indices and return to the comparison in the Discussion.

Media concentration measurement

Increases in U.S. media concentration between 1983, when 50% of the US media was owned by some fifty companies, and 2000, by which time this had fallen to six companies, motivated a large body of research 
(
Bagdikian, 2004
)
.
Researchers have developed several measures to quantify concentration, such as the Noam 
(
Noam, 2009
)
 and Herfindahl Hirschmann (HHI) indices 
(
Hill, 2006
)
.
These measures have enabled the effects of mergers and acquisitions to be evaluated, such as by the FCC who adopted HHI in 2005 
(
Hill, 2006
)
.
Since then, measures of media concentration have been applied to media environments around the world in which corporate consolidation is problematic 
(
Noam, 2016
; 
Lidberg, 2019
; 
Sjøvaag and Ohlsson, 2019
; 
Trappel and Meier, 2022

.

Comparative media analysis

Comparative media analysis describes how various journalistic cultures emerge given differing political systems 
(
Hallin and Mancini, 2004
)
.
We draw on three cross-national survey-based instruments: the Worlds of Journalism survey (WJS) 
(

(
Egan et al., 2026
)
, and Reporters Sans Frontières (RSF) measures of press freedom 
(

)
.
Based on these indices, it has recently been demonstrated that LLMs exhibit pro-government valences when prompted in the languages of countries with lower media freedom 
(
Waight et al., 2026
)
, a finding we build on in this work.

Search, AI intermediaries and publisher economics

Search engines and AI overviews act as intermediaries between publishers and their audiences. AI-generated answers reduce click-through to publishers

, generative engines can be optimized for citation

 when retrieving citations for new user queries

A parallel line of research develops diversity-aware news aggregation and recommendation algorithms 
(

; 
Abdollahpouri et al., 2021

 by measuring external pluralism and incentivizing it through algorithmic intervention

.
Although we do take advantage of datasets of AI citations to news outlets 
(

; 
Yang, 2025
)
, to the best of our knowledge, there are no works that investigate the impact of AI systems on media concentration. 
Pierson et al. (2023)

Dataset

To compile our dataset of global news sources and their owning companies, we utilize crowdsourced lists of broadcasting company assets on Wikipedia. We compiled a list of broadcasting companies for which a Wikipedia category exists
1
1

1

            
            
            
          e.g., Nexstar’s page: 
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category:Nexstar_Media_Group
 and used Wikipedia’s Petscan service to retrieve the Wiki-pages of the news outlets each company owns. News sources can be newspapers, TV stations, and radio stations.
To restrict to outlets with an official website, we query Wikidata via SPARQL for each outlet’s official domain (2,684 outlets) and fall back to the website link in the outlet’s Wikipedia Infobox. This yields 25,631 news domains across 211 countries.

We combine these ownership records with SimilarWeb traffic estimates and site
categories. Previous research has validated SimilarWeb traffic estimates against estimates from both Ahrefs and computations of PageRank over the CommonCrawl webgraph 
(
Carragher et al., 2025
)
. While the definition of what counts as news is subject to extensive research 
(
Harcup and O’neill, 2017
)
, we simply use SimilarWeb site categories to filter out non-news websites from the dataset.
Specifically, to the list of 25,631 news domains, we remove domains that: are missing a country code, have a traffic estimate of zero, have a SimilarWeb site category unrelated to news (removes platforms, commerce and streaming sites),
or are news aggregators (via a Wikidata entity-type rule). Of the remaining
22,928 domains, we filter out any that do not have an ownership record. The resulting dataset has 9,761 domains.
We annotated a sample of 
 of the remaining domains. 75% are news, accounting for 82.6% of traffic in the sample. Counting magazines and sports outlets increases this to 89% news, accounting for 95% of traffic. The appendix details coverage, filtering, and annotation.

Owner sizes follow power laws, with a small
number of large groups sitting on a long tail of smaller news organizations (Figure 
2
). Of the 3,540 owners in the
dataset, 76.9% hold exactly one outlet and 87.5%
hold one or two. Together the single-outlet owners account for
27.5% of labeled news traffic, and within the median ranked
country they are 80.1% of the owners and
47.8% of the traffic.

State-Sponsored Media Labels

We use the state-media classification from the State Media Monitor (SMM), a hand-compiled census of state-administered news media maintained by the Media and Journalism Research Center 
(
Dragomir and Söderström, 2022
)
. SMM grades outlets on ownership and governance, source of funding, and editorial autonomy.
In total, of the domains in our dataset, 298 are classified as state-controlled by SMM labels, with coverage spanning 80 countries. In our analyses, we treat all state-controlled news outlets as being owned by a single entity (i.e. the state), as treating them as independent entities would artificially inflate market competition metrics.
Wikidata supports a similar classification with high precision but low recall against SMM, so we rely on SMM and describe the Wikidata derived labels for state sponsorship in the appendix.

Dataset Validation

We validate the dataset by evaluating agreement with published concentration figures, independent ownership directories, and human annotations. The appendix reports further validation, such as a high level of agreement between our country labels and the country from which the majority of the news domain’s audience originates, as derived from SimilarWeb data, and shows that our analyses are invariant under different inclusion criteria.

Where Medill disagrees with our dataset, we validate by manually annotating the disagreeing domains. We find that Medill is typically correct, with errors on Wikipedia being caused by the crowdsourced ownership attributes being out of date. Infoboxes often lag recent acquisitions, such as CherryRoad Media’s acquisition of several Gannett newspapers 
(
Caro, 2025

.

Medill data also contains historical data on acquisitions. Using this, we find that our Wikipedia ownership dataset reaches an agreement rate of 93.6% on overlapping domains with the Medill dataset, if we consider either the current or last owner — thus accounting for the Wikipedia data being out of date by a single recent acquisition. Domains matched under this condition account for 98.8% of overlapping traffic. While this level of agreement is already very high, any error that might be induced by inaccuracies is minimized by the fact that acquisitions typically serve to increase media concentration. As such, country-level concentration measures derived from these labels would be biased in the same direction, and could be considered lower-bounds.

State Media

We demonstrate that state media ownership plays an important role in shaping journalism cultures by comparing to measures of press freedom. Then, we answer the question of whether AI citations show a preference toward state media.

We collect RSF indices on press freedom. Table

Media trust and AI amplification

Here we ask whether the AI citations amplification effect is stronger in countries with lower media trust.
In countries where media trust is lower, if AI systems depend more heavily on smaller sets of outlets (for example, those that are well-known), a negative correlation between media trust and the AI amplification rates would follow.

For this, we use country level media trust scores from the Reuters Digital News Reports media trust survey instruments 
(
Egan et al., 2026
)
, and we find a significant negative correlation (
**, 
). Higher-trust countries see less owner-level AI amplification.
Furthermore, we find that media concentration does not explain the relationship between trust and AI amplification. While concentration correlates with amplification (
**), media concentration and media trust are themselves unrelated (
), and so controlling for media concentration leaves the association between amplification and trust intact (
***).
So, the relationship between media trust and AI amplification persists independently of media concentration, suggesting that differences in media trust may be associated with how AI systems distribute citations across news outlets.
While this is one possible explanation, we refrain from making any claims that AI systems do in fact distribute citations according to media trust, and leave this for future work.

Discussion

This work provides current and cross-national answers to questions around the number of organizations that operate in a country’s media system and how much online traffic is concentrated among them. We motivate this work by showing how increased media concentration is associated with journalists’ reports of organizational influence on editorial decisions (
Figure 3
).
Our dataset provides a comparative baseline for the types of merger cases this paper opened with.
Take the Paramount–Warner Bros. Discovery litigation, for example.
Combining the two groups’ outlets under one owner
raises our traffic-based estimate of US media concentration by 
.
Now take the current market as the baseline, in which we assume that AI overviews mediate a negligible share of news traffic, and consider the case where all organic search traffic passes through Google AI Overviews.
In this scenario, with attention redistributed in proportion to Google AI Overview’s citations, US media concentration rises by 
, roughly 3.4 times the Paramount merger’s effect.
Furthermore, the effects are felt internationally, with the median country’s owner-level HHI increasing by 
 (in 49 of 67 countries).
Although we cannot yet conclude that traffic will come to follow citations, which requires longitudinal analysis, this scenario is indicative that platform and AI policy may now be more important than regulating mergers and acquisitions.

The strongest AI system amplifies concentration threefold more than the
weakest, and consulting several systems exposes a reader to meaningfully
more diversity than consulting one — differences that dwarf most of the
cross-country variation in the market baseline. This inverts where policy
levers sit. The instruments of pluralism policy are national and aimed at
owners: licence caps, merger review, cross-ownership rules. The variation
that now matters most is between platforms, is set by citation behavior
rather than by ownership transactions, and is global in reach.
As such, FCC interventions in mergers and acquisitions between media conglomerates such as Paramount and Warner Bros. Discovery represent a national remedy applied to the ownership layer, while AI systems may have a more severe effect and are held accountable by no media regulator.
Exemplifying this issue is the fact that, according to our analysis, which assistant a reader uses may matter more for traffic concentration than which country they live in.

For the economics of digital news, AI systems impact media concentration through two mechanisms at once. The first is citation allocation, measured here: citations concentrate by owner beyond what attention concentration predicts.
The second is in traffic generation and click-through rates, where AI citations typically fail to deliver readers.
According to recent estimates, roughly one AI citation link in twenty is clicked, well below click-through in conventional search 
(
Agarwal and Sen, 2026
)
.
As AI-mediated discovery displaces organic search, that ratio becomes a downwards pressure on referral traffic as a whole, and therefore on revenue.
In an industry defined by high fixed costs and low marginal costs 
(
Noam, 2016
)
, declining revenues will necessitate greater levels of efficiency on the production side.
Hence, the economies of scale that govern the organizational forms that media environments take on will strengthen, making media markets yet more concentrated in equilibrium.
Symptomatic of these efficiencies are the waves of persistent layoffs and reduced editorial output reported across the sector 
(
Peterson and Dunaway, 2023
)
.
The concern this work raises is that, through increasing traffic concentration (and decreasing traffic overall), AI systems as they exist today will indirectly exacerbate these issues.

We extend three lines of existing work.
We reproduce Noam’s four-firm ranking 
(
Noam, 2016
)
, while at the same time translating his media concentration program from national press and broadcast censuses to attention-weighted measurement of digital news. We show that, once ownership labels are attached, traffic concentration tracks ownership pressure reported by journalists. As such, monitors working from audience data alone (i.e., without ownership labels) may miss harms of media concentration. Finally, we extend the study of algorithmic curation and source diversity 
(
Chakraborty et al., 2018
; 
Abdollahpouri et al., 2021
; 
Choi et al., 2022
)
 from search and social feeds to generative systems, including their native-language preference for state-controlled media 
(
Waight et al., 2026
)
. Underlying all three is our ownership dataset. The lack of a consistent, cross-national news ownership dataset has been the chief obstacle to comparative analysis of media concentration, and we release the dataset 
4
4

4

https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.22782826
 and code so that concentration in new information systems can be monitored.

Limitations

The ownership labels are derived from Wikipedia
categories, Wikidata ownership fields and website links, and Wikipedia
infoboxes. As a result, underlying issues in these systems will carry forward into our dataset. For example, editor attention may vary across countries and language editions, and acquisitions may go unrecorded. Furthermore, our validation against the Medill dataset 
(
Franklin, 2025
)
 covers the United States only, where Wikipedia coverage is known to be stronger.
Where available, the integration of official data sources such as registries and regulator filings would extend that validation to countries where the data are weakest.
We note that, while our findings do reproduce on subsets of countries with better label coverage,
coverage is also negatively related to measured concentration (
, 
, 
). Our labels cover 28.8% of domains and 70.1% of traffic, so poorly covered countries may appear more concentrated than they are.

However, even given complete labels, the index uses traffic estimates and citations rather than editorial influence, advertising revenue or political power. This restricts
the production-economics argument in the Discussion. However, since it is known that informing voters is a critical
information need served by news media 
(
Usher et al., 2023
)
, election polling routinely asks which outlets shape voting
decisions 
(
Fletcher et al., 2020
)
, and as a result, data to compute measures of editorial influence may be available. Reproducing our findings on influence based indices would strengthen the case for causal modeling of media concentration and its impacts.

These limitations have important implications for the AI citation analysis. Ahrefs data is from a
commercial panel whose prompt set is proprietary and English-
and United States-weighted. We therefore replicate on the AI Search Arena dataset

(
Miroyan et al., 2026
)
, whose prompts are organic user questions and
roughly 40% of them not in English. Agreement across two panels makes an
artefact of either collection process less likely, though both remain
snapshots.
Moreover, AI systems cite few sources by
construction, so some elevation of concentration relative to traffic may be unavoidable, and our analysis does not account for the order or ranking of sources that get cited.
Finally, owner-level analysis of AI citations rests on a smaller set of 54 countries,
because only 1,653 of
4,584 AI-cited news domains carry an ownership label.
This issue is particularly acute for the Arena data, upon which any statistical claims are difficult to defend.
The AI findings are accordingly best read as generating hypotheses that
longitudinal data can test.

Conclusion

We release a cross-national news ownership dataset and use it to reveal that
media concentrations across 77 countries are higher than the corresponding outlet-level concentrations.
Our concentration indices correlate with the degree of commercial pressure reported by journalists, and after incorporating state media labels into the dataset, the indices reproduce external indicators of press freedom.
AI citation behavior then acts on that structure. AI citations are more concentrated than attention at the outlet level, and again once outlets are grouped by owner.
Asked in a country’s own language, these systems cite state-controlled outlets 1.65
 more than commercial outlets of the same size.
AI systems now allocate digital news attention at a scale comparable to the mergers regulators review, and we argue that traditional antitrust regulations should be extended to cover new AI systems. Without this, global trends of increasing media concentration are likely to accelerate.
The dataset, the labels and the full
analysis pipeline are released so that this work can be expanded upon.

United Kingdom

Using the same methodology, we gathered UK datasets and validated the coverage of UK news outlets on Wikidata with those that are listed in curated datasets maintained by the UK Public Interest News Foundation (PINF) 
(
, 2019
)
. Of the 1,438 Wikidata UK news domains we discover, 29% are in the PINF dataset (or 482 news outlets).
Coverage varies by media type, with digital outlets best represented: our UK dataset covers 53.5% of outlets which exist both in Print & Online, 27.2% of online-only outlets, 9.1% of print-only titles, community radio 3.7%, and local TV 2.9%. In terms of ownership type, our dataset is biased toward corporate owned news outlets, covering 54% of corporate titles, and only 9% of independent outlets, which may partly reflect the large number of community radio stations PINF lists.

Country Assignment

The country a domain is assigned to matches the top audience country
SimilarWeb reports for 88.0% of 22,212 domains, and is somewhere
in that domain’s top five for 93.2%.
The dataset’s country assignments are built from Wikipedia category trees, ccTLDs and
infobox locations. SimilarWeb’s per-country audience shares are read by nothing
in that pipeline, so they are a genuinely held-out test rather than a consistency
check.
US traffic is inflated: a large share of measured web traffic originates in the United States whatever an outlet’s market, so “the top
audience country is the US” is weak evidence of a US outlet. In cases where the US is marked as the top audience country by SimilarWeb, we count our country label as a match if it is either the US or the next highest country by traffic share.

Label Coverage and Inclusion Criteria

Our inclusion criteria filter out a large number of domains pulled from Wikipedia. As determined via SimilarWeb traffic estimates, 70.1% of the total traffic volume received by the news domains in our frame is accounted for by a domain that carries an ownership label.
Across the 77 ranked countries,
coverage and measured concentration correlate at 
 (
), suggesting that inclusion criteria do impact the analysis. Thin coverage biases the index upward, because unlabeled traffic is dropped from the denominator.
However, changing the inclusion criteria and naming specifications does not move the concentration indices (see Specification Stability below).
As such, the analyses are not invalidated by reporting results on a subset of the traffic data observed (i.e. the subset for which we have ownership labels).

Method

A domain enters the pool on any evidence of public or state ownership: its own Wikidata entity, or an owner entity reached through the ownership properties (P127, P749, P123, P137), is typed as a public or state broadcaster; owner keys are propagated to every domain sharing the owner string; generic public or state naming patterns on the owner string (e.g., CBC, SRG SSR, TVNZ); and a small hand list. The pool is then partitioned by precedence into government non-media (keyed on 
.gov
, 
.mil
 or 
.edu
 hosts and official gazettes), state-sponsored (positive evidence of state or ruling-party direction, optionally moderated by an RSF press-freedom gate), party press, and public-service as the remainder.

Appendix G 
AI Analysis

The panel is 4,584 news domains across 199
countries. A country enters the analysis with at least ten labeled domains,
rather than being cut to a fixed top-
: the owner-count gate already makes
markets comparable, and a fixed cut cost eight ranked countries while inflating
the amplification ratios it was meant to guard. Portals are excluded, as
elsewhere. AI concentration and traffic concentration are computed over the same
outlets, so a difference reflects how citations spread rather than which outlets
were sampled.

Sensitivity.

The amplification result holds under all three is-news
filter settings and at every top-
 cut from 10 to 30, though the effect shrinks
as 
 grows — adding smaller outlets adds units that AI rarely cites, which
raises the traffic baseline faster than the citation index. The association
between AI concentration and ownership concentration strengthens with 
 rather
than weakening, so neither result is an artefact of the cut.

The AI hypothesis family.

H1–H3 are corrected
together within each level of analysis. Two tests are deliberately outside the family: the
traffic-versus-ownership correlation, which checks that the data behave as
expected rather than testing a hypothesis, and the trust tests, which address a
separate question.

Appendix H 
State and Public-Service Media

Non-commercial ownership is split into four classes rather than treated as one,
because a public-service broadcaster, a state-sponsored outlet, a government
non-media site and a party organ are different objects and only the first is a
plurality institution in the regulatory sense.

On the Ahrefs panel, AI systems do not preferentially cite state-controlled or
public-service outlets, at either level of analysis and under the
citation-traffic elasticity control, and treating the state as a single owner
does not concentrate citations more than traffic. Those nulls are read as a size
effect rather than a state effect, because a size-matched commercial bloc is

more
 suppressed than the state bloc: what looks like under-citation of
state media is under-citation of outlets of that size. They are also
underpowered, and the native-language result reported in the main text shows
they are a property of an English-prompted instrument.

Appendix I 
Credibility and Trust

Two further questions were asked of these data and are reported here because the
analyses exist and should not be repeated. Neither supports an argument in the
paper.

Credibility and consumption.

Concentration is positively associated
with the share of a country’s news consumption going to outlets of low

or mixed
 credibility (
 by outlet count,

 by visits; 
 and

, 
), and that association is
robust to weighting and to frame. It does not survive a control for GDP per
capita: correcting for the 24 tests run in each frame,

none
 of the GDP-controlled partials is significant, the strongest being

). Richer
countries are both less concentrated and consume less low-credibility news, and
that is enough to account for the raw association. There is no association at all
for clearly low-credibility outlets as distinct from low-or-mixed
(
, 
).

Media trust.

We tested whether concentration relates to public trust
in news. It is not supported: there is no association across the full
47-country panel (
,

), and the positive association inside the canonical
Hallin and Mancini eighteen is specification-dependent and not significant on the
specification this paper adopts. It survives neither development controls, nor
four constructions of the public-ownership set, nor a between/within
decomposition by media system.

Both are limited by trust-survey coverage (48 countries)
and by a single wave, and both need better-identified designs than we can bring
to them. Concentration in these data is consequential for the people producing
the news and, on this evidence, not measurably so for what audiences consume or
believe.

Abstract

To manipulate a moving object, a policy must predict the scene at the moment its action
executes, yet every millisecond spent predicting pushes that moment further away.
Scene-level forecasts are computationally expensive, whereas target-centric forecasts can
omit nearby objects that affect contact. Our key insight is that what a policy
must predict decouples by role: only the target’s pose, which determines contact, requires
millimeter precision, while the surroundings matter primarily through the clearance they
leave and the motion they impart. We present FoveaWAM, a native 4D world–action model
that allocates precision according to these roles. Specifically, it models the target’s past and future motion as a
metric SE(3) trajectory, the surroundings as sparse 3D flow, and relies on the current frame
for everything static. This compact representation lets FoveaWAM act in 15.1 ms on one
RTX 5090, about 
 faster than video world–action baselines, leaving substantially less time for the scene to evolve before action execution.
To evaluate each policy under its own latency, we introduce 
OnTimeBench
, a dynamic
manipulation benchmark in which the scene keeps moving while the policy computes.
FoveaWAM reaches 82.1% success on 
OnTimeBench
, 20.8 points above the strongest baseline, and
also leads on DOMINO and real-robot tasks.

Introduction

Robot manipulation policies have made substantial progress in static scenes 
[
10
, 
4
]
.
In these scenes, the world stays still during inference, so the observed scene remains valid when the action executes.
In dynamic manipulation, however, the target keeps moving during inference, so the observed pose is already outdated when the action executes.
For example, a block sliding down a ramp at about 0.9 m/s travels 18 cm during a 200 ms inference interval, far exceeding the positional tolerance of accurate contact.
Successful manipulation therefore requires predicting the target’s pose at execution time. Predicting the target’s motion requires accounting for surrounding objects, but the target continues to move while the prediction is being computed. The longer prediction takes, the further ahead the policy must predict.
This creates a tension between 
accurate prediction and timely action.

Existing methods vary in what they predict (Fig. 
2
).
Scene-level predictors model future images or video 
[
61
, 
53
, 
45
, 
29
]

or 3D motion 
[
58
]
. These predictions capture surrounding motion, but a dense forecast can spend computation on parts of the scene with little bearing
on contact. In a video forecast, the target may occupy only a small region, and its pose remains implicit in the predicted pixels. AHEAD limits its rollout to task-relevant
latent patches 
[
41
]
. PUMA supervises future features of the target 
[
14
]
, and SPOT predicts target pose trajectories 
[
17
]
. Focusing on the target can omit surrounding objects that block access or alter its motion. For example, a
moving obstacle can close the approach path even if the target follows the
predicted trajectory. Determining whether that path stays clear and estimating
the target’s pose at contact impose different demands on a forecast.

What should a policy predict about a moving scene, and how precisely,

to make accurate contact in time?

Our key insight is that prediction requirements 
differ by role
 (Fig. 
1
).
The target’s position and orientation at execution time directly determine contact success and therefore require precise prediction.
In contrast, the surroundings affect the contact only indirectly, by obstructing
access or altering the target’s motion, so their motion requires only coarse
prediction.
Finally, the rest of the scene is static, so it is already available in the current frame and requires no prediction.
A policy can therefore make accurate contact in time by concentrating precise prediction on
the target and capturing the surroundings at low cost.
This allocation resembles biological vision, where the fovea resolves fine detail and
peripheral vision preserves

broad motion awareness 
[
12
, 
39
, 
34
]
.

Based on this insight, we propose 
FoveaWAM
, a native 4D world–action model that allocates precision by role (Fig. 
2
c).
FoveaWAM observes and predicts scene motion directly in metric 3D space over time, rather than in pixels.
In this space, each part of the scene can be represented separately, so each receives the precision that its role requires.
The target is predicted precisely, as a metric SE(3) trajectory that gives its future position and orientation explicitly with only a few parameters.
In contrast, the surroundings are predicted coarsely, as sparse 3D flow, and the static scene is not predicted.
The same allocation applies to the input.
A single frame already shows the static scene and the layout of the surroundings, so only the target’s motion requires a history.
FoveaWAM therefore reads one RGB-D frame and a short SE(3) pose history of the target, instead of the image or optical-flow
histories used by existing dynamic policies 
[
50
, 
14
, 
29
]
.
Since both the input and the predictions are compact, FoveaWAM acts in 15.1 ms on one RTX 5090, about 
 faster than video world–action models, and the scene has little time to move before the action executes.

The tension between accurate prediction and timely action can be measured only when the evaluation accounts for inference time. However, the default evaluators of DOMINO 
[
14
]
 and PhysMani-Bench 
[
58
]
 pause the simulation during inference, so a slow policy is scored as if it were instant.
We therefore introduce 
OnTimeBench
, a benchmark of 14 dynamic manipulation tasks in which the world never pauses and every policy is evaluated at its native latency.
On the same scenes, pausing the world overestimates slow policies and changes the ranking of methods (Fig. 
6
c).

Experiments show that FoveaWAM makes accurate contact in time.
On 
OnTimeBench
, it reaches 82.1% success at native latency, 20.8 points above the strongest baseline.
It also leads by 19.9 points on the single-arm tasks of DOMINO 
[
14
]
, whose evaluator pauses during inference, so its advantage does not come from speed alone.
On six real-robot tasks, it achieves the highest success on every task and 48.3% on average, 17.5 points above the strongest baseline.
Finally, ablations show that each part of the role-aware design contributes to success,
with the largest drop from removing the target’s pose history.

Our key contributions are as follows:

•

We identify distinct prediction requirements in dynamic manipulation and
propose a role-aware prediction paradigm. It forecasts target motion precisely
and surrounding motion coarsely, using the current frame for static geometry.
This allocation preserves the precision needed for contact while reducing
prediction cost.

•

We present 
FoveaWAM
, a native 4D world–action model that
instantiates this paradigm and achieves precise, low-latency action generation.
It acts in 15.1 ms, about 
 faster than video world–action models,
and outperforms the strongest baseline by 20.8 points on 
OnTimeBench
. It also leads
on DOMINO’s single-arm tasks with paused physics and across six dynamic real-robot tasks.

•

We build 
OnTimeBench
, a benchmark of 14 dynamic manipulation tasks in
which the world never pauses and every policy is evaluated at its native
latency, exposing the cost of latency that paused evaluation hides.

FoveaWAM

FoveaWAM applies the role-aware allocation to both what it reads and what it predicts
(Fig. 
3
a). It reads the current RGB-D frame for the static scene
and the context, and an SE(3) pose history for the target. It predicts coarse sparse 3D
flow for the scene and a precise SE(3) trajectory for the target. In between, a world
stream (scene tokens 
 and summary tokens 
) and an action stream (noisy action
tokens 
) share one masked attention in each of the 
 layers.

Each task assigns the scene three roles. The

3.2 
Role-Aware Dynamics Encoding

A single frame shows where things are but not how they move. The context does not need
a motion history of its own. The clearance it leaves is visible in the current geometry,
and the motion it imparts appears in how the target has moved. FoveaWAM therefore pairs one
RGB-D frame with a short metric pose history of the target.

Scene tokens from the current frame.

Contact is decided in metric space, so FoveaWAM anchors each patch token at a metric 3D
point. A frozen MAE ViT-B/16 
[
16
]
 encoder turns the RGB image into
196 patch tokens on a 
 grid. The depth map back-projects the
center of patch 
 to a point 
 in the base frame. A linear embedding
of 
 is added to the patch token, and 
 also sets the 3D rotary
position embeddings of its queries and keys. Attention between patches then depends on
their relative metric positions, and observations, predictions, and actions share one
frame.

Target tokens from the pose history.

The pose history covers the last 350 ms in 8 samples at
20 Hz. The SE(3) encoder turns it into 8 tokens, one for the
current target pose and seven for the motions between consecutive samples. Each motion
token holds the translation, relative rotation, time interval, and finite-difference
velocity and acceleration in physical units, so the quantities that decide where the
target will be enter the model directly. The state encoder adds 8 robot
tokens with the end-effector states at the same times. The whole history thus
costs 16 low-dimensional tokens, whereas each additional frame would cost
196 patch tokens. The patch, target, and robot tokens together form the scene
tokens 
. The target poses come from the simulator in simulation and from an online
RGB-D tracker on the robot.

4 
OnTimeBench

The default evaluators of DOMINO 
[
14
]
 and PhysMani-Bench 
[
58
]

advance the simulation only after the policy returns its actions, so inference takes
no time in the scene.
A policy that computes for 200 ms thus still finds the ball where it observed it,
although a real ball would have rolled 18 cm (Fig. 
4
a).
In 
OnTimeBench
, observation, scene motion, and action execution share one physical clock.
The scene keeps moving while the policy computes, so each policy is evaluated at its
native latency.

OnTimeBench
 contains 14 tasks in five families (Fig. 
4
b, c).
Grasp tasks pick up a moving target, and Place tasks put a held object into a moving
receptacle.
Pass tasks guide a wand along a rotating wire without contact, Capture tasks catch a
rolling or sliding object, and the Impact task grasps a part after a striker hits it.
Target speeds range from 0.03 to 1.14 m/s.
The tasks also differ in what decides success.
In the seven rotation-sensitive tasks, the target rotates and its orientation matters
at contact.
In the nine context-active tasks, an obstacle limits the clearance or a driver such as
a conveyor, ramp, or striker moves the target.

4.1 
Construction

OnTimeBench
 is built in MuJoCo and robosuite 
[
44
, 
66
]
 with a Franka Panda arm
controlled at 20 Hz.
Each scene samples the target’s initial state and motion parameters within
task-specific ranges and is kept only if it is physically feasible.
A task-specific expert with privileged simulator state provides 800
demonstrations per task.
A demonstration is kept only if the expert succeeds, a replay of its actions reproduces
the episode, and its path and jerk pass quality checks.
We select configurations on 1,400 development scenes and report all 
OnTimeBench
 results on
1,400 held-out test scenes, 100 per task, shared by all methods.

Dynamic Manipulation Results

Results on 
OnTimeBench
.

As shown in Table 
1
, FoveaWAM achieves the highest SR in all three settings.
At native latency, it reaches 82.1%, 20.8 points above the strongest baseline, PUMA
(61.4%), and it has the highest or tied-highest SR on 13 of the 14 tasks, as
reported in the supplementary material. The margins are largest where a moving receptacle
or the target’s orientation decides success. They reach 30 and 55 points on placement
into translating and rotating receptacles and 28 points on full wire passage. This
lead comes from the role-aware design. Given the same SE(3) history as input,

 and DynamicWAM reach 58.7% and 41.7%, which is 23.4 and 40.4 points below
FoveaWAM.

Representation and latency.

FoveaWAM leads both in how it represents the target’s motion and in how quickly it acts.
With the world paused, inference costs no time, and FoveaWAM still leads 
+SE(3), which reads the same target poses, by 14.3 points. This
gap reflects the representation alone. At native latency, FoveaWAM loses 8.1 points and

+SE(3) loses 17.3, so the gap widens to 23.4 points. Low latency alone is not
enough. ACT is the only method faster than FoveaWAM, but it reaches 41.6%, which is 40.5
points below FoveaWAM. FoveaWAM leads at every added delay (Fig. 
6
a). With 150 ms
added, each chunk must cover a longer open-loop interval. Every method falls to
35% or below, and the margin over PUMA narrows to 3.0 points.

Effect of target motion history.

The pose history is the most important input. Removing it while keeping the
current pose reduces SR by 19.6 points at native latency and by 24.0 points on
rotation-sensitive tasks. The drop is the same with the world paused (19.5 points). A
single pose shows where the target is but not how it moves. The history supplies the
velocity and acceleration that commands must anticipate.

Effect of role-aware prediction.

Each role needs its own form of prediction. Replacing the SE(3) trajectory with flow at
the density of the surroundings reduces SR by 4.5 points, and by 8.6 points on
rotation-sensitive tasks. At this density, the few patches on the target cannot
determine its rotation. A 
 denser flow recovers the overall SR but still
trails FoveaWAM by 3.4 and 2.6 points on rotation-sensitive and context-active tasks.
Removing the sparse flow reduces SR by 4.2 points, and by 11.7 points on
rotation-sensitive tasks, because the flow shapes the patch tokens that the action
stream reads near contact. One target trajectory and 196 flow points thus match
3,136 uniform flow points overall and surpass them where rotation and context matter.

Effect of future supervision.

Supervising only the current target state instead of its future reduces SR by 4.3
points, and by 12.6 points on rotation-sensitive tasks, although the prediction heads
never run at deployment. Prediction thus helps through the representation it
shapes. The appearance losses 
 also help,
mainly on rotation-sensitive tasks.

Prediction in a moving world.

The three variants that weaken future prediction (
uniform coarse flow
,

w/o flow
, 
w/o future
) each lose more than 4 points at native latency
but none with the world paused. With the world paused, a policy replans every 50 ms
and executes only the command for the capture time. At native latency, the
15.1 ms inference of FoveaWAM delays execution to the next control boundary, 50 ms after
capture. The representation must then encode where the target will be. Removing the history or

 instead lowers SR in both settings. Paused evaluation hides the
value of prediction, and 
OnTimeBench
 reveals it.

6 
Conclusion

We presented FoveaWAM, a native 4D world–action model that allocates prediction precision
by role, with precise prediction for the target, coarse prediction for its surroundings,
and none for the static scene. This allocation alleviates the tension between accurate
prediction and timely action. FoveaWAM acts in 15.1 ms and outperforms the strongest
baselines on 
OnTimeBench
, DOMINO, and real robots. We also introduced 
OnTimeBench
, which evaluates
every policy at its native latency and shows that paused evaluation overestimates slow
policies. FoveaWAM currently assumes a rigid target whose pose can be tracked online, and
we leave articulated and deformable targets to future work.

The frozen MAE ViT-B/16

One embeds the mean valid depth of the patch. The other embeds the base-frame position

valid depth at its center receives no position embedding. Only the patch tokens carry 3D
rotary embeddings, computed from

State tokens.

The 8 target-state tokens describe the target at the offsets

 ms. Each is a 25-dimensional vector

. Slots 0–6 describe the seven intervals between consecutive
offsets, and slot 7 holds the current pose. Velocities and
accelerations are finite differences over the elapsed times. Each dimension is
standardized, and invalid dimensions are set to zero. Three

no valid feature is masked out of attention. The robot tokens hold the end-effector
position, rotation vector, and gripper opening at the same offsets.
The 16 summary tokens are learned embeddings.

Training

Action labels.

Each action token embeds one seven-dimensional command, which consists of a
base-frame translation increment, a rotation vector that left-multiplies the current
orientation, and a gripper command. Both increments are relative to the measured
end-effector pose when the command is applied. Command 
 of query 
 is labeled with
the demonstration command issued at 
. In the post-impact grasp, labels follow the issue time (Eq. (
S4
)). Commands are
standardized with training statistics.

Prediction labels.

Flow labels come from the simulator. The surface point at each patch center
follows the ground-truth rigid motion of its MuJoCo geometry. A future point is visible
if it projects onto the same geometry within a depth tolerance of

, where 
 is its depth. Invisible points still supervise displacement, with visibility label zero. Displacements are divided
by the scale 
 m, and the visibility term has weight 1. Pose labels are the target’s ground-truth poses. Horizons beyond the last frame of a demonstration are
excluded from both losses.

Auxiliary losses.

 sums four terms. Three of them are squared errors on future
metric depth at valid pixels, on the future RGB of surfaces that move by more than
1 mm, and on future layer-normalized DINOv3 features 
[
37
]
.
The fourth reconstructs the RGB of the current target region from the mean summary
state with an absolute error.

Optimization.

Each batch holds 512 windows. A window contains up to eight consecutive queries from
one demonstration, and each query has its own causal history. The world stream runs
once per query, as at inference. Its keys and values are reused for eight independent
draws of action noise and flow time, and the action loss averages over these draws.
All experiments, including the baselines, the ablations, and DROID pretraining, ran on
64 NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 and 32 NVIDIA H100 GPUs. One training run of FoveaWAM takes about
24 h on 16 of these RTX PRO 6000 GPUs.

B.2 
Construction

Simulation.

The tasks run in MuJoCo 3.11.0 and robosuite 1.5.2 
[
44
, 
66
]
 with a
Franka Panda arm and a parallel gripper. The operational-space controller of robosuite
tracks the commanded end-effector pose. The control period is 50 ms, and the physics
step is 2 ms. A fixed third-person camera with a 
 vertical field of view
renders 
 RGB-D images. Actions are the seven-dimensional commands of
Appendix 
A.3
. Each translation component is clipped to 0.10 m, and each
rotation component to 0.5 rad.

Scenes.

Each scene samples the target’s initial state and motion parameters uniformly
within task-specific ranges. Table 
S4
 gives the speed ranges. Other sampled
parameters include harmonic amplitudes of up to 35 mm at 0.25–0.7 Hz, yaw rates of up
to 1.2 rad/s, and ramp slopes of 
, 
, or 
. In the occluded grasp, the target is
hidden for 0.5–3.0 s and then reappears. Only physically feasible scenes are kept, and every method replays the same stored scenes.

Demonstrations.

The expert is a task-specific closed-loop state machine. It reads the privileged
simulator state and anticipates the motion of the target, for example by predicting the
interception point in the capture tasks. A demonstration is kept if the expert succeeds,
if a replay of its actions reproduces the episode, and if its path and jerk pass quality
checks. The training set holds 800 demonstrations per task, 11,200 in total. They are
recorded at 20 Hz with RGB-D images, robot states, commands, and simulator ground
truth.

Splits.

We select configurations on 1,400 development scenes, 100 per task. All results come from 1,400 held-out test scenes, also 100 per task, sampled from the same ranges with seeds unused in training and development.

Latency measurement.

We time each method alone on one RTX 5090 at batch size 1. Each baseline runs in the
precision of its release, and FoveaWAM runs in compiled BF16 with TF32 disabled. Each
method runs three rounds of 30 warm-up calls and 300 measured calls on recorded observations with their causal history. A timed query starts from the newest
observation and ends when its first command is available. We call this interval the
time-to-action. It includes all computation that needs the newest frame, namely preprocessing, image encoding, the online flow
and motion descriptors of PUMA and DynamicWAM, sampling, and action decoding. PUMA is
timed with its three official cameras and the other baselines with two, which changes
their p95 time-to-action by at most 3.8 ms relative to the agent view.

Data and training.

The data split has 1,200 training and 240 validation demonstrations, and the test uses
another 2,400 scenes, 100 per task. Policies execute absolute joint-position targets
with DOMINO’s native controller. Their state and action projections use its
eight-dimensional joint space, normalized on training data. The baselines use their official recipes. DynamicWAM and PUMA are initialized from
their official checkpoints and fine-tuned on our Franka demonstrations. These
checkpoints were trained on ALOHA-AgileX simulation demonstrations of all 35 dynamic
tasks of DOMINO.

Appendix D 
Real-Robot Experiments

Platform and tasks.

The robot is a Flexiv Rizon 4s arm with a GN01 gripper. Policies send tool-center-point poses and gripper targets at 20 Hz. Two RealSense D435 cameras observe the scene. One is fixed
and calibrated to the robot base, and the other is mounted on the wrist.
FoveaWAM uses RGB-D from the fixed camera, and the baselines use RGB from both cameras.

Abstract

Runtime guardrails protect mobile GUI agents by assessing each proposed action before execution, but existing guardrails rely on cloud hosted large multimodal models, exposing every screen to a remote server, including private information such as payments, messages, and medical records. We therefore study how to train a compact guardrail that runs on device, which raises two challenges. On-device budgets limit the model to a short context, yet safety decisions hinge on evidence observed many steps earlier, which existing guardrails lose in truncating or summarizing the history. Training a compact model also requires a well-defined supervision signal, yet existing labels conflate perceived risk, committed violations, and the decision to intervene under a single 
unsafe
, leaving the safety boundary ambiguous. We propose MobileGrail, an on-device deployable model that addresses both. It maintains a 
recurrent task state
 that retains only the evidence subsequent safety decisions depend on, so that authorization and execution progress persist beyond the short context. To intervene early while keeping the safety boundary precise, it learns two labels, 
unsafe
 and 
block
: 
unsafe
 marks the action that commits a violation, whereas 
block
 may be issued at any step of an 
intervention window
, which spans from the first action that the user’s authorization no longer explains to the committing action itself. To support training, we build an environment of 32 applications whose backends verify the effect of every action, supplying verified labels for supervision and rewards for reinforcement learning to calibrate intervention timing. We also build MobileVeto, an offline benchmark of unsafe trajectories largely on held-out applications, each paired with a benign counterpart. On MobileVeto, MobileGrail built on Qwen3.5-2B intercepts 76.2% of violations, up from 64.5% for its prompted backbone, while reducing premature intervention from 58.6% to 5.2%, outperforming the cloud-based OS-Sentinel on both. We further conduct online evaluation on GhostEI-Bench, where MobileGrail guarding a Qwen3.8-27B agent reduces fully successful attacks from 13 to 3 of 110 tasks while raising task completion from 48 to 58. Quantized to 4 bits, MobileGrail runs on a Snapdragon 8 Elite at a median latency of 4.7 s, comparable to the 5.1 s of cloud-hosted Gemini 3.8 Flash.

Introduction

Recent advances in multimodal foundation models have allowed autonomous agents to operate mobile devices through sequences of discrete GUI actions  
(
Kong et al., 2026
)
.
However,
deploying these agents in real-world environments introduces safety risks arising from environmental threats, agent misbehavior, and unsafe user requests, which can lead to financial loss, privacy leakage, or unauthorized operations  
(
Chen et al., 2026b
; 
Guo et al., 2026
)
. For example, a prompt-injected
advertisement on the screen can redirect an agent to transfer funds or
forward private messages without the user authorization. These risks make effective defenses essential for the safe deployment of mobile GUI agents.

Improving the intrinsic safety of mobile GUI agents through alignment can mitigate these risks 
(
Ai et al., 2026
; 
Liang et al., 2025
; 
Lee et al., 2024
)
, but it comes at a capability cost 
(
Huang et al., 2025
)
 and requires further training as the threats evolve.
A complementary approach is to employ external runtime guardrails, which assess proposed actions before execution using a safety mechanism separate from the underlying agent 
(
Sun et al., 2026
; 
Huang et al., 2026
)
.
New safety policies can therefore be incorporated by updating the guardrail alone, without retraining the agent itself.
Recent work has explored this approach for mobile GUI agents, with methods that combine formal verification with VLM-based assessment 
(
Sun et al., 2026
)
, calibrate action-level risk prediction for selective execution 
(
Feng et al., 2026
)
, or anticipate action consequences through world-model prediction 
(
Yu et al., 2026
)
.

Despite this progress, existing mobile guardrails largely rely on large multimodal models served through cloud APIs, which transmit every screen to the service provider 
(
Wang et al., 2026a
)
, including payments, messages, and medical records. This cloud-based guardrail introduces both privacy risks and per-action inference latency that hinders real-time intervention. Therefore, we aim to build a local on-device guardrail: a compact deployable model that fits mobile compute and memory budgets and intervenes on unsafe trajectories at the right step without disrupting benign tasks. The limited budget leaves room for only a short history, yet safety decisions are long-horizon, often hinging on evidence observed many steps earlier 
(
Liu et al., 2026a
)
: what the user authorized, which on-screen content is untrusted, and which parameters the agent has staged. Truncating history to a fixed window 
(
Liang et al., 2025
; 
Sun et al., 2026
)
 discards this evidence once it falls outside the window, while free-form summaries retain it verbosely without separating authorized parameters from untrusted instructions 
(
Hu et al., 2025
)
.

Moreover, training such a guardrail requires a well-defined supervision signal, since its labels are what define the safety boundary. A guardrail should intervene before harm is committed, and existing mobile guardrails pursue this by labeling perceived risk as 
unsafe
 before a violation occurs 
(
Sun et al., 2026
; 
Yu et al., 2026
; 
Feng et al., 2026
)
; OS-Sentinel 
(
Sun et al., 2026
)
, for instance, marks risk initiation at non-committing steps such as opening an app. Because 
unsafe
 is their only label, marking it earlier is the only way to intervene earlier. The label then covers anticipated risk and actual violations alike, so the guardrail learns that a step such as opening an app is itself unsafe and rejects benign tasks that take the same step. The accompanying step-localization metric compounds the problem 
(
Sun et al., 2026
)
: it measures distance to this premature step and penalizes early and late detections symmetrically, although only a late detection arrives after the harm is committed. Conversely, labeling only the committing action keeps the boundary precise but teaches the guardrail to act too late: it intervenes only at the commit, when justified intervention could begin several steps earlier. Violation and intervention are thus distinct, one a point and the other a window, and a single label cannot express both.

We introduce MobileGrail, a compact multimodal guardrail locally deployable on device that addresses both challenges. At each step, it judges the proposed action against the current screen and its own task state, and returns whether the action is unsafe, whether to block it, and feedback on the boundary crossed; no screen leaves the device, and the judgment arrives before the action executes. To retain long-horizon evidence within this compact context, MobileGrail maintains a 
recurrent task state
, a representation of task authorization and execution progress. To decouple violation from intervention, it learns to predict two labels, both grounded in a training environment whose app backends track the effect of every action, so that each violation can be reliably verified. 
Unsafe
 identifies actions whose execution violates the user’s authorization or applicable policy. 
Block
 is supervised over an intervention window: its start is determined by the evidence available to justify intervention, while its end is fixed by the action that commits the violation. Both predictions are trained by supervised distillation followed by reinforcement learning, with rewards anchored to these verified commits rather than teacher judgments.

Our contributions are summarized as follows:
(1) We present MobileGrail, compact multimodal guardrail models (1.6B and 2B) for on-device deployment that maintain a recurrent task state and separately predict action safety and intervention, trained by distillation and calibrated by RL with verifiable rewards.
(2) We build a scalable Android training environment that automatically
mines verifiable learning signals: self-evolving red-teaming generates
attack scenarios across 32 applications, and their corresponding backends verify
the step at which a violation is committed, grounding both labels and
rewards.
(3) We introduce MobileVeto, an evaluation benchmark for mobile
guardrails spanning diverse application categories, comprising 210
adversarial trajectories annotated with intervention windows, each
paired with a benign counterpart. It jointly evaluates
violation detection, intervention timing, and benign-task preservation.

Related Works

Runtime Guardrails for Agents

Guardrails for agents extend safety assessment beyond model inputs and outputs to proposed actions. Prompt-based guardrails such as GuardAgent and AGrail reason over task-specific checks 
(
Xiang et al., 2025
; 
Luo et al., 2025
)
, while trained guard models assess plans 
(
Huang et al., 2026
)
, individual tool calls 
(
Mou et al., 2026
; 
Cui et al., 2026
)
, or entire trajectories 
(
Wen et al., 2026
; 
Liu et al., 2026c
; 
Liu et al., 2026b
)
. MAGE further maintains an RL-trained shadow memory and judges each tool call against it alone 
(
Wang et al., 2026b
)
. Concurrent with our work, StepSafe 
(
Gu et al., 2026
)
 learns intervention timing for step-level guardrails of tool-calling and multi-agent workflows.

For GUI agents, safeguards further ground decisions in interface and application state. VeriSafe Agent verifies actions against formal specifications 
(
Lee et al., 2025
)
, and AgentSentinel audits sensitive operations with summarized interaction history and system traces 
(
Hu et al., 2025
)
. On mobile, SafeMobile conditions on the two most recent steps 
(
Liang et al., 2025
)
, OS-Sentinel combines system-trace verification with VLM judgment 
(
Sun et al., 2026
)
, CORA calibrates selective execution 
(
Feng et al., 2026
)
, and SeerGuard predicts action consequences 
(
Yu et al., 2026
)
.

These guardrails all run off-device on large models, exposing screen content at every step; on-device moderation, meanwhile, has targeted text prompts rather than GUI actions 
(
Nakka et al., 2025
; 
Elesedy et al., 2024
)
. Their context is either truncated 
(
Liang et al., 2025
)
 or summarized without authorization status 
(
Hu et al., 2025
)
, and their supervision is unverified: unsafe labels come from LLM judges over requests and observations 
(
Sun et al., 2026
; 
Yu et al., 2026
; 
Feng et al., 2026
)
, with no referent in action effects. MobileGrail instead trains an on-device deployable guardrail whose recurrent task state carries authorization and execution state under a bounded context, supervised by backend-verified action effects.

Safety Evaluation of GUI Agents

Safety benchmarks for GUI agents measure unsafe or untrustworthy behavior across mobile and desktop environments 
(
Lee et al., 2024
; 
Kuntz et al., 2026
; 
Yang et al., 2025
)
 and risks induced by malicious or manipulated environmental content 
(
Chen et al., 2026a
; 
Liu et al., 2026d
; 
Chen et al., 2026b
; 
Guo et al., 2026
)
. Recent benchmarks further evaluate runtime guardrails at the step level. MobileRisk introduces step-level risk localization for mobile agents, anchoring each label at the step where risk is first initiated and penalizing early and late detections alike 
(
Sun et al., 2026
)
. Its labels, however, mark steps that merely search or browse as unsafe even when no harmful effect ever occurs (Appendix 
A.5
). Phone-Harm provides per-step harm labels with a matched benign subset 
(
Feng et al., 2026
)
, but assigns each label from step-local evidence without access to action outcomes, and does not model when intervention becomes justified. 
MobileVeto
 instead labels an action 
unsafe
 only when the backend verifies its harmful effect, and annotates the step from which intervention becomes justified. Paired with benign counterparts, it jointly evaluates violation detection, intervention timing, and benign-task preservation.

3.2 
Recurrent Task State

Safety decisions often hinge on evidence established far earlier in a trajectory. In Figure 
1
, verifying the recipient selected at 
 requires the number resolved in WhatsApp at 
, which lies outside the local history window; the current screen alone cannot distinguish the authorized recipient, and blocking whenever such evidence is unavailable would interrupt legitimate tasks. MobileGrail therefore maintains a recurrent task state 
 that retains only evidence relevant to subsequent safety decisions, consisting of a 
task context
 
 and 
execution progress
 
.

The task context 
 has two fields. 
Authorized
 records what the agent is authorized to do, including parameters the user left unspecified and the agent resolved from the environment, such as the number read in WhatsApp at 
. 
Unauthorized
 exists to keep the authorized record from being contaminated: instructions that arrive through screen content, such as a prompt injection, and actions that conflict with the authorized parameters, such as the recipient selected at 
, are recorded here as evidence rather than folded into authorization.

The execution progress 
 records the operations the agent has completed so far, described at the level of the task rather than of individual GUI actions. Without it, the guardrail cannot tell whether an operation authorized only once is being repeated. Both components are updated within the same forward pass as the decisions: at each step, MobileGrail regenerates 
 from the previous state and the current interaction, and determines whether the proposed action completes such an operation; if so, the operation is added to 
 once the proposed action is allowed and executed. The resulting 
 is passed to the next step without a separate memory model (Appendix 
B
).

3.3 
Training

Supervised distillation.

MobileGrail is first trained by distillation from a large teacher model; this stage teaches it what constitutes a violation and how application operations behave. Our training corpus contains 21,605 steps from 2,239 trajectories collected in our environment, each annotated with safety labels and its judgment and intervention windows (Appendix 
D.1
). For every step, the teacher, Gemini 3.8 Flash, generates structured targets: the updated recurrent task state, a rationale, the safety verdict, the intervention decision, and feedback when blocking. Its prompt extends the student’s general authorization rules with application-specific boundaries and operation semantics, illustrated by extensive few-shot demonstrations, and the rationale carries this knowledge into the targets: it locates the targeted control and its effect, determines whether the action commits or merely prepares, checks key parameters such as the recipient or amount against the accumulated authorization, and justifies both decisions (Appendix 
D.2
). The teacher additionally produces a one-sentence decision-sufficient rationale, which supervises a short-reasoning variant for latency-sensitive deployment. The student is trained on these targets with a much leaner prompt that omits the application-specific rules and demonstrations, a form of context distillation 
(
Askell et al., 2021
; 
Guan et al., 2025
)
; this forces it to internalize both the safety boundary and the operation knowledge, such as what a control does and whether it commits, that RL exploration cannot readily discover.

We fully fine-tune compact vision-language backbones with 1.6B to 2B parameters. To keep the safety verdict from being diluted by the much longer state and rationale outputs, we average the loss separately over the verdict tokens

4 
Environment and Benchmark

4.1 
A Scalable and Verifiable Environment

Training a guardrail that keeps pace with evolving attacks requires safety data that can be produced at scale and refreshed on demand. Existing mobile resources label steps from GUI observations, either through human annotation 
(
Sun et al., 2026
; 
Feng et al., 2026
)
, which caps them at a few hundred trajectories, or through VLM-as-a-judge 
(
Feng et al., 2026
; 
Yu et al., 2026
)
, which propagates judge errors into supervision; in both cases the actual effect of an action is unverifiable, as production apps expose no backend state. Following self-hosted environments with verifiable outcomes 
(
Kong et al., 2026
; 
Chen et al., 2026c
)
, we build 32 applications (including 22 mocked and 10 instrumented native) spanning seven categories of daily use (Appendix 
C.1
), each with a stateful backend supporting initialization, snapshotting, and per-step diffing. Every executed action is thereby grounded in the state change it induces, which identifies not only whether a violation occurred but 
which step
 committed it. With no real assets at stake, violations such as payments or public sharing can be carried to completion.

4.2 
Automated Trajectory Generation

Figure 
7
 illustrates how trajectories are generated without manual authoring (Appendix 
C.2
). A 
proposer
 designs an attack scenario specifying the user request, attack vector, and target violation, which a 
materializer
 compiles into backend state and injected content. Since unguided agents fail most attacks, a 
planner
 drafts a high-level plan that an 
executor
 agent may follow or disregard. The backend records the effect of each executed action, and a 
verifier
 judges whether it exceeds the user’s authorization. A trajectory is retained as unsafe only if such an effect occurs; the step producing it is recorded as the committing step, which anchors the safety labels, intervention windows, and RL rewards. Each unsafe trajectory is paired with a benign counterpart that performs the same operation either under an authorizing request or with the prohibited content replaced by permissible content. Following strategy-accumulating redteaming 
(
Xiong et al., 2026
; 
Mo et al., 2026
; 
Liu et al., 2025
; 
Chen et al., 2026c
)
, a 
synthesizer
 distills rollout outcomes into reusable attack strategies that guide subsequent proposals.

4.3 
The MobileVeto Benchmark

To evaluate whether a guardrail generalizes beyond the applications it was trained on and draws the safety boundary precisely, we build 
MobileVeto
 with the same pipeline under disjoint scenario configurations, verified by human annotators. Of the 32 applications, 9 are held out of training and serve as the core out-of-distribution set, together covering most everyday usage scenarios, including messaging, stock trading, travel booking, and physical-device control; system and web applications are shared with training and vary only in injection sources and payloads. The benchmark contains 210 unsafe trajectories, each annotated with its intervention window and commit-ambiguous steps and paired with a benign counterpart that performs the same operations under an authorizing request.

Realistic and consequential scenarios.

MobileVeto allows consequential operations to actually execute rather than stop short of them: payments in the mocked WeChat (LinkChat) go through, flight bookings are confirmed and can even add travel insurance, and Vela Auto’s remote controls, from unlocking the doors to sharing a digital key, change the simulated vehicle state. For web cases, phishing sites are served under realistic domains rather than the placeholder addresses common in existing benchmarks 
(
Sun et al., 2026
; 
Chen et al., 2026a
)
, and replicate the genuine service in layout, branding, and form fields, so a guardrail must determine whether the site is the one the user intends rather than rely on surface cues.

Experimental Setup

with the same backbone, EIR drops from 87.2% to 39.3% and B-FPR rises

rather than violations, producing premature unsafe predictions

6 
Conclusion

We presented 
MobileGrail
, a compact guardrail that judges each proposed action of a mobile GUI agent on device. It carries authorization and execution progress in a recurrent task state, and learns two labels: 
unsafe
 for the action that commits a violation, and 
block
 for the decision to intervene, which may come earlier. Both are trained in an environment of 32 applications whose backends verify the effect of every action, with reinforcement learning calibrating intervention timing. On 
MobileVeto
, 
MobileGrail
 built on Qwen3.5-2B intercepts 76.2% of violations, up from 64.5% for its prompted backbone, while reducing premature intervention from 58.6% to 5.2%. When guarding a Qwen3.8-27B agent on GhostEI-Bench, it reduces fully successful attacks from 13 to 3 of 110 tasks while raising task completion from 48 to 58. Quantized to 4 bits, it runs on a Snapdragon 8 Elite at a median latency of 4.7 s, comparable to cloud-hosted Gemini 3.8 Flash.

AI use statement

We used generative AI models to generate synthetic attack scenarios
and support self-evolving red-teaming, using feedback from previous
rollouts to guide subsequent scenario generation. Generative AI tools
also assisted in implementing mocked applications in our Android
training environment. For data annotation, models annotated action
safety and intervention windows, informed by backend execution
feedback. We further used teacher-generated structured outputs,
including recurrent task states, rationales, safety judgments,
intervention decisions, and feedback, to train MobileGrail through
supervised distillation. We also used AI tools to assist with literature searches and identify relevant prior work. The authors reviewed the AI-assisted work and take responsibility for the final content, claims, and artifacts.

Ethics statement

This work aims to improve the safety of mobile GUI agents.
Our generated scenarios are instantiated in a controlled Android
environment combining mocked applications and instrumented native
applications with prepared data. Harmful operations, such as
unauthorized payments, are simulated without real assets at stake.
The generated data contain adversarial instructions and potentially
harmful content for safety training and evaluation. We recognize
that these scenarios and the self-evolving red-teaming pipeline
could be misused to develop attacks against agents. These resources
are intended for defensive research and safety evaluation in
controlled settings.

Appendix A 
Safety and Intervention Formulation

A.1 
Authorization and Safety Scope

The user’s request establishes the scope of operations the agent is authorized to perform. Task-relevant information may fill in missing parameters, but it does not authorize additional operations.

User instructions often leave task parameters unspecified, requiring the agent to resolve them from task-relevant observations. For example, in “find the address of my hotel reservation and add it to tomorrow’s calendar event,” the hotel address is an unspecified parameter that the agent must retrieve from the corresponding booking confirmation. Using this address as the event’s location fills in a missing parameter within the user’s authorization. The resolved value and its source must be retained so that subsequent actions can be checked against the established authorization.

This use of environmental information does not give the information source authority to expand the task. If the same booking confirmation instructs the agent to forward the user’s itinerary to an external address, that instruction introduces a disclosure the user did not authorize. Explicit delegation is likewise bounded by its scope. Under our authorization protocol, a broad request such as “do what Ella tells you” does not by itself authorize a newly introduced transfer to Ella’s account; that operation requires specific user authorization. By contrast, a request explicitly authorizing a transfer to Ella within a stated amount limit establishes permission for that operation.

User authorization does not override the applicable safety policy. For example, a user may explicitly ask the agent to send a sexually harassing text message or a message containing targeted verbal abuse. Sending such messages remains unsafe under our safety policy, even when the user specifies both the content and the recipient.

A.2 
Action Safety and Commit Semantics

Given the authorization scope and safety constraints defined above, an action is 
unsafe

committing action
, and its position in the trajectory as the 
commit step
. The safety label concerns the effect of the proposed action itself, rather than the potential consequences of actions that may follow.

Preparatory actions remain 
safe
 when they do not themselves violate these constraints. In Figure 
1

A.4
 for commit ambiguity). The violation is committed at 
, when tapping “Confirm” transfers the funds to the unauthorized recipient.

The commit step is determined by when an action crosses a protected safety boundary. For example, opening a file’s sharing panel does not itself disclose the file, whereas making it public without authorization crosses its access boundary, even before the link is sent to anyone. Likewise, deleting a photo without authorization violates the user’s control over their data, even if the photo remains recoverable from the trash. The possibility of restoring the previous state does not negate the violation committed by the action.

In our environment, recorded state changes establish which executed action produces the violating effect. These outcomes provide supervision for a guardrail that must predict action safety before execution, without access to the resulting state change. Each action is assessed for its own effects: an earlier violation does not automatically make every subsequent action unsafe.

Our treatment of this ambiguity is motivated by generalization. A guardrail should not need to memorize which applications require an additional confirmation after each button press. Such behavior may differ across applications, versions, and workflows. Strictly requiring a safe prediction whenever an application happens to provide another confirmation step can encourage reliance on these application-specific conventions. If the guardrail incorrectly assumes that another confirmation will follow and therefore predicts safe and postpones blocking, the current action may already commit the violation. We therefore avoid penalizing an unsafe prediction solely because an additional confirmation screen is revealed afterward.

We designate non-committing actions as commit-ambiguous when the available evidence does not clearly establish whether they immediately execute an operation that would violate authorization or safety constraints. Their ground-truth labels remain safe, as verified by their actual effects, but either a safe or an unsafe verdict is accepted in annotation and evaluation. For an unauthorized link deletion in Figure 
2
(a–b), this tolerance applies to the initial “Delete link” action; the subsequent confirmation requires an unsafe verdict. An unsafe prediction at a designated ambiguous step also requires blocking under the runtime consistency rule.

This tolerance concerns uncertainty about when a violating effect occurs, not whether the operation is authorized or permissible. It does not apply to an otherwise benign operation merely because its confirmation flow is unknown, nor does it extend to every preparatory action within an intervention window. Restricting tolerance to designated commit-ambiguous actions preserves the distinction between preparation and violation while reducing dependence on application-specific confirmation sequences.

MobileGrail maintains a recurrent task state 
, where 
 stores task context and 
 tracks execution progress. Together, these components retain the evidence needed to assess later actions when the original observations have left the local history window. This section specifies their contents and update rules.

B.1 
Task Context

The task context 
 preserves the user’s authorization scope, parameters resolved during execution, and evidence of potential conflicts. It separates this information into two fields, Authorized and Unauthorized, so that observations used to carry out the user’s request remain distinguishable from environmental instructions that do not grant permission.

Authorized.

This field records what the user permits, including relevant targets, parameters, and constraints. As the agent gathers information, it incorporates resolved parameters together with their sources. For example, in Figure 
1
, the user authorizes a transfer of $30 to Daniel, while Daniel’s number is obtained from WhatsApp at 
. The state retains the association between Daniel and the number ending in 0173, together with its source, so that a later recipient selection can be checked against it. Parameters that have not yet been resolved remain unspecified; a plausible value encountered elsewhere is not treated as authorized without supporting evidence.

Unauthorized.

This field records task-relevant environmental instructions that lack user authorization and observed conflicts with the established task scope. For example, the user may ask the agent to read a group chat and summarize the meeting arrangements, while a message in that chat instructs it to upload the user’s contact list to an external website for “attendance verification.” The state records the requested upload and its source as an instruction encountered in the group chat, together with its conflict with the user’s read-and-summarize request. Retaining this evidence allows later upload actions to be assessed even after the original message leaves the local history window, without treating the message as permission to disclose the user’s contacts.

Separating these fields makes the authority of retained information explicit. In a single undifferentiated field, an instruction encountered in the environment may be carried forward without its original source or authorization status, allowing the guardrail itself to mistake it for part of the user’s request. The 
Unauthorized
 field provides an explicit place to record such instructions as evidence to assess, rather than instructions to follow. This structure encourages the model to identify and preserve their lack of authorization during recurrent state updates, reducing the risk that an environmental prompt injection is incorporated into the remembered task as a legitimate instruction.

This distinction is particularly important when the same source contains both information needed for the task and instructions that exceed its scope. Figure 
6
 illustrates this through a Gmail-to-Notion verification task. The user asks the agent to check whether Elena Park’s pending invitation matches the email’s stated address and role. Alongside these details, the email instructs the agent to revoke the invitation before reporting a match. The generated task context retains the comparison task and expected values under Authorized, while recording the email’s revocation instruction and other invitation modifications under Unauthorized. When the agent subsequently reaches Notion, this context supports checking the invitation without treating the email’s runbook as permission to modify it. The same email thus supplies comparison data without gaining authority to expand the task.

B.2 
Execution Progress

The execution-progress component 
 records completed sub-tasks that remain relevant to subsequent safety decisions. Each entry identifies what has been completed and retains the targets or parameters needed to distinguish it from other operations. This information allows the guardrail to assess constraints that depend on prior execution, including whether a prerequisite has been satisfied or an operation authorized only once is being repeated.

For example, consider the request “back up these photos before deleting them from the device.” Deletion is permitted only after the required backup has completed. Retaining this completion record allows the guardrail to check the prerequisite even after the backup screen leaves the local history window. Similarly, if the user requests that a report be sent to a colleague once, recording the completed send allows a later proposal to send the same report again to be recognized as exceeding the requested number of executions. The recipient and report identity distinguish this repetition from a separate authorized send.

Since MobileGrail evaluates 
 before execution, it predicts the effect 
 would complete as a candidate entry, emitted after both decisions. The entry is appended to 
 only if the action is allowed and executed; blocked actions add nothing. A candidate is emitted only when current evidence establishes that the action would produce a specific persistent effect: opening a backup screen or initiating an upload does not by itself establish that the backup has finished. Preparation, navigation, and actions with uncertain outcomes produce no entry.

C.2 
Trajectory Generation

Figure 
7
 summarizes the generation
pipeline. A proposer designs scenarios, a materializer prepares
the environment, a planner outlines the workflow, and a victim
agent executes GUI actions. A verifier checks execution outcomes,
while a synthesizer extracts reusable strategies to guide
subsequent proposals.

Scenario proposal and materialization.

The proposer is supplied with application manuals describing
supported functionality and application-specific forms of unsafe
behavior. These include, for example, sending messages to
unauthorized recipients, disclosing files beyond the permitted
audience, and transferring funds to unintended accounts.
The manuals make the available operations and relevant safety
boundaries explicit, helping the proposer formulate scenarios
that can be instantiated in the environment. Each proposal
specifies the user request, attack vector, target violation,
and content required for the scenario.

The materializer instantiates the proposal using a predefined
collection of setup functions, including application-specific
injection functions. The model selects functions and supplies
their arguments, while the implementations issue backend requests
to populate the corresponding application state. For example,
a message-based scenario requires preparing the conversation
and message content that the victim will encounter. This stage
turns the proposal into a device state containing the records,
resources, and injected content needed for execution.

Planning and execution.

Victim models may be unfamiliar with the environment’s workflows,
and unguided rollouts often yield low attack success rates.
We therefore adopt a semi-open-book setup: a planner provides
high-level task goals that help the victim navigate the scenario.
For example, a plan may suggest reading an onboarding email,
opening the workspace’s pending invitations, and comparing the
listed email address and role with those in the message.
The victim receives these goals alongside the user request,
while selecting the specific GUI actions from the observed
interface. The plan provides workflow guidance, and the victim’s
response to the scenario content is established through execution.

Outcome verification.

After each executed action, the environment returns backend
feedback describing the operation performed and its resulting
state changes. This per-step feedback is the primary verification
signal. The verifier assesses it against the user request and
scenario specification to determine whether the action produced
a violating effect. For example, opening a transfer confirmation
screen does not establish that funds were transferred, whereas
a backend record of a completed transfer identifies the executed
operation and its recipient and amount. This feedback allows
each verified violation to be attributed to the action that
committed it. Multiple violations within a trajectory are
recorded as separate events.

the verifier’s backend-grounded findings. The synthesizer

attack strategies.

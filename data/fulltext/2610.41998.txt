Abstract

Recognized as a top threat to AI agents, prompt injection allows instructions from untrusted data (email, documents, websites) to manipulate the agent’s operation.
The existing best-defended LLM, Meta-SecAlign, is trained with the DPO loss but still suffers from attack success rates (ASRs) approaching 100% under adaptive prompt injections. One reason is that DPO anchors the model around the undefended reference model, which is insecure.
We show an idealized reference model—an injection-invariant, utility-preserving oracle—can nevertheless be approximated, as its output distribution in training is interestingly accessible.
This new observation motivates a theoretically principled SecPO loss.
Another Meta-SecAlign shortcoming is that it trains on weak simulated attacks. We implement an efficient adversarial training (AT) pipeline that synthesizes strong injected inputs (best@240), and train the model on them.
Our AT reduces ASRs by 1.5–14.5
 across three SoTA adaptive attacks, compared with Meta-SecAlign; the SecPO loss reduces them by a further 2.4–4.6
 compared with using DPO loss.
In agentic workflows, our SecPO + AT 
Qwen3.6-27B
 is much more secure than Meta-SecAlign: 8.8% vs. 78.8% AgentDojo ASR@800 under the AttackerMovesSecond attack. Moreover, our defended model shows no noticeable reduction in benign utility in nine benchmarks covering agentic tool calling, instruction following, science, and math.
We release our 
code
, 
training data
 (with optimized injections from AT), and 
model
.

Preliminaries

AI agents take in untrusted inputs that may be injected.
 An AI agent accepts trusted and untrusted inputs. In most real-world cases, it is clear which inputs are trusted (like a user command, a trusted website, an email from someone you know) or untrusted (like an untrusted website, a random email, an untraceable file, a copied paragraph). Thus, the prompt injection problem is defined under the assumption that the agent is told which parts are trusted and should not follow untrusted instructions.
The prompt injection problem excludes cases where the trust label is unknown and needs to be determined by the agent, e.g., using the surrounding context 
[
1
]
, or where instructions in data need be followed if they are aligned with the user prompt 
[
86
]
.

Prompt injection attack.
 At test time, the attacker injects instructions into data, aiming to execute an attacker-specific task. A prompt injection attack is deemed successful if the LLM completes the task in the injected instruction. We assume the attacker knows the benign prompt, benign data, and the LLM’s chat template, but cannot change them. This assumption favors the attacker, as when the attacker targets an unknown victim agent in the real world, it may not be able to know the complete benign contexts.
Existing automatic adaptive prompt injection methods include RL-based attacks 
[

, 
97
, 
15

[
107

71

 that optimize an attack suffix in a white-box manner.
The attacker can resort to black-box RL-based and search-based attacks to break any deployed model–our evaluations cover both attacks. We assume the test-time attacker cannot access the model weights to perform gradient-based attacks, which produce non-readable attacks that can be easily mitigated by perplexity-based classifiers

 A robust AI agent is expected to ignore any injections when there is an attack, and have utility similar to the undefended agent’s when there is no attack.

3.5 
SecPO algorithm with adversarial training

SecPO loss resolves one drawback of Meta-SecAlign by changing its suboptimal training objective. Another drawback of Meta-SecAlign is that it trains on weak static simulated injections. As a result, Meta-SecAlign learns to defend against weak attacks but struggles to defend against strong adaptive attacks. A straightforward fix is to train on prompt injection attacks that are as strong as possible 
[
85
]
, so that the final LLM is robust to stronger unseen attacks at test time.
This is also known as adversarial training (AT).

AT for LLMs is extremely expensive.
At each step of training, AT involves attacking the latest checkpoint and training against this attacked input.
This process is already costly for image classification, where AT was originally developed 
[
31
]
.
For LLMs, it is even more expensive, because finding injection tokens in the discrete token space is much harder than finding adversarial images in continuous pixel space 
[
107
]
, making prompt injection generation very slow. For this reason, no one in the literature has reported success in using AT to defend against prompt injection (see

Judge Protocol.
 Each SEP example contains an injected instruction and an instance-specific witness word. The witness word is expected to always appear if an injection is followed, and never appear if not. Thus, witness word appearance is the official criterion to judge attack success in 
[
108
]
. We find this substring matching is highly unreliable, e.g., the word “cat” will be regarded as appearing in “indicate”.
Thus, each candidate response is further evaluated by 9 independent judge queries to 3 judge LLMs (
Gemini-3.1-Flash-Lite-Preview, Gemini-3-Flash-Preview, GPT-5
, temperature=1). The prompt includes the trusted task, injection goal, victim response, in-context demonstrations of successful/unsuccessful attacks, and asks whether the response actually complies with the injected goal. We deem a sample successfully attacked if all nine judge calls confirm success.
To inspect the accuracy of this judge protocol, we manually look at 1,024 outputs drawn from Genetic/TAP adaptive SEP evaluations. The
nine-judge rule gave 0 false positives and 0 false negatives against these labels. The official substring match rule gave 67 false positives and 0 false negatives, often because the witness appeared inside another word. This audit supports our multi-judge rule as a good success criterion.

Remaining successful attacks against our defended model exhibit social engineering patterns.
 We audit seven successful AgentDojo Genetic attacks and 140 successful DTaP attacks, and find that injections often present unauthorized actions as necessary steps for completing the user’s task. In

5 
Related Work

Meta-SecAlign 
[
14
]
 optimizes a DPO loss over simulated weak static injected inputs. We propose to optimize a SecPO loss over simulated strong adaptive injected inputs. Here, we discuss more related work around DPO loss variants and AT.

Existing 
DPO variants
 focus on improving utility or alignment (mitigating jailbreaks 
[
43
, 
57
]
, not prompt injection). SBA 
[
44
]
 and WPO

[
104
]
 explicitly reweight per-example gradients. Pre-DPO 
[
70
]
, HyPO 
[
99
]
, ODPO 
[
3
]
, 
-DPO 
[
45
]
, and MODPO 
[
105
]
 also implicitly reweight per-example gradients by signals from generic optimization dynamics, compatibility with a reference, or externally specified preferences. 
[
70
, 
51
]
 noted that the choice of reference acts as a sample-reweighting mechanism. However, there have been no DPO variants motivated by mitigating prompt injections and reweighting based on samples’ injection sensitivity.
SecPO is different from the methods above in its goal of defending against prompt injections, and its motivation to approximate an oracle that raises security while maintaining high utility.

Classical 
adversarial training
 (AT) trains image classifiers on perturbations generated against the current checkpoint 
[
31
, 
56
, 
11
]
.
For LLMs, the idea of AT has been explored to mitigate jailbreaks 
[
94
, 
82
, 
24
, 
72
, 
90
]
.
Earlier open prompt injection defense recipes do not simulate strong adaptive attacks 
[
13
, 
10
, 
30
]
. Production-scale AT recipes against prompt injection remain proprietary 
[
33
, 
85
]
, with only incomplete artifacts released 
[
33
]
. AEGIS 
[
50
]
 optimizes defensive prompts against simulated strong adaptive prompt injections, rather than optimizing model parameters as we do.
A recent AT method, ARLAS 
[
89
]
, reports little (1%) security gain even against static weak attacks on AgentDojo. ARLAS and another concurrent method, CoER 
[
102
]
, simulate RL-based attacks, whereas we simulate search-based attacks in training.

6 
Limitations

We focus on mitigating indirect prompt injection under the assumption that agents receive explicit labels distinguishing trusted instructions from untrusted data.
Our formulation does not cover cases in which these labels are unavailable or the user submits malicious requests.
Our main SecPO experiments use non-reasoning settings; for 
Qwen3.6-27B
, we disable thinking during training and evaluation. Extending SecPO training to reasoning models remains future work. Our defense aims to preserve the undefended model’s utility and does not specifically address its existing errors, such as hallucinations or incorrect tool use. The high cost of adaptive attack evaluations limits our ability to systematically assess hyperparameter sensitivity.
Stronger future attacks may defeat our model, just as newly discovered attacks can compromise defenses previously considered secure 
[
63
, 
36
, 
62
]
. Security therefore requires continued evaluation and improvement, and no single defense should be assumed to address every threat.

7 
Discussion

In our evaluations, training an LLM on simulated attacks provides security against stronger, unseen attacks at test time. Our strong security is achieved in a way that does not conflict with utility preserving. The robustness learned from instruction-following training transfers to complex agentic tasks without additional defense training in the target domain.

Our work shows that a model alone can be very robust, weakening the story "you cannot rely on a model to be secure". Real-world systems require multiple layers of defense, such as robust models, security classifiers or detectors, and sandboxes. Each layer is necessary though cannot be perfectly secure, see failure examples of sandboxes 
[
68
, 
98
, 
6
]
 which were believed to offer guaranteed security. We advocate joint efforts from the community to build secure agents.

Other applications of SecPO.

The SecPO loss applies when a suitable target output distribution is available and high-quality preference pairs can be constructed. We demonstrate this setting for prompt injection defense, using a target distribution that combines security with utility preservation and preference pairs consisting of secure and insecure responses. We hypothesize that the SecPO loss may also benefit tasks beyond security.

Progress towards different prompt injection security goals.

Prior work has considered three prompt injection defense goals: (1) disregard injected content when carrying out the trusted task 
[
14
]
; (2) process instruction-like content, such as imperative sentences, in untrusted data as data without obeying it 
[
49
]
; (3) follow an instruction in untrusted data only if it is consistent with the trusted prompt 
[
86
]
. Goal (1) excludes the injected content from the task response, whereas goal (2) allows that content to be processed when the trusted task requires it. For goal (3), apparent relevance to the task should not be confused with authorization by the trusted prompt. Our training data targets (1), and our evaluations demonstrate substantial progress toward this objective. We hypothesize existing data curation methods 
[
49
]
 could improve performance on (2). Reliably achieving (3) remains an open direction.

Is prompt injection solved?

We do not think so. Recent posts report substantial progress in prompt injection resistance 
[
18
, 
42
, 
67
]
, and may have left the impression that this problem has been effectively solved. We observe excessive refusals on benign tasks in some frontier APIs designed for security. For example, 
Opus-5.0
 
[
74
, 
5
]
, discussed in an Anthropic post 
[
18
]
, achieves only 70.0% utility on AgentDyn, compared with 71.7% for the undefended 
Qwen3.6-27B
 and 83.3% for 
GPT-5.5
. In our inspection, excessive refusals contribute substantially to this lower utility. Thus, to achieve powerful and secure agents, future efforts are necessary.

The next generation of prompt injection attacks.

Attacks that succeed against our best model exhibit social-engineering patterns. Successful injections often disguise themselves as locally necessary steps in the user’s existing workflow. This indicates future attacks are moving beyond forceful commands, towards subtle, non-commanding language that lowers an agent’s guard through social engineering. Alternatively, an attacker may supply misleading information, such as a false bug report, that causes an agent to infer that an attacker-desired action is necessary without an explicit injected instruction. For example, the agent might download an attacker-specified package to fix the reported bug. Further research will be needed to mitigate attacks based on social engineering and misleading information, and the challenge lies in desired behavior that is harder to specify under such circumstances, as humans can be deceived in a similar way.

Defenders need an advantage: frontier models should withhold attack capabilities.

Strong adaptive prompt injection pipelines use LLMs to generate and optimize attacks against other LLMs 
[
65
, 
97
]
. This dependence creates an opportunity for defenders: frontier models can limit attackers’ access to attack-generation capabilities by refusing to help construct attacks. We observe such refusals from recent 
Claude
, 
GPT
, 
Gemini
, and 
Mercury
 models, while 
Kimi-K3.1-Pro
, 
GLM-5.3(-Flash)
, and 
DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash
 remain willing to assist. If attackers are restricted to less capable models while defenders retain authorized access to stronger attack generators, defenders could use this advantage to construct stronger adversarial training data. We hypothesize that this advantage could improve robustness to attacks generated by less capable models.

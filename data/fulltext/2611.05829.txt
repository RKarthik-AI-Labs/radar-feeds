Abstract

Researchers frequently encounter difficulties re-finding scientific papers when they remember aspects of the content but cannot recall reliable identifiers, such as titles or author names. This type of re-finding task, known as tip-of-the-tongue (ToT) retrieval, is particularly challenging for scientific documents, where recollections may involve textual information and visual elements, including figures, diagrams, and plots. Existing ToT retrieval benchmarks do not address scientific search.
We introduce a multimodal benchmark for scientific ToT retrieval, comprising 108,512 open-access scientific documents across five domains and 11,499 synthetically generated queries. To construct the benchmark, we propose a scalable query elicitation pipeline that independently extracts textual and visual memory cues from source documents and uses large language models to generate queries resembling incomplete human recollections. We characterize scientific ToT information needs through an empirical analysis of publicly available re-finding requests and use the outcomes to assess the generated queries using human annotations and automated quality control. The benchmark includes lexical, dense, late-interaction, and visually grounded retrieval baselines. We release the dataset, extracted document representations, generated memory cues, evaluation splits, and accompanying generation and retrieval pipelines under an MIT license to facilitate research on multimodal scientific paper re-finding.

 Retrieval

Arguello et al. [8]

[

, 
7

 introduce a domain-diverse and partially multilingual

ToT

2.2 
Scientific Literature Search

Known-item search is especially prevalent in scientific literature search, with resources reporting up to 57% of queries as directly looking for a known item 
[
56
, 
69
, 
83
, 
42

[

13

Schultheiß et al. [69]

23
, 
60

21

Scientific Tip-of-the-Tongue Information Needs

We follow the definition of a 
ToT
 information need by 
Arguello et al. [8]
 as “an item identification task where the searcher has previously experienced or consumed the item, but cannot recall a reliable identifier.” We consider two requirements for successful scientific ToT query elicitation:

(i)
 
alignment of the query with ToT information needs, assessed through an empirical characterization of re-finding requests in the scientific domain (
Section
 
3.1
).

(ii)
 
alignment of the query with the target document, which we evaluate through human annotation as well as LLM-based filtering (
Sections
 
4.5
 and 
4.6
)

.

4.2 
Visual and Textual Element Extraction

To generate clues and enable textual and visual retrieval methods, we extract visual and textual elements from PDF documents. Content extraction from documents is a substantial field of research 
[
50
, 
43
, 
58
, 
79
, 
80
, 
76
]
. Since content extraction is not the focus of this work, we rely on MinerU 
[
76
]
, which satisfies the requirements of being open-source, has a low compute cost and is fine-tuned for scientific documents. For each document, we extract the text into markdown, and extract figures and tables into separate images. Qualitative inspection of extracted documents indicated that textual content generally preserved the source material and that figures and tables were generally correctly identified.

4.3 
Memory Clue Extraction

To facilitate query generation, we generate potentially remembered clues from the target document. Memory clues are extracted from both images and text. To ensure that clues are only related to a single modality and element, we separate clue extraction for text and images, following previous work on query generation 
[
72
]
.

For textual clues, we apply Qwen3-4B 
[
82
]
, providing it with instructions and the text of the paper, truncated to the token limit, following 
He et al. [41]
. The model is instructed to output JSON-formatted text with a set of unstylized derived clues that are non-specific, and to exclude named entities or directly identifying information. For visual clues, we apply Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct 
[
11
]
. The model is provided with a single image and is instructed to derive clues about the structural appearance as well as high-level semantic elements.

4.4 
ToT
 Query Elicitation

Our 
ToT
 query elicitation generates a query from the set of clues of the target document. We apply Phi-4 14B 
[
1
]
, using a different model provider from cue extraction to reduce single-provider bias and stylistic monotonicity 
[
71
, 
17
, 
49
, 
84
]
. We generate queries based on both modalities, selecting the visual clues of at most 15 figures to stay within token limits. We closely follow the prompt style of 
He et al. [41]
, providing the model with general role-play instructions, the clue set, a set of strict requirements (musts), and optional requirements (coulds). The prompt was adjusted to align with the scientific re-finding task, instructing the model to apply the clues exactly, and to follow the clue style elements described in 
Section
 
3.1
. We exclude instruction of the model to provide temporal or physical contextual clues, as these would require further complex user simulation, without adding a significant new challenge for retrieval. For visual elements, we instruct the model to focus on figures that stand out to it, as these are most likely to be remembered 
[
52
, 
44
]
. All prompts are made available in our code repository.

4.5 
Human Annotation

To assess the quality of generated 
ToT
 queries, we conduct a manual evaluation of 100 queries sampled from the test set with 20 queries per scientific domain. Two independent annotators with research backgrounds, neither involved in the development of the benchmark, evaluate all 100 query–document pairs. Annotators are presented with the generated query and the full source document and independently assess two dimensions: relevance, measuring the correspondence between the query and its source document, and well-formedness, assessing the clarity and interpretability of the query. Both dimensions are evaluated on three-point scales (0–2), corresponding to non-relevant, partially relevant, and highly relevant for relevance, and not well-formed, partially well-formed, and well-formed for well-formedness, aligning with previous work 
[
47
, 
66
, 
77
]
. Annotators received written guidelines containing label definitions and illustrative examples and completed a calibration round using a separate set of examples covering all annotation categories during a one-hour training session. We describe annotator judgments and report the label distributions and inter-annotator agreement using weighted Cohen’s 
 for each dimension. Annotator instructions are made available in our code repository.

4.6 
Filtering

We systematically verify the alignment of the target documents with the generated query. We do so by applying LLM-as-a-Judge to verify whether the query components are supported by the document, and if the final query aligns with the document in general. We apply Gemma4-12B-IT 
[
74
]
, aligning with previous patterns of applying a different model family for evaluation 
[
30
]
, as well as using a model with long-context capacity to ensure sufficient encoding capacity 
[
73
]
. The judge is supplied with the figures and full text of the target document, as well as the target query. The judge is instructed to assess each remembered component conservatively, and provide judgments with justifications; following this, it is instructed to take all components into account, and grade the target query on the same three-point scale as used by annotators. In filtering documents, we follow the approach of 
Weller et al. [81]
, discarding documents that do not align with the target. We discard all 0 relevance score elements for the training and test set, and report on the agreement with annotators.

Dataset Validation

Validation of generated queries is carried out following the two components described in 
Section
 
3
. We first describe accuracy statistics for the Query dataset derived from human relevance annotations and LLM-as-a-Judge filtering, and then detail the statistical and qualitative alignment with tip-of-the-tongue queries.

5.1 
Query-Document Alignment

Table
 
3
 summarizes human evaluations of 100 test queries. For relevance, 92.5% of annotations were annotated as highly relevant, although inter-annotator agreement was limited, potentially reflecting the known sensitivity of Cohen’s 
 to highly skewed label distributions 
[
36
, 
24
]
. For well-formedness, 94.5% of annotations received the highest score, but no meaningful chance-corrected agreement was observed. We attribute this to differences in annotators’ preferences regarding query formulation, and limited difference in query formulation quality.

In 
Table
 
4
 we show the LLM judge results, and agreement with human annotators. Overall, we observe the judge is slightly more lenient than human annotators, and assigns a score of 2 more often. We do find that it is better aligned with human judges than inter-human agreement, with higher agreement for both annotators. Overall, the automated judgments should primarily be interpreted as a filtering mechanism rather than a fine-grained measure of query quality. Across the full dataset, the judge identifies two non-relevant queries, which were manually confirmed and removed. The relatively modest agreement with human annotations limits the conclusions that can be drawn about the reliability of this filtering process. These findings support the overall quality of the generated queries while highlighting differences in evaluation judgments.

5.2 
ToT Query Alignment

We assess alignment between generated and real-world ToT queries using the annotation procedure described in 
Section
 
3.1
, applying the same codebook and annotators to 
 generated queries. Figure 
3
 compares code frequencies across three groups separated by dashed lines. The rightmost group contains contextual elements deliberately excluded from generation, as they require modelling user history beyond paper content, as noted in 
Section
 
4.4
. The middle group comprises visual elements, which are overrepresented by design to emphasize multimodal retrieval. The leftmost group contains elements moderated in frequency by the generation prompt, where we find high agreement, with an average 
 difference in frequency. A notable difference includes that we observe fewer specific claims, likely because the selected sample of real-world queries included many mathematics-related requests for proofs. We also find more references to methods, document structure, and applications, which can be explained by the tendency of LLM-extracted clues to emphasize these aspects. Overall, generated queries reproduce much of the diversity and relative frequency of characteristics observed in real-world ToT queries.

6 
Retrieval Benchmark

Scientific 
ToT
 retrieval inherits several challenges from traditional 
ToT
 retrieval, including incomplete recollections and uncertain descriptions 
[
8
, 
41
]
. These challenges are compounded in scientific literature search, where relevant information may be confined to a small portion of a lengthy document. To facilitate future work and provide insights into the challenges associated with our dataset, we provide implementations and evaluations of several baseline methods.

We evaluate scientific 
ToT
 retrieval as a known-item search task, where the objective is to retrieve the single target document from a collection based on an incomplete recollection query. Each scientific domain is evaluated independently, allowing future work to investigate both domain-specific retrieval effectiveness and cross-domain generalization. Since each query has a single target document, we report MRR@10, Recall@1, Recall@10, and Recall@100.

6.1 
Retrieval Baselines

We evaluate pretrained retrieval baselines across four
configurations: textual, visual, page-level, and multimodal
retrieval. For textual retrieval, we consider BM25 
[
67
]

for lexical matching, E5-small-v2 
[
78
]
 and Qwen3-Emb-0.6B 
[
82
]
 for dense semantic retrieval, and ColBERTv2 
[
68
]

for token-level late interaction. For visual and page-level
retrieval, we evaluate OpenCLIP 
[
26
]

and Qwen3-VL 
[
11
]
. For page-level retrieval, we also apply ColQwen2.5, a variant of the ColPali late interaction method for VLM retrieval 
[
35
]
. We additionally evaluate Qwen3-VL for multimodal retrieval using both extracted text and figures. All exact model versions and implementation details can be found in our public repository.

All methods are evaluated without fine-tuning.
Textual methods use extracted paper text, truncated to satisfy model input limits. OpenCLIP encodes individual figures or pages, using maximum cosine similarity for relevance scores. For Qwen3-VL we jointly encode all available inputs for each configuration, truncated to the token limit, producing a single document-level embedding to allow for in-document context. ColQwen2.5 is designed as a page-level method, to retain this architecture, we apply individual page embeddings and use the max-similarity in the ranking stage 
[
35
]
. To reduce the size of the index to a feasible size for our storage budgets, we apply built-in hierarchical token pooling.

8 
Conclusion

We have introduced SciToT, a benchmark for scientific 
ToT
 retrieval, comprising over 100K scientific documents across five domains. We have proposed a scalable query elicitation pipeline that combines textual and visual memory cues to generate queries reflecting incomplete recollections of scientific papers. Human evaluation has demonstrated high query–document alignment and well-formedness, while an empirical comparison with real-world scientific re-finding requests showed that the generated queries capture many of their characteristics.

Our retrieval experiments highlighted the challenges of scientific 
ToT
 retrieval. Conventional lexical and dense retrieval baselines achieved limited effectiveness, but more expressive document representations improved retrieval performance, highlighting the importance of matching remembered information to localized content within scientific documents and capturing visual elements.

The synthetic nature of the queries in the SciToT benchmark and their deliberate emphasis on visual clues may limit their representativeness of naturally occurring requests. Users of SciToT should be mindful of these limitations when training or fine-tuning their retrieval models. We encourage future work on expanding the coverage and resources of SciToT, in terms of both 
ToT
 query (sub)types and additional scientific domains. We also call for research on generalizability across these two dimensions.

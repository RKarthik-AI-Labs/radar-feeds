Abstract

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) typically optimises evidence selection
for semantic relevance, whereas compliance-sensitive long-form generation
also requires evidence supporting explicit structural obligations.
Semantically relevant retrieval may therefore omit information needed for
mandatory downstream clauses. We propose Constraint-Aligned RAG

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated strong capabilities in conditional text generation and few-shot adaptation on a wide range of natural language processing tasks 
(
Brown et al., 2020
)
. However, many real-world long-form generation tasks, including contracts, policy reports, clinical documentation, regulatory disclosures, and enterprise workflow artefacts, require outputs that satisfy explicit structural and compliance constraints in addition to factual relevance. In these settings, generation quality depends not only on retrieving semantically related evidence, but also on preserving clause-level completeness and document-level consistency across extended contexts.

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) improves grounded generation by incorporating external evidence during decoding 
Lewis et al. (2021)
. However, existing RAG pipelines optimise retrieval primarily for semantic similarity to the input query, largely independent of the structural requirements that govern the final document 
Zhao et al. (2024)
; 
Gao et al. (2024)
. Semantically relevant retrieval may still omit structurally required evidence, leading to omission failures, inconsistent sections, and incomplete policy synthesis in long-form generation settings. We hypothesise that a substantial subset of these failures arises from structurally incomplete retrieval rather than decoding alone.

Recent work has explored integrating structured knowledge graphs (KG) and relational reasoning into language models to improve grounding and interpretability 
Yasunaga et al. (2022)
. However, these approaches primarily focus on reasoning and answer selection rather than retrieval for long-form generation. Similarly, existing query expansion, reranking, and prompt-based control methods improve semantic recall or generation control without explicitly conditioning retrieval on downstream structural requirements.

We propose Constraint-Aligned RAG (CA-RAG), a retrieval-conditioning framework that incorporates ontology-derived schema constraints and policy-grounded relational dependencies prior to evidence selection. Unlike query expansion or reranking, CA-RAG does not simply improve semantic recall or reorder retrieved passages. Instead, structural constraints condition which evidence becomes retrievable for downstream generation. Ontology constraints encode document-level structure, while knowledge graph relations capture dependencies between clauses, entities, and policy conditions. By aligning retrieval with downstream requirements, CA-RAG aims to improve structural completeness and reduces omission failures in long-form generation.

We evaluate 
CA-RAG
 across education, finance, and healthcare
using three open-weight LLMs and multiple retrieval baselines. We additionally evaluate retrieval independently using manually judged evidence to isolate retrieval-stage effects. Results show improved conventional retrieval performance and domain-dependent changes in structural evidence sufficiency, with downstream gains in compliance and reliability across several model–domain settings. These findings highlight the value of structurally sufficient evidence for compliance-sensitive long-form generation.

Related Work

Retrieval-Augmented Generation.

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) improves grounded text generation by conditioning language models on external evidence retrieved at inference time 
Lewis et al. (2021)
. Subsequent work has extended RAG across adaptive retrieval strategies, retrieval architectures, and knowledge-intensive tasks, including question answering, summarisation, and long-context generation 
Zhao et al. (2024)
; 
Gao et al. (2024)
. However, most RAG systems optimise retrieval primarily for semantic similarity to the input query, without explicitly considering whether the retrieved evidence is structurally sufficient for downstream long-form generation. Consequently, semantically relevant retrieval may still produce incomplete evidence for section-wise synthesis and clause-level consistency.

Controlled, Knowledge-Grounded, and Adaptive Retrieval Methods.

A complementary line of work explores structure-aware and controllable generation through hierarchical planning, dynamic content control, and constrained decoding 
Hu et al. (2022)
; 
You et al. (2023)
; 
Stolfo et al. (2025)
. Other approaches integrate structured knowledge graphs and relational reasoning to improve factual grounding and interpretability 
Yasunaga et al. (2022)
; 
Li et al. (2024)
. Adaptive retrieval methods such as 
Self-RAG
 
Asai et al. (2023)
 and 
Flare-RAG
 
Jiang et al. (2023b)
 further refine retrieval dynamically during generation to improve factual consistency and response quality.

Despite these advances, existing approaches generally target semantic relevance, evidence refinement, relational grounding, or generation-time control rather than explicitly conditioning retrieval on the structural requirements of the target document. Query expansion methods typically introduce semantically related terms, KG-based retrieval incorporates structured relational knowledge, and re-ranking methods reorder candidate passages using downstream relevance signals. In contrast, CA-RAG conditions retrieval using ontology-derived and policy-grounded structural constraints before evidence selection, explicitly guiding retrieval toward structurally sufficient evidence for downstream generation.

Positioning of This Work.

CA-RAG therefore shifts the focus from improving semantic relevance alone to enforcing structural sufficiency during retrieval itself. This directly targets omission failures arising from incomplete retrieval by constraining evidence selection before generation begins.

Domains and Tasks

For each domain, we curate corpora of regulatory policies, governance documents, operational guidelines, and domain ontologies. Conclusion

We presented 
CA-RAG
, a retrieval-conditioning framework that aligns
evidence selection with structural and policy requirements for
compliance-sensitive long-form generation. Retrieval-only evaluation
with manually annotated evidence shows improved evidence selection across

, with 
CA-RAG
 improving Precision@
, pooled
Recall@
, and F1@
 over standard RAG. Structural evidence sufficiency
varies across domains, with gains in constraint and clause-evidence coverage
most apparent in education and healthcare, while finance remains more
challenging. Generation experiments show corresponding improvements in compliance and reliability in several model–domain settings, although gains remain model and domain dependent. These findings suggest that semantic relevance alone can be insufficient for omission-sensitive long-form generation, and that
retrieval-stage structural alignment can improve evidence sufficiency before
generation.

Limitations

While 
CA-RAG
 improves structural completeness and reduces omission failures, several limitations remain.

Dependence on structured resources.

Our approach relies on the availability and quality of domain ontologies and policy-grounded knowledge graphs. In domains where such structured resources are incomplete, noisy, or unavailable, retrieval conditioning may be less effective. Although ontology and KG construction are largely automated, missing or incorrect relations can propagate into retrieval conditioning.

No guarantee of complete retrieval.

Constraint-aligned retrieval increases the likelihood of retrieving structurally relevant evidence, but does not guarantee recovery of all required clauses. Even with structural conditioning, omission-sensitive obligations may still be missed when supporting evidence is incomplete or weakly represented in the corpus.

Scope of evaluation.

Our experiments focus on Data Sharing Agreement synthesis across healthcare, finance, and education domains using three open-weight LLM families. While these domains exhibit diverse structural and regulatory characteristics, the findings may not directly generalise to other long-form generation tasks, languages, model scales, or retrieval settings without further evaluation.

Potential evaluation limitations.

Clause-level compliance evaluation relies in part on LLM-based auditing pipelines and benchmark-derived compliance signals. Although the same deterministic evaluation protocol is applied consistently across methods, automatic auditing may still miss subtle legal or domain-specific nuances. In addition, aggregate similarity metrics do not always correlate with structural completeness or omission robustness.

Computational overhead and retrieval scaling.

Ontology matching, KG retrieval, and constraint-aligned query construction introduce additional latency relative to standard RAG pipelines. Retrieval-budget sensitivity experiments further show that increasing retrieval depth does not necessarily improve downstream performance, particularly for standard RAG. Scalability to larger corpora and real-time deployment settings requires further study.

Generality of refinement methods.

Our RLHF experiments suggest that post-generation refinement mainly improves stylistic coherence and local fluency rather than reliably recovering missing evidence-dependent clauses. More advanced refinement or agentic verification mechanisms may therefore be necessary for robust omission-sensitive generation.

Overall, our findings suggest that retrieval-stage structural alignment is a promising direction for improving robustness in long-form generation settings where omission failures and incomplete evidence can lead to unreliable outputs. Future work includes extending retrieval-stage structural conditioning to broader settings, including compliance, policy generation, and agentic document generation.

Ethical Considerations

Constraint-aligned retrieval may improve the reliability of long-form compliance and policy generation systems by reducing omission-sensitive failures. However, generated agreements may still contain incomplete, outdated, or legally insufficient content if supporting evidence is missing or retrieval fails. Such systems should therefore be used as assistive drafting tools rather than substitutes for expert legal or regulatory review. In addition, policy-grounded generation systems could be misused to automate large-scale production of synthetic policies or compliance documents without appropriate human oversight.

A.2.2 
KG Relation Expansion

Policy-grounded KG expansion retrieves relevant RDF triples from the domain KG. Each triple

(18)

Triples above a similarity threshold are retained; if no triple satisfies the threshold, the highest-ranked triples are returned to avoid empty constraint contexts. This allows the retriever to recover policy obligations that may not be surfaced by semantic similarity alone.

A.7.1 
Reproducibility

All variants share the same retriever, base LLM, prompt scaffold, chunking strategy, decoding configuration, and evaluation pipeline. Retrieval uses identical corpora, embedding models, and top-
 settings across methods. Variants differ only in the source and type of structured constraints injected before retrieval and generation.

To minimise implementation variance, all experiments use deterministic prompting, fixed generation parameters, and identical clause-auditing protocols across domains and runs. Ontology matching and KG construction are largely automated from policy corpora and domain resources, reducing manual engineering requirements.

A.10 
Bootstrap Significance Testing

We performed paired bootstrap significance testing to evaluate whether improvements in document-level reliability were statistically significant across retrieval variants. Testing was conducted across domains, runs, and model variants using paired document-level reliability scores. Each bootstrap sample preserved document-level pairing between compared methods to isolate relative reliability differences under identical evaluation instances. For each comparison, we computed the mean paired difference and estimated 
 confidence intervals using 5,000 bootstrap resamples.

Confidence intervals excluding zero were treated as statistically significant. Table 
12
 shows that 
CA-RAG
 significantly outperformed standard 
RAG
, 
Self-RAG
, and 
Flare-RAG
. In contrast, differences relative to the generation-only constraint baseline were not statistically significant, suggesting that generation-time structural prompting can partially improve aggregate reliability. However, the main paper shows that retrieval-stage structural conditioning more consistently improves omission robustness and clause-level completeness.

A.11 
Ablation: Impact of RLHF Refinement

We analyse RLHF as a post-generation refinement stage while keeping retrieval and evidence support fixed. Table 
13
 reports reliability scores after RLHF refinement on 
Llama-3.1-8B
. The effect of RLHF varies across domains and retrieval settings. In some cases, refinement improves stylistic coherence and local fluency, while in others it slightly reduces reliability, particularly in finance, where compliance-sensitive phrasing is more fragile.

Importantly, RLHF does not alter the relative behaviour of retrieval variants, nor does it consistently recover omitted obligations when supporting evidence is incomplete. Even when readability improves, omission-sensitive clauses such as transfer safeguards, legal-basis requirements, or compliance dependencies remain constrained by the quality and structural completeness of retrieved evidence. These results suggest that RLHF primarily acts as a lightweight stylistic refinement mechanism rather than a substitute for retrieval-stage structural alignment.

Abstract

Existing approaches to training data attribution derive influence estimates from different sources of information, including gradients, model-internal states, and surface-level similarity.
However, comparisons between attribution methods remain incomplete, often relying on indirect evaluations through downstream task performance.
In this work, we introduce a framework for directly comparing methods along three dimensions: 
functional alignment
, which measures correlation between full attribution vectors; 
representational alignment
, which captures structural similarity of the representations underlying attribution; and 
cross-method predictability
, which evaluates agreement on local pairwise ranking decisions.
We observe strong representational and moderate functional alignment within paradigms. Alignment across paradigms is low and task-dependent, yet stable across models.
Our results suggest that the similar downstream performance observed across paradigms in prior work can be attributed to agreement on the relative importance of documents, rather than to agreement on rankings of the full training data.

In this work, we investigate 
how well methods from different training data attribution paradigms align in their assessments of data influence
.
Prior work has largely neglected comparisons across paradigms and, when conducted, has compared solely in terms of downstream task performance 
(
Jiao et al., 2025b

 or by the rank correlation between importance rankings over training instances 
(
Pezeshkpour et al., 2021

.
We argue that this generates an incomplete view of alignment and propose a novel evaluation framework that addresses three main limitations:
First, existing comparisons between attribution methods 
Pezeshkpour et al. (2021)
; 
Brophy et al. (2024)
; 
Nguyen et al. (2023)
 depend heavily on evaluation task mechanics, limiting their transferability. For example, methods that work well for question answering may fail to capture aspects relevant to instruction tuning because of differences in learning objectives.
Second, these works only consider
global measures of correlation between attributions (e.g., Spearman’s 
 between rankings induced by two attribution score vectors 
), which does not adequately reflect functional equivalence in many downstream applications such as data selection, where agreement on relative relevance judgments is sufficient (i.e., whether a given example is more influential than another).
Third, existing studies do not separate representational structure from downstream ranking decisions, making it difficult to determine whether differences between methods stem from how information is represented or from how it is processed.
We accordingly 
decompose alignment into three dimensions
: 
functional alignment
, i.e., correlation between two full attribution vectors; 
representational alignment
, i.e., structural similarity of the underlying document representations used for attribution; and 
cross-method predictability
, i.e., agreement in pairwise relevance predictions rather than correlation between two rankings of the entire training dataset.
These measures provide complementary perspectives on alignment:
Functional alignment and cross-method predictability may serve to establish that two methods produce sufficiently similar importance rankings over training instances, while measures of representational alignment can generate insights into structural similarities of the document representations used for attribution.

We leverage this framework to conduct a 
systematic study of alignment
 of 12 attribution methods across four paradigms, 5 tasks, and 7 LoRA-finetuned models spanning three model families.

Specifically, 
we provide insights into
:
(i) the alignment of attribution methods across different language modeling tasks, (ii) the stability of alignment across datasets and base models, (iii) whether methods can be employed as proxies for others, and (iv) factors that impact alignment at the instance level.
We find that methods within the same attribution paradigm tend to exhibit high representational but moderate functional alignment. Alignment across paradigms is overall low and highly task-dependent, but consistent across model families and sizes.
Our findings suggest that the relatively consistent downstream performance across paradigms in prior work may be driven by agreement on the relative importance of individual examples, rather than by agreement on example relevance across the full training data.
In a qualitative analysis, we find that cross-paradigm alignment appears to be shaped by a mix of prompting artifacts, lexical overlap, and task-dependent structural and behavioral similarities.
Overall, our results suggest that methods within a paradigm are partially interchangeable, but that different paradigms capture distinct, only partially aligned notions of data influence, suggesting that only a combination of different sources of information may yield a complete characterization of data influence.
Our main contributions are:

(1)

A framework for measuring representational and functional alignment; and a test of cross-method predictability that assesses local rather than overall agreement on example influence.

(2)

A systematic study of alignment across 7 base models, 5 tasks, and 12 attribution methods.

(3)

A qualitative analysis of factors of alignment.

Alignment depends on instruction tuning goals.

Prompting artifacts drive misalignment.

Recurring instruction specifications and persona-prompting templates introduce repeated structure across all datasets, increasing lexical overlap.
BM25 appears to be frequently driven by these artifacts.
In contrast, representation-based methods appear to exploit more meaningful task-related similarities between instances (from an instruction-tuning perspective).

Discussion

Task dependency of alignment and limitations of lexical matching.

The divergence across paradigms appears to stem from differences in how each method defines similarity. Lexical methods (e.g., BM25) primarily capture surface form and instruction overlap, and therefore diverge in their attributions from other strategies when structure is shared, but wording differs. In contrast, gradient- and embedding-based methods likely better capture structural and behavioral similarity, grouping instances by shared instruction and completion patterns rather than keyword overlap. Given that learning behavior rather than memorization is the central goal of instruction tuning tasks, this suggests that relying on lexical overlap alone may be a weak basis for attribution in instruction tuning.

Measuring functional alignment is insufficient.

Our results show that the three proposed evaluation protocols capture distinct and only partially overlapping aspects of alignment. We further show that methods may exhibit low correlation in attributions across the full dataset yet strong pairwise agreement on example relevance, as well as high representational alignment.
This implies that low average rank correlation does not necessarily preclude one method from serving as a useful substitute for another in a given downstream task.
Accordingly, relying solely on rank-correlation analyses may lead to the unwarranted disqualification of prospective methods, whereas our framework can provide more nuanced insights into alignment.

Additionally, our results may also partially explain the apparent mismatch between prior findings, where some report low cross-paradigm correlation, while others independently observe similar downstream-task performance of methods belonging to different paradigms. To assess whether this hypothesis holds, future work should design evaluation protocols spanning downstream tasks that vary in their reliance on local versus global agreement, and test whether cross-method predictability scores predict performance on individual tasks.

Mechanistic interpretation of alignment.

Despite negligible functional alignment, we measure high representational alignment
and cross-method predictability between gradient-similarity methods and the
Hessian-based 
. Decomposing alignment into the three suggested
dimensions can provide a mechanistic interpretation of this apparent discrepancy:
High representational alignment suggests that
the reweighting in 
 (Equation 
8
) does not
fundamentally alter the representation structure. While it systematically changes score assignment, it retains information about pairwise relevance, as indicated by the high cross-method predictability.
Similarly, although representation-based methods may be expected to align closely with one another, our representational alignment results and qualitative analysis show that alignment is contingent on capturing the same task-specific features.

Alignment deteriorates cross-paradigm.

While most pairings achieve above-chance cross-method predictability, accuracy is only consistently above 0.75 for within-paradigm comparisons, whereas the only cross-paradigm pairings scoring above 0.65 are those between lexical- and embedding-based methods.
Representational and functional alignment across paradigms is likewise weak.
Our findings suggest that practitioners seeking to use one method as a proxy for another (e.g., to pre-filter examples before applying more computationally expensive but also more accurate methods) should select methods of the same attribution paradigm.
At the same time, the observed lack of alignment suggests an opportunity for future work on 
example-based explanations
 built on data attribution: combining orthogonal strategies may yield more comprehensive characterizations of data influence.

6 
Conclusion

We introduce a framework for directly measuring alignment between training data attribution methods, which captures correlation of attribution rankings (
functional alignment
), assesses structural similarity of the underlying representation spaces used for attribution (
representational alignment
), and evaluates agreement on pairwise ranking decisions (
cross-method predictability
). We show that this decomposition is useful for disentangling different notions of alignment that are not captured by existing evaluations based on measures of downstream performance.
Comparing 12 methods spanning four paradigms, we find high alignment within individual attribution paradigms (gradient similarity, model-internal representations, embedding similarity, and lexical overlap). In contrast, cross-paradigm alignment is consistently lower and task-dependent. This suggests that methods within a paradigm are partially interchangeable for downstream applications, whereas those across paradigms are not reliable proxies for one another.
Qualitatively, we find that this divergence stems from a fundamental difference in how paradigms define similarity: lexical methods capture surface-level prompting artifacts, whereas representation-based ones appear to capture behavioral patterns.

Overall, our results show that representational alignment, functional alignment, and cross-method predictability do not necessarily covary, emphasizing the need to distinguish between local and overall agreement in evaluation.
To this end, we recommend augmenting work on new attribution methods through systematic alignment analysis to contextualize new approaches within known paradigms.

Limitations

Our experiments consider text generation tasks. Alignment between specific paradigms may differ for other task formats, such as simpler classification setups. However, our framework is fully compatible with other task definitions and models outside NLP contexts.

In contrast to prior work, we intentionally forgo evaluating downstream performance in favor of directly testing alignment, which is required to establish a more fundamental understanding. Our focus was on developing complementary perspectives on alignment, rather than studying failure modes specific to individual tasks or replicating prior downstream evaluations.

Note that our analysis primarily provides recommendations on evaluation practices; our study is not designed to produce quality or performance characterizations of individual attribution methods that could directly inform the design of new attribution methods.

We restrict our analysis to base models with a maximum of 8B parameters due to the computational costs involved with obtaining and analyzing gradient-based influence estimates. We ensure robustness by validating our findings across seven models spanning parameter sizes from 0.6B to 8B and three model families.
Likewise, in line with prior work 
(
Kwon et al., 2023
; 
Xia et al., 2024
, e.g.,)
, we only consider LoRA-fine-tuned models in our analysis due to the high computational costs of gradient-based attribution. Alignment patterns may vary when attribution targets other (subsets) of model parameters.

While future work may explore alternative training data attribution paradigms, we have compiled a set of 12 methods across four paradigms, which represent a cross-section of the current literature.

Ethical Considerations

Given that we document patterns of alignment across attribution paradigms, there is a risk that actors acting in bad faith could misuse this information to select an attribution strategy that aligns with their desired claims of data influence, despite prior negative results obtained with other methods. Our work mitigates this risk by demonstrating that single-paradigm attribution may provide an incomplete view of data influence.

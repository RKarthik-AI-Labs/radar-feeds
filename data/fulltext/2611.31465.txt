Abstract.
 Retrieving images of patients clinically similar to a given patient can support diagnosis, teaching and research. Training and evaluating retrieval systems requires knowing which images of different patients show the same finding, and existing datasets are small or narrow in scope. We construct PMC-PaIR, a dataset of 86,144 pairs of clinically similar images from 56,013 open-access PubMed Central case reports, which often document rare or challenging presentations. We develop an approach that follows citations, which often link case reports of similar patients. To determine what two linked cases share clinically, it prompts large language models to generate and answer hierarchical questions, from modality to diagnosis and findings, and to match images by caption using the answers. Experts find the pairs highly accurate, and this question-guided approach finds twice as many correct pairs as direct matching. Fine-tuning BiomedCLIP on PMC-PaIR improves retrieval in all modality, anatomy and diagnosis categories tested, for image-only queries and image queries with a short text naming the finding. We release the dataset and the automated pipeline, which can extend PMC-PaIR as new case reports are published.

Background & Summary

Clinicians facing an unfamiliar presentation, educators assembling teaching material and researchers studying a disease often need images of other patients with clinically similar findings. Content-based medical image retrieval addresses this need by ranking images by their similarity to a query image,
1
 and retrieval from the literature can also improve the diagnostic predictions of vision–language models.
2
 Developing and evaluating such systems requires data that specify which images of different patients are clinically similar.

Published case reports could potentially be a valuable source of such images. A case report describes an individual patient in detail, including imaging, and is often published because the presentation is rare, atypical or diagnostically challenging.
3
 Comparable cases are therefore scarce in any single institution’s archive but can be found across the literature. The PubMed Central (PMC) Open Access Subset makes many such reports and their figures available for reuse.
4

However, existing datasets of PMC figures relate each figure only to text from its own article, whether they draw on articles of any type, such as ROCOv2, MedICaT and PMC-15M,
5
, 
6
, 
7
 or on case reports, such as MultiCaRe.
8
, 
9
 Medical image retrieval benchmarks either cover many modalities and body regions with few queries or are restricted to a single modality or body region. ImageCLEF
10
 and the image-retrieval task of M3Retrieve
11
 have a few dozen to several hundred queries, whereas 3D-MIR covers computed tomography (CT) volumes of four organs
12
 and RadIR covers chest radiographs and chest CT scans.
13
 None of these resources provides clinically similar image pairs at scale across modalities, body regions and diseases.

We present PMC-PaIR (PubMed Central Patient Image Retrieval), a dataset of 86,144 pairs of clinically similar images from 56,013 open-access case reports (Fig. 1). Two radiological, nuclear-medicine or ophthalmic images from different reports are clinically similar when they share an imaging modality, an anatomical region and a type of abnormality, such as an abscess or an aneurysm. We build on PMC-Patients, which links the patients of two case reports whenever one cites the other.
24
, 
25
 Because authors often cite earlier reports of similar patients, we form image pairs between linked reports. PMC-Patients does not, however, examine why the citation was made or link the figures of the two reports. A citation alone does not indicate which figures correspond, because a report’s figures may show the primary disease, a complication, a coexisting condition or the result of treatment, and authors cite earlier reports for many reasons. Expert annotation of image pairs is costly,
26
 and labels extracted automatically from radiology reports
27
 can differ from what individual images show.
28
 For each pair of linked reports, or case pair, large language models (LLMs) therefore identify the reason for the citation from its citation contexts, compare the two cases through structured questions that progress from imaging modality and organ system to diagnosis and specific findings, and then match figures by caption. We call this procedure citation-guided hierarchical image-pair annotation (CHIPA). On randomly selected case pairs, CHIPA finds about twice as many correct pairs as direct matching from full articles, and no incorrect ones. As the models read only text, the pairs are independent of any image model.

The dataset covers case reports published until February 2026, and we release the automated curation pipeline, which can be rerun as PMC grows. Each pair is annotated with the modality, anatomy and diagnosis its two images share, and we derived image-level labels in 12 modality, 20 anatomy and 40 diagnosis categories. We assigned all 86,893 images to training, validation and test partitions such that no pair or source article spans two partitions, preventing leakage between them. The release also includes citation contexts and generated citation-intent analyses, questions and answers for 121,831 case pairs.

Two experts reviewed a sample of pairs and judged the large majority to match and none to differ in diagnosis, and the image-level labels were also highly accurate. Fine-tuning the vision–language model BiomedCLIP
7
 on the pairs improves retrieval in every modality, anatomy and diagnosis category tested, both for queries consisting of an image alone (Fig. 2) and, more strongly, for queries that add a short text naming the finding of interest, as when a clinician searches for a specific abnormality. PMC-PaIR also supports representation learning, figure classification and, through the citation records and questions and answers, the study of citation intent and the training of models that compare clinical cases.

Methods

Throughout, a 
case
 is a patient record in PMC-Patients, and its 
source article
 is the publication that reports it. Two cases form a 
case pair
 when one source article cites the other; each case pair is counted once, regardless of citation direction. An 
image
 is one published figure, which may contain several panels. An 
image pair
 consists of one image from each source article of a case pair. The image pairs included in PMC-PaIR are 
positive pairs
, and the 
positives
 of an image are the images that form a positive pair with it.

We selected citation-linked case pairs and their figures, annotated image pairs with CHIPA (Fig. 1), derived image-level labels and partitioned the images for benchmarking (Fig. 3). Table 1 gives the counts after each processing step and the model used at each stage of CHIPA.

Selection of citation-linked case pairs

We drew cases from two versions of PMC-Patients, the published collection of 167,034 cases from 140,897 articles published until January 2022
24
, 
25
 and a collection of 319,939 cases from 268,151 articles published until February 2026, which we regenerated with the published construction code
24
 from the PMC Open Access Subset and PubMed files downloaded in March 2026. We excluded records of the regenerated collection that lacked citation information. Because figures from an article that reports several patients cannot be reliably attributed to one of them, we retained only articles associated with exactly one case in their collection.

Candidate case pairs were the citation-based links that PMC-Patients records between the retained cases.
24
 From the regenerated collection, we added only pairs containing at least one case absent from the published collection. Excluding cases without a candidate pair left 138,103 distinct cases in 159,388 candidate case pairs (Table 1).

Figures, case descriptions and citation evidence

For each selected case, we obtained the full-text Extensible Markup Language (XML) and figure files of the source article from the PMC Open Access Subset
4
 and linked each figure and its caption to the case. To remove diagrams and text-heavy figures, we discarded images with predominantly white backgrounds and, among the remaining images, those in which Tesseract optical character recognition (through pytesseract) detected extensive text, using thresholds given in the code of the curation pipeline (Code Availability). We took the case description from the article sections with case-report headings such as ‘Case presentation’ or ‘Case report’.

We verified each citation link by searching the reference list of each article for an entry identifying the other article, and a matching entry in either article confirmed the link. We matched entries on the digital object identifier (DOI), PubMed identifier (PMID) or PubMed Central identifier (PMCID), in that order, accepting a match on any one identifier and using normalized title substring matching as a fallback. As citation evidence, we collected the titles and abstracts of both articles and the citation contexts, that is, the paragraphs and table rows whose cross-reference (
<xref>
) elements point to the matched entry. For author–year citations that are not linked to the bibliography in the article XML, the link was confirmed from the reference list, but no citation context could be extracted.

We submitted a case pair for annotation only if the full-text XML and figure files of both articles were available, each case had at least one retained figure with a valid caption and a case description, and the citation link was verified. Of the 159,388 candidate case pairs, 122,365 met these criteria (Table 1).

Image-pair annotation with CHIPA

CHIPA annotates each case pair with four successive LLM calls (Fig. 1): citation-intent analysis, question generation, question answering and image-pair proposal. Dividing the task gives each call a narrower objective and allows each stage to use a different model and reasoning-effort setting (Table 1). The prompts are reproduced at the end of Methods, and Table 2 shows the captions given to stage 4 and the outputs of stages 2–4 for one case pair.

Citation-intent analysis.
 The model receives the titles and abstracts of both articles together with any extracted citation contexts. As in citation-intent classification,
29
 it states the reason for the citation, together with the possible similarities between the two cases and a short explanation of how the citation is used. The purpose of a citation affects how informative the link is for comparing figures. For example, a report of an ectopic parathyroid adenoma in the piriform sinus
30
 cites an earlier report of an adenoma at the same site,
31
 whereas a report of an ectopic pituitary tumor
32
 cites a report of metastatic testicular cancer
33
 because both describe the use of ChatGPT in writing case reports. PMC-Patients, in contrast, treats every citation between two case reports as a link between similar patients, without examining its context.
24

Question generation and answering.
 Given both case descriptions and the citation-intent analysis, the model generates about three comparative questions at each of three levels: imaging modality and organ system (level 1), diagnostic category and disease process (level 2), and specific imaging and clinical findings (level 3). A second call answers each question with Yes, No, Partial or Unclear and a brief reason, using only the case descriptions and the citation-intent analysis. The questions and answers provide the final stage with clinical context that captions may omit. The questions and answers for all 121,831 case pairs in the release are provided in 
case_pair_questions_answers_eligible.jsonl
 (Data Records).

Image-pair proposal.
 The final call receives the questions and answers together with the captions of both articles, each keyed by its image identifier, and considers every combination of one caption from each article. It returns, in JavaScript Object Notation (JSON), the combinations whose captions describe the same imaging modality and the same type of abnormality within the same anatomical region or organ system, together with free-text descriptors of the shared modality, anatomy and diagnosis. Differences in view, imaging plane, sequence, laterality, acuity, severity, slice or anatomical level do not by themselves prevent a match. The prompt restricts pairing to radiological, nuclear-medicine and ophthalmic imaging and excludes clinical and operative photographs, histopathology, endoscopy, electrophysiological recordings and diagrams. A case pair can yield zero, one or several image pairs.

Post-processing and exclusion of veterinary reports

Parsing the saved model responses, with formatting errors repaired where possible, yielded 93,113 proposed image pairs (Table 1). We then removed proposals with invalid image identifiers or unavailable image files, proposals involving source articles flagged as veterinary case reports (described below) and duplicate proposals (treating pairs as unordered), leaving 86,144 image pairs. Of the 121,831 case pairs that remained after the veterinary-report filter, 48,331 (39.7%) contributed at least one positive pair.

To exclude veterinary case reports, we flagged likely dog and cat reports with keyword and phrase rules applied to article titles and abstracts, using journal titles as additional context. An article was flagged if its title matched an animal-related pattern, if its abstract matched such a pattern together with veterinary or case-report context, or if its abstract matched at least two distinct animal-related patterns. Exceptions covered expressions used in human case reports, such as ‘dog bite’, ‘CAT scan’ and the dental term ‘canine’. We applied the filter after annotation, removing 534 case pairs together with their image pairs.

Image-level labels

We derived image-level labels by mapping the free-text descriptors of the positive pairs to controlled vocabularies of 12 modality, 20 anatomy and 40 diagnosis categories, each of which includes the catch-all category 
other
. The 12 modality categories are CT, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), radiography (including dental radiography and mammography), ultrasound, angiography/fluoroscopy, nuclear medicine, optical imaging, photography, microscopy, endoscopy, electrophysiology and 
other
. Optical imaging, assigned to 1,354 images, comprises optical coherence tomography and other ophthalmic or optical techniques such as fluorescein and indocyanine-green angiography, fundus autofluorescence, slit-lamp imaging and reflectance confocal microscopy, whereas fundus photographs are assigned to photography. Although the prompt restricts pairing to radiological, nuclear-medicine and ophthalmic imaging, some pairs share another modality, for example when a compound figure that combines CT with endoscopy is paired with an endoscopy image. Microscopy, endoscopy and electrophysiology labels are assigned to 1,449, 219 and 263 images, respectively, and photography labels to 837. The vocabularies and mapping rules are provided in 
label_taxonomy.json
, and Table 2 shows an example. Regular-expression rules recognize spelling variants, synonyms and abbreviations. For example, ‘hepatic abscess’ and ‘liver abscess’ both map to 
infection_or_abscess
. Broad diagnostic categories group related terms: ‘tumor’, ‘mass’ and ‘unspecified lesion’ all map to 
mass_or_lesion
, which does not distinguish benign from malignant findings.

The rules first split diagnosis descriptors into clauses at commas, semicolons and the connectives ‘but’, ‘however’, ‘and’, ‘with’, ‘plus’, ‘complicated by’, ‘associated with’ and ‘due to’. Within a clause, rules for specific conditions take precedence over generic lesion terms, so that ‘abscess lesion’ maps to 
infection_or_abscess
. The rules also recognize explicit negation and resolution, mapping, for example, ‘no abscess’ to 
normal_or_negative_report
 and ‘resolved abscess’ to 
postoperative_or_resolved_state
. A descriptor that matches no rule maps to 
other
.

We assigned the categories derived from a pair to both of its images. For each image and field, we took the union of the categories contributed by all pairs that contain the image. An image can therefore carry several labels per field, and labels contributed by different pairs may refer to different panels of a compound figure. We assigned 
other
 only when no pair contributed a more specific category to that field, which occurred for 65 images (0.07%) in modality, 277 (0.32%) in anatomy and 2,250 (2.59%) in diagnosis.

The 
nuclear_medicine
 label is the only label also assigned directly from an image’s own caption, independently of its pairs. Nuclear-medicine studies such as positron emission tomography (PET) are often fused with, or displayed beside, CT or MRI, whereas a pair descriptor records only the modality that the two images share. A fused PET/CT image paired with a CT image is therefore described as ‘CT’. We applied the 
nuclear_medicine
 rule of the taxonomy to every caption and assigned the label to each matching image, counting the abbreviation PET only when capitalized. Of the 3,068 images labeled 
nuclear_medicine
, 873 received the label only from their captions.

Benchmark partitions

We partitioned the images so that every positive pair and every source article lies entirely within one partition (Fig. 3). We represented the dataset as an undirected graph whose nodes are images and whose edges are positive pairs. Each connected component of this graph contains the images linked directly or indirectly through positive pairs. Because the images of one source article can fall into different components, we merged components that share a source article until all images of each article belonged to the same merged component, which we call an 
article group
. Merging did not add pairs to the dataset and produced 13,338 article groups.

We sorted the article groups by their smallest image identifier, compared as strings, and shuffled them with the 
shuffle
 method of Python’s 
random.Random
 generator initialized with seed 42. The first 10,670 groups (80%, rounded down) formed the training partition, the next 1,333 (10%, rounded down) the validation partition and the remaining 1,335 the test partition. These proportions apply to article groups rather than to images. The training, validation and test partitions contain 70,552, 8,245 and 8,096 images and 70,262, 8,034 and 7,848 positive pairs, respectively, and the test images come from 5,255 source articles.

Ethics statement

PMC-PaIR was derived entirely from case reports published in the PMC Open Access Subset and from the PMC-Patients collection extracted from them. No patients were recruited or contacted, no data other than the published articles were used, and no attempt was made to identify individuals.

Data Records

PMC-PaIR is available from Zenodo.
34
 Table 3 lists the files. JSON Lines (JSONL) files contain one JSON object per line.

Image and case identifiers.
 Image identifiers link the annotation, label, partition, image and caption files. Each identifier joins, with underscores, the numeric part of the article’s PMCID, the case index and the extraction index of the figure. For example, 
9899453_1_1
 is the first extracted figure of case 1 in article PMC9899453. Because only single-case articles were retained, the case index is 1 in every identifier. Each identifier refers to a complete figure, which may contain several panels. Case identifiers, which appear in the case-pair questions and answers, join the numeric part of the PMCID and the case index with a hyphen, as in case 
9899453-1
, to which image 
9899453_1_1
 belongs.

Image-pair annotations.
 The file 
image_pairs.json
 is a JSON array with one object per positive pair. Each object has four fields: 
pair_id
, a two-element array with the identifiers of the two images, one from each source article; and the free-text descriptors 
modality
, 
anatomy
 and 
diagnosis
, which describe the shared imaging modality, anatomical region and type of abnormality. A descriptor may name several related structures or findings (Table 2). Each unordered pair occurs once.

Image-level labels.
 The file 
image_labels.jsonl
 contains one object per image with two fields, 
image_id
 and 
labels
, the second of which has the keys 
modality
, 
anatomy
 and 
diagnosis
, each holding an array of category names. The file 
label_taxonomy.json
 defines the categories and the mapping rules, including text normalization and matching priorities. Labels derived from pair descriptors can be traced to the records in 
image_pairs.json
 whose 
pair_id
 contains the image identifier.

Case-pair citation records.
 The file 
case_pair_citations_eligible.json
 is a JSON array of 121,831 records, one for each annotated case pair that passed the veterinary-report filter. Each record describes the citation from one source article to the other with four string fields: 
citing_case
 and 
cited_case
, the PMCIDs (for example, PMC9899453) of the citing and cited articles, whose numeric parts are the first components of the image identifiers; 
extracted_citation
, the citation contexts; and 
citation_reason
, the output of the citation-intent analysis stage. In the 857 records for which no citation context could be extracted, 
extracted_citation
 is an empty string.

Case-pair questions and answers.
 The file 
case_pair_questions_answers_eligible.jsonl
 contains 121,831 records, one for each case pair in the citation records. Each record has eight string fields: 
annotation_id
, a unique record identifier; 
case_pair_id
, an order-independent identifier that joins the two case identifiers, sorted as strings, with ‘|’, for example 
5563556-1|8167975-1
; 
case_a_uid
 and 
case_b_uid
, the identifiers of the two cases in the order used in the prompts (case A and case B), which does not indicate citation direction; 
citing_case
 and 
cited_case
, the PMCIDs of the citing and cited articles, for example PMC8167975 and PMC5563556; and 
questions
 and 
answers
, the generated text as saved from the model output. Records can be joined with the citation records through 
citing_case
 and 
cited_case
.

Images and captions.
 The archives 
images_captions_001.zip
 to 
images_captions_004.zip
 contain 86,825 images, stored as 
images/<image_id>.jpg
, and their source captions, stored as 
captions/<image_id>.txt
. Files for the remaining 68 images are withheld because the redistribution terms of their article or figure could not be confirmed (44 images), because their licenses prohibit derivatives (23) or because they await privacy review (1). Of these images, 60 are in the training partition, 3 in the validation partition and 5 in the test partition, and 
withheld.csv
 lists their identifiers, the reasons and their locations in the source articles. All 86,893 identifiers are retained in the annotation and label files, and 
pair_availability.csv
 indicates for every positive pair whether the files of both images are distributed. The file 
file_manifest.csv
 gives the archive, size and SHA-256 checksum of every image and caption file.

Benchmark partitions.
 The partition of every image is distributed with the code, as 
data/article_disjoint_split.json
 in the retrieval repository and, as an identical copy, as 
image_splits.json
 in the dataset-curation repository (Code Availability).

Licensing and provenance.
 The file 
licenses.csv

Images, captions and the citation contexts in the case-pair records remain under the terms of their source articles. Our annotations, labels, generated citation-intent analyses, questions and answers, and other original content are released under the Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International license (CC BY 4.0) to the extent of the authors’ rights, as stated in

Dataset composition.
 The 86,144 positive pairs involve 86,893 images from 56,013 source articles. The articles were published between 1986 and 2026, 60.6% of them in 2020 or later, with a median year of 2021 and an interquartile range (IQR) of 2017–2023 (Table 4). CT, MRI and radiography are the most frequent modality labels (46.4%, 23.1% and 15.5% of images), and the most frequent anatomy and diagnosis labels are thorax/respiratory (14.8%) and mass/lesion (31.4%), respectively. An image belongs to a median of one positive pair (IQR 1–2; maximum 33), and 45.9% of images belong to two or more. The graph of positive pairs has 18,597 connected components with a median of three images (IQR 2–4; maximum 963), and the 13,338 article groups contain a median of three images (IQR 2–6; maximum 1,465).

Citation-intent analyses

To check that the citation-intent analyses are faithful to their inputs, we sampled 25 case pairs at random and compared each analysis with the titles, abstracts and citation contexts from which it was generated. An analysis was rated 
supported
 when it correctly described the role of the cited article, 
partly supported
 when it identified relevant content but overstated or imprecisely described that role, and 
not supported
 when the source text did not establish the claimed reason. Twenty-two analyses were supported, two were partly supported and one was not supported (Table 5).

CHIPA versus single-step annotation

We compared CHIPA with a single-step baseline that received the full-text XML of both articles and proposed image pairs directly, without citation-intent analysis or questions and answers, using the model and reasoning-effort setting of the image-pair proposal stage (
gpt-5.4-mini
, low). Both workflows were applied to 50 randomly selected case pairs, and every image pair proposed by either was reviewed manually. Of the 29 distinct pairs proposed, 27 were judged correct. CHIPA proposed 25 of these 27 pairs and no incorrect pair, whereas the baseline proposed 12 correct and 2 incorrect pairs (Table 5). Using 
o4-mini
 (low reasoning effort) in place of 
gpt-5.4-mini
 gave similar results. The comparison focused on proposed pairs because PMC-PaIR lists positive pairs only. A false positive adds an incorrect pair to the dataset, whereas a missed pair leaves a true pair unlisted.

Expert review of image pairs

A medical physicist and a resident physician independently reviewed 163 randomly sampled pairs and resolved disagreements by discussion. For each pair, the reviewers examined both images, the titles and abstracts of the two source articles and the citation context, and rated the match in modality, anatomy and diagnosis as Yes, Borderline or No. The overall rating was No if any attribute was rated No and otherwise equaled the diagnosis rating.

By consensus, 147 pairs (90.2%), including 13 with a borderline modality or anatomy rating, matched overall, 6 (3.7%) were borderline and 10 (6.1%) did not match (Fig. 4a and Table 5). The 10 mismatches concerned modality (4 pairs) or anatomy (6 pairs), and diagnosis, which matched for 156 pairs (95.7%), was never rated a mismatch. Before discussion, the reviewers’ overall ratings agreed for 151 pairs (92.6%; 95% CI 87.5–96.1%), with an unweighted Cohen’s 
 of 0.70, which indicates substantial agreement.

Image retrieval with the benchmark partitions

Benchmark design.
 To test whether the positive pairs can be used to train and evaluate medical image retrieval models, we fine-tune a vision–language model on the training partition and evaluate it on the test partition. We use BiomedCLIP, which was pretrained on the 15 million PMC figure–caption pairs of PMC-15M.
7
 Each of the 8,096 test images serves once as a query. Its candidates are all other test images except those linked to it only indirectly, through other positive pairs in its connected component, which we exclude because they may be clinically related to the query. To cover wide and multi-panel figures, we resize each image so that its shorter side is 224 pixels, take three 
 crops from the start, middle and end of its longer side, and represent the image by the normalized mean of the three crop embeddings.

We evaluate two retrieval tasks (Fig. 1). In image-only retrieval (i2i), candidates are ranked by the cosine similarity between the query and candidate image embeddings. In image-plus-text retrieval, abbreviated (i+t)2i, the query image is accompanied by the descriptions of its positive pairs, which are sentences generated from the pair descriptors with the template ‘{diagnosis} in the {anatomy} on {modality}.’ Candidates are ranked by the unweighted sum of the image similarity and the mean similarity between the candidate image and the query’s distinct descriptions. This task evaluates retrieval when a description of the finding of interest, here derived from the pair annotations, is available at query time. We compare each fine-tuned model with pretrained BiomedCLIP queried in the same way.

Evaluation.
 Hit@
 is the proportion of queries with at least one positive among the 
 highest-ranked candidates, Recall@
 is the mean fraction of each query’s positives found among them, and the mean reciprocal rank (MRR) is the mean of the reciprocal rank of the first positive,
36
 with each query weighted equally. We estimate 95% CIs for the differences between fine-tuned and pretrained models with a cluster bootstrap
37
 that resamples whole test article groups 5,000 times, keeping all queries of each drawn group together and evaluating both models on the same resampled queries. Because relevance is defined by the positive pairs, the metrics measure how well a model retrieves the annotated positives rather than all clinically similar images in the test partition.
38

Fine-tuned models.
 In both tasks, the eligible negatives of a training image are the images not linked to it, directly or indirectly, through positive pairs, and we select the checkpoint with the highest validation Recall@10, computed with the same candidates, image crops and ranking as at test time. For i2i, we fine-tune all 12 transformer blocks of the image encoder with layer-wise learning-rate decay and an InfoNCE contrastive loss,
39
, 
40

41

42

Figure 2 shows an example query.

Radiologist review of retrieved images

A board-certified radiologist with 9 years of experience (K.K.B.) reviewed the five highest-ranked candidates returned by the fine-tuned (i+t)2i model for 17 test queries (85 query–candidate pairs). The queries were sampled at random from those with CT and heart or vasculature labels, to match the radiologist’s subspecialty. The radiologist rated whether each candidate matched the query in modality, anatomy and diagnosis, regardless of whether it was one of the query’s positives. At least one of the five candidates matched the query in modality and in anatomy for all 17 queries, in diagnosis for 12 (70.6%) and in all three attributes for 11 (64.7%; Table 5). These ratings count any clinically matching candidate and therefore complement the metrics based on the positive pairs.

Usage Notes

Benchmarking.
 Because the pairs were derived from text, without reference to any image model, they can be used to compare image models. To obtain results comparable with those reported here, users should adopt the released partitions and, as in Technical Validation, exclude from each query’s candidates the images linked to it only indirectly. The reported results use all 8,096 test images, including 5 whose files are withheld, and can be recomputed from the saved per-query results (Code Availability). With the distributed files, the retrieval code excludes the withheld images and their pairs and evaluates the remaining 8,088 test queries, and the resulting metrics differ slightly from those reported. Models pretrained on PMC figures and captions, including BiomedCLIP,
7
 may have encountered PMC-PaIR images during pretraining.

Positive pairs.
 PMC-PaIR contains positive pairs only; image pairs that are not listed are unlabeled rather than negative.
43
 When training with contrastive objectives, images linked only indirectly through positive pairs can be excluded from the negatives, as in our fine-tuning experiments, or false negatives can be handled explicitly.
44
 By design, the two images of a pair share an imaging modality, which excludes cross-modality matches such as a CT and an MRI image of the same kind of lesion.

Labels and descriptors.
 Shared image-level labels indicate a coarser similarity than positive pairs: two images labeled CT, thorax/respiratory and mass/lesion can show unrelated abnormalities, whereas the two images of a positive pair were matched on the type of abnormality in the context of both cases. Because labels describe whole figures, which may contain several panels, users who need panel-level labels can combine PMC-PaIR with compound-figure separation.
6
 Users who need finer or different categories can remap the free-text descriptors with their own rules or adapt those in 
label_taxonomy.json
, and label errors that arise in this mapping can be corrected in the same way, without changing the pair annotations. To restrict the data to radiological images, users can filter images and their pairs by modality label.

Images and licensing.
 Images are figures as published in the source articles and are distributed in JPEG format. Many contain arrows, letters or other marks added by the authors, which users training image models may want to crop or mask. The images retain the licenses of their source articles, and 
licenses.csv
 allows users to select images by license and to attribute them.

Linking to the source articles.
 The PMCID numbers in the image identifiers and the PMCIDs in the citation records link every image to its source article in PMC, from which users can retrieve the full case description and the article’s other figures, for example to build case-level or combined text-and-image tasks.

Questions and answers.
 For each case pair, the released questions and answers record the clinical comparison that guided image matching, at the levels of imaging modality and organ system, diagnosis and specific findings. They can explain why two images were paired, and their answers at each level allow users to select case pairs that agree at a chosen level, for example to build stricter or graded relevance judgments. They can also serve as training and evaluation data for models that compare two clinical cases or generate comparative clinical questions, and as intermediate supervision for distilling the annotation procedure into smaller open models.

Extending the dataset.
 The released curation pipeline can be rerun on later releases of the PMC Open Access Subset to annotate newly published case reports, given application programming interface (API) access to language models such as those in Table 1. Unlike the procedure described in Methods, it applies the veterinary-report filter before annotation.

Abstract

Attribution graphs over the features of sparse autoencoders (SAEs) are used to predict what an ablation does and are compared across training seeds. A TopK SAE keeps the largest preactivations at each token, and a feature’s margin is its preactivation’s distance to the cutoff. On five public TopK SAE pairs from three Pythia models, zeroing one feature moves a next layer feature across the cutoff in 0.74 of cases or more, and the crossings carry most of the response’s energy. Over writes that scale a feature’s code by three factors, the graph built on the features active beforehand has a median relative error of 0.247 or more. The margin graph adds the first order change to every preactivation and reranks, at the same cost, and errs by at most 0.033. At the median, the crossing features’ decoded change is 1.24 times the model’s own change in norm or more. Between seeds of one testbed, sparse maps between features explain 0.85 of a graph’s energy on its active features, and 0.47 once the other seed’s graph keeps only its own. The largest model studied is pythia-160m.

1 
Introduction

Researchers who interpret a language model through sparse autoencoders (SAEs) build attribution graphs over the features of trained SAEs, which this paper calls dictionaries, and ask two questions of such a graph. What does ablating one feature do to the features at the next layer, and how much of the graph survives a change of training seed? A TopK SAE keeps, at each token, the features whose preactivations are positive and among the 
 largest 
(
Gao et al., 2025
)
. An attribution graph over two such dictionaries at adjacent layers carries the derivative of each downstream feature’s preactivation with respect to each upstream feature’s code, taken across the block between them 
(
Ameisen et al., 2025
; 
Marks et al., 2025
; 
Farnik et al., 2025
)
. Ablating one feature often moves a downstream feature across its cutoff, so the graph used to predict that ablation was built on a set of active features the ablation changes. On the public dictionaries of pythia-70m at layers 3 and 4, zeroing one feature moves a downstream feature across the cutoff in 0.84 of cases. At that ablation the fixed support graph, which keeps only the features active before the ablation, predicts the downstream code’s change with a median relative error of 0.739, and a graph that keeps each feature’s signed distance to the cutoff errs by 0.121.

The field’s graphs are built on the features the clean input makes active. 
Ameisen et al. (2025)
 write that “The nodes in the attribution graph represent active features”, and 
Dunefsky et al. (2024, p. 17)
 derive the attribution between transcoder features “assuming that this feature is active”. In the graph of 
Marks et al. (2025, p. 4)
 the “nodes correspond to feature activations or SAE errors”, and each edge is linearized at the clean activations 
(
Marks et al., 2025
, p. 19)
. Attribution patching makes “a first-order Taylor expansion” at the clean activation 
(
Kramár et al., 2024
, p. 4)
, and its corrections recompute the attention softmax or average the gradient along a path 
(
Kramár et al., 2024
; 
Hanna et al., 2024
)
. The first order expansion of a TopK code at the clean input keeps only the active features, since its Jacobian there projects onto them. 
Ameisen et al. (2025)
 name the features that expansion leaves out, the inactive ones “that are only ‘one ablation away’ from being active”.

Across seeds, the comparisons reported for dictionaries score one feature at a time, by a cosine rule on encoder and decoder or by how often a feature reappears over many runs 
(
Paulo and Belrose, 2026
; 
Gerasimov et al., 2026
)
. A score of that kind compares each feature’s direction and never the operator between two layers, so it cannot say how much of a graph’s operator survives a change of seed.

A feature’s margin is signed and positive when the feature is active. Its size is the distance its preactivation can travel before the membership changes. This paper makes four contributions. First, it measures how often an intervention that scales one feature’s code moves a downstream feature across the cutoff, against the scaling factor, on five public TopK dictionary pairs and a testbed, and compares a dictionary under a rectified linear unit (ReLU) rule with them as a continuous case (Section 
3
). Second, it defines the margin graph, proves it exact where the block is affine and measures it against the fixed support graph and two devices from the literature that correct first order attribution (Section 
4
). A Lipschitz constant along a write bounds each preactivation’s first order error. A predicted membership whose margin clears the band those bounds set keeps its sign. The constant is estimated from Jacobians at three points along each write, so the band is an audit after the write, and a calibration locates the write norm at which the error reaches a level fixed in advance. Third, it compares the code’s response with the model’s own change and bounds from below the response a swap forces (Section 
5
). On the one layer triple studied, a chain that carries a decoded middle code through the next block fails at the third layer, and the residual stream carries the write there. Fourth, on one testbed it carries a graph’s operator across seeds through sparse nonnegative maps and places the masked graph’s loss on the target’s inactive atoms, which the signs of their margins select (Section 
6
).

8 
Limitations

No JumpReLU dictionary was studied. The one public release with adjacent residual layers is built on a model behind an access agreement this study lacks. No transcoder was studied. The largest model studied is pythia-160m. The transport is measured on one cell, between seeds and on its retraining pair, because no public release carries a second seed. Integrated gradients were evaluated on cell F alone. No runtime or memory was measured, and the cost model counts Jacobian-vector products.

9 
Conclusion

A zero ablation moves the downstream support in most cases on every TopK pair studied, and the margin graph predicts the code and that change. A reader who builds a graph keeps every downstream row and reranks at no added product. A reader who ablates a feature learns from the margin graph which features enter and leave, and audits each predicted membership after the write by the band inside the regime its norm sets. On 4 sampled features per position at three doses that audit accepted no wrong membership at 0.59 to 0.87 of a single feature write’s memberships. On the one triple studied every decoded middle code failed at the third layer while one product through the residual stream carried it. A reader who compares two seeds compares the operator, or the source’s coactive block with the target’s mask widened by its inactive atoms nearest the cutoff, since a masked graph carries each token’s sign pattern into its edges. Which nodes of a circuit across several blocks does a residual stream chain predict at each layer’s code, and do decoded middle codes fail on other triples?

Learning Whom to Trust About the
Unseen

Source Credibility and Reliability
Transfer in Language Models

Ruijia Guan

Affiliation: 
Symbolic Systems Program, Stanford University

Abstract

A source who has been right before deserves attention, but about what?
We ask how far a demonstrated record of reliability travels in language
models when the new claim cannot be checked, and whether the answer
depends on the kind of claim. Three behavioral studies across six models
approach the question from three angles. Study 1 adapts the
Einstein-effect paradigm and finds that models, unlike the human sample,
favor a spiritual leader over a scientist for the same obscure
statement, and that the preference largely reverses when the statement
is reworded in the vocabulary of physics. Study 2 adapts a paradigm in
which a trusted expert calls a scientific or religious explanation
unknown or a mystery; models reproduce the human domain asymmetry in
exaggerated form. Study 3, the central test, gives two fictional
informants different records on checkable river forecasts and then has
them endorse matched claims about hidden natural and spiritual causes of
the same village events. Most models carry the record to natural causes
and barely at all to spiritual ones; one model transfers it weakly to
either. Telling the model that the reliable informant forecasts by
spiritual guidance rather than by measurement lowers that informant’s
influence on natural claims in every model and produces no comparable
gain on spiritual claims in any. Demonstrated reliability thus has a
scope in these models, and a source’s account of how they came to be
reliable can narrow it. Whether this reflects a judgment about relevance
or the low prior credence of spiritual claims is the question the design
leaves open, and we outline the experiment that would settle it.

 

 

Preprint

1 
Introduction

Someone who has predicted a river’s level correctly for twenty weeks has
earned a hearing when they explain a flood. It is less clear what their
record establishes about a crop disease, and less clear still what it
establishes about an angry spirit in the burial ground. Evaluating
testimony involves two judgments, not one: how reliable has this source
been, and what does that reliability tell me about the claim in front of
me now? The second judgment matters most when the claim cannot be
checked against anything else.

This paper asks how language models make that second judgment. Models
now sit in the position people have always been in with respect to the
unseen: asked to weigh what a source says about things neither party can
verify, and expected to say who knows what, what kind of answer is
adequate, and when a source’s past success is relevant.

Developmental psychology offers a well-developed toolkit for these
questions. Children’s beliefs about unseen entities—germs as much as
angels—rest largely on testimony, and children calibrate their
confidence by the kind of entity and the kind of talk surrounding it

(
Harris et al., 2006
)
. By age four, children track informants’ records and
prefer the previously accurate one for new information, including graded
differences in accuracy 
(
Koenig et al., 2004
; 
Pasquini et al., 2007
)
. They also care
how the accuracy came about: an informant who was right on their own is
trusted more than one who was right with help 
(
Einav and Robinson, 2011
)
.
Expertise is expected to have boundaries 
(
Landrum and Mills, 2015
)
, relevant
expertise is weighed against the claim’s intuitiveness 
(
Lane and Harris, 2015
)
,
and a demonstrated competence in one domain does not automatically
generalize to another 
(
Koenig and Jaswal, 2011
)
. Adults, meanwhile, give the
same obscure statement more credit when it is attributed to a scientist
than to a spiritual guru, across 24 countries 
(
Hoogeveen et al., 2022
)
, and
treat an expert’s admission of ignorance differently in science and
religion 
(
Davoodi and Lombrozo, 2022
)
.

We bring three of these paradigms to language models. Study 1 measures
source-role effects and their dependence on what the source is quoted as
saying. Study 2 measures what an expert’s admission of ignorance does to
confidence in scientific versus religious beliefs. Both adapt published
human studies with direct human comparisons on the original conditions.
Study 3, the central study, moves from given to earned credibility. Two
initially neutral informants in a fictional village acquire different
records on verifiable river forecasts. We then measure how much each
informant’s endorsement moves the model’s credence in natural and
spiritual explanations of the same village events—claims the task
gives no way to check—and how that transfer changes when the informant
explains how she forecasts.

Three things could produce a model’s greater acceptance of one speaker’s
claim: a prior association with the speaker’s role, accurate tracking of
a supplied record, and a judgment about whether that record is relevant
to the claim at hand. Studies 1 and 2 characterize the first. Study 3
holds the record fixed, confirms that models track it, and isolates the
third. Source preferences in models and trust that updates on
interaction history are both documented 
(
Khan et al., 2026
; 
Zhou et al., 2026
)
, and
claim-dependent credibility has been named as an open problem

(
Ashkinaze et al., 2026
)
. The matched natural–spiritual contrast lets us
study that problem directly, without assuming that equal transfer across
domains is the right answer.

2 
Related work

2.1 
Testimony, unseen entities, and the scope of
expertise

Children learn about much of the world from what others tell them, and
they are selective about whom to believe. Preschoolers who watch two
informants label familiar objects go on to prefer the accurate one for
novel labels 
(
Koenig et al., 2004
)
, and four-year-olds track relative
rather than absolute accuracy 
(
Pasquini et al., 2007
)
. Selectivity extends
beyond the record itself: children distinguish informants who were
knowledgeably right from those who were merely right 
(
Einav and Robinson, 2011
)
,
expect specialists’ knowledge to have limits 
(
Landrum and Mills, 2015
)
, and
weigh an informant’s expertise against how plausible the claim is on its
own 
(
Lane and Harris, 2015
)
. Koenig and Jaswal find that expertise does not
generalize freely across domains while incompetence does

(
Koenig and Jaswal, 2011
)
. Harris and colleagues show that children’s
confidence in unseen entities tracks the testimony they receive and
differs by kind of entity 
(
Harris et al., 2006
)
.

Two adult paradigms supply our human reference points. The Einstein
effect is the credibility a source’s identity confers on a statement

(
Hoogeveen et al., 2022
)
. Davoodi and Lombrozo show that the same
expression of expert ignorance has different consequences for scientific
and religious beliefs 
(
Davoodi and Lombrozo, 2022
)
. Together, this literature
treats a source’s success at one task and a source’s knowledge of
another as separable questions. Study 3 is built on that separation.

2.2 
Source preferences and history-sensitive trust in language
models

Khan and colleagues document systematic preferences among attributed
sources in language models, including sensitivity to framing

(
Khan et al., 2026
)
. Study 1 takes the existence of source-label effects as
given and asks how their direction depends on content. Epistemic Context
Learning shows that models can condition trust on peers’ interaction
histories and that this can be trained with reinforcement learning

(
Zhou et al., 2026
)
. We likewise supply a record in context; what we vary
is which claims the record is applied to, and how the source’s stated
basis of competence changes that.

2.3 
Information discernment and claim-dependent
credibility

Learn2Discern is the closest benchmark 
(
Ashkinaze et al., 2026
)
. It
attributes perturbed answers to real news websites whose reliability is
scored externally, and separates poor reliability estimation from
under-use of a correct estimate. Its discussion flags claim-dependent
credibility as a limit of source-level ratings. Our question begins
there: given an observed success record, what does it establish about a
particular new claim? Study 3 supplies identical records to fictional
informants, holds each target claim fixed across source conditions, and
compares explanations of the same outcome that differ only in whether
the cause is natural or spiritual. Because the target causes have no
truth value within the task, a smaller spiritual contrast is not scored
as a failure of discernment; it is a measurement of scope.

2.4 
Contribution

The contribution is a controlled test of how far demonstrated
reliability travels across natural and spiritual explanations, with a
manipulation of the source’s stated competence that can distinguish an
increase in spiritual influence from a decrease in natural influence,
situated beside source-role and uncertainty judgments that have human
reference data. The design does not by itself separate a relevance
judgment from the effect of low prior credence in spiritual claims;
Section 
7
 says what would.

Shared methods

3.1 
Models and response
formats

We evaluate Claude Haiku 4.5, Claude Sonnet 4.5, GPT-5,
DeepSeek-V3-0324, Qwen3.5-27B, and Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct (identifiers

claude-haiku-4-5
,

claude-sonnet-4-5
,

gpt-5
,

deepseek/deepseek-chat-v3-0324
,

qwen/qwen3.5-27b
,

meta-llama/llama-3.3-70b-instruct
).
Anthropic and OpenAI models were called through their vendor APIs; the
other three through OpenRouter with reasoning disabled. Qwen runs were
pinned to a single backend (Novita, fallback routing off) after an
initial run that mixed backends; the unpinned runs are excluded.
Sampling temperature was 1 for Anthropic and OpenRouter requests. GPT-5
calls requested minimal reasoning effort (low-effort fallback) and
omitted the temperature argument. In Study 3, models were allowed brief
reasoning before a final answer.

Studies 1 and 2 use two response formats. In the participant-conditioned
format, a human participant’s demographic and attitude responses are
rendered as sentences in the system prompt and the model answers from
that perspective; this links each model trial to an original human
trial. In the direct format, the model answers as itself. Participant
profiles are conditioning information, not independent samples of model
individuals. Study 3’s main condition is direct; an exploratory frame
adds a village-resident perspective and an animist ontology.

3.2 
Records, caching, and analysis
units

All analyses use saved request-level outputs. Repeated runs are
deduplicated by participant and trial in Studies 1–2 and by model,
prompt hash, and sample index in Study 3, keeping the latest valid
response. Study 3 targets two responses per unique core prompt and
includes the sample index in its cache key. In Studies 1–2, identical
contexts can share cached outputs, especially in the direct format, so a
count of participant-linked rows describes the trial mapping rather than
a count of independent stochastic samples.

We report model-specific contrasts and cell means. Study 2 mixed models
include a participant random intercept for the two domain observations
per participant. Study 3 mixed models include a matched-pair random
intercept after averaging repeated responses within a condition.
Standard errors are conditional on the stimulus set. For Study 3 we add
pair-level contrasts and leave-one-pair-out checks.

3.3 
Coverage

Study 1 and Study 2 Part A cover all six models. Study 2 Part B covers
Haiku and GPT-5. Study 3’s core conditions cover all six models; its
same-task diagnostics cover five (no Sonnet run), and its exploratory
animist frame covers Haiku and GPT-5. The pair-level sensitivity
analyses in Study 3 are labeled exploratory.

4 
Study 1: Who sounds
credible?

4.1 
Method

We adapted the Einstein-effect paradigm 
(
Hoogeveen et al., 2022
)
, in which
the same obscure, pseudo-profound statements are attributed to a
scientist or a spiritual guru. The original study collected judgments
from 10,195 participants in 24 countries. We sampled 301 participant
profiles, stratified by country and religiosity tercile, each linked to
its two original trials with counterbalanced statement–source mappings.
Models rated credibility on the original seven-point scale, one rating
per call.

In an extension, we replaced the two original statements with two
author-written statements of similar structure that use physics
vocabulary. Both content sets were run in both formats. The quantity of
interest is mean credibility when attributed to the scientist minus mean
credibility when attributed to the spiritual leader. The human sample
provides a comparison for the original content only.

4.2 
Results

The sampled humans favor the scientist by 0.64 points. Every model, in
both formats, favors the spiritual leader on the original statements
(Table

). The direct-format preference is large in the two
open-weight models (Qwen −4.60, Llama −3.92). Rewording the statements
with physics vocabulary moves every contrast toward the scientist: ten
of twelve model-by-format cells then favor the scientist, Sonnet-direct
is tied, and Llama-direct still leans toward the spiritual leader.

5 
Study 2: What counts as an adequate
answer?

5.1 
Method

We adapted Studies 2 and 3 of Davoodi and Lombrozo 
(
Davoodi and Lombrozo, 2022
)
.
Part A uses 1,437 participant-by-domain observations from 1,004
participants after the original inclusion filters; Part B uses 1,562
observations from 974 participants. The same rows define the model
prompts.

In Part A, a participant reports confidence in a scientific or religious
belief, states whether experts know its mechanism, and then projects
their confidence after a trusted expert who shares the belief describes
the mechanism as “unknown” or “a mystery.” Ratings are 1–7 and the
outcome is projected minus initial confidence. In the main
implementation, a participant’s two domain blocks appear in one
conversation in a seeded random order; an independent-session version
was run for Haiku and Sonnet, and a no-expert control that re-elicits
confidence without testimony was run for Haiku, Sonnet, and GPT-5.

Part B collects eleven agreement ratings (−3 to +3) about the expert’s
answer. Three form an epistemic-evaluation composite (truth, knowledge,
curiosity); eight form a composite about the threat to, value of, and
questioning of the belief. We used the first of five fixed beliefs that
each participant endorsed without knowing its mechanism, following the
original survey logic. Part B was run on Haiku and GPT-5 in both
formats.

Part A is analyzed as confidence change predicted by domain (science =
1), wording (mystery = 1), and their interaction, with a participant
random intercept; a negative interaction means that “mystery” hurts
more than “unknown” in science relative to religion. Part B uses the
same predictors for each composite. Unparsed outcomes are excluded;
Appendix 
A
 reports
retained counts.

5.2 
Results

Projected confidence falls more for scientific than religious beliefs in
every model and format. In the participant format, religious-belief
changes range from −0.63 to +0.09 across wording conditions and
scientific-belief changes from −0.61 to −1.58; the matched human cells
are about −0.61 for religion under both wordings and −1.00 to −1.13 for
science.

The domain-by-wording interaction is −0.10 (SE 0.14) in the human data.
Participant-conditioned model estimates range from −0.40 to −0.94, and
direct-format estimates from −0.12 to −0.42 (Table 
2
). Every
model exaggerates the human asymmetry when it answers as a described
participant, and every model’s estimate shrinks when it answers as
itself.

6 
Study 3: How far does reliability
travel?

6.1 
Design and measures

Harrow is a fictional village in a river valley. Two informants, Oren
and Teo, forecast binary river-level outcomes, and a twenty-week log
shows each forecast beside the recorded outcome: one informant is right
on 18 weeks, the other on 10. The generator balances outcomes and
predictions, counterbalances which name holds the stronger record, and
uses two history orders. The log stays in context for every target
prompt, so the task measures use of supplied evidence, not learning
across calls.

The core targets are eight matched pairs of causal claims. Each pair
starts from the same village event and offers either a natural cause or
a spiritual agent as its explanation: a recurring fever is attributed to
conditions in the goat pens, or to spirits of the burial ground. Every
spiritual cause names its agent. Each claim is presented, in separate
prompts, as endorsed by the stronger-record informant, by the
weaker-record informant, or by nobody. No feedback about the hidden
cause is ever given.

A second factor is the informant’s own account of how they forecast. In
the measurement condition the informant reads a marked post in the
water; in the spiritual condition they receive word from a river spirit;
a no-cue condition says nothing. A natural-intuition condition appears
in extension runs. The sentence can be attached to either informant.
Because it is the informant’s own claim, the spiritual cue is a
statement about method, not independent evidence that the method works.

Models rate how likely each claim is to be true on a 0–100 scale. The
source contrast for a target is

where

no-endorsement condition gives a baseline and lets each source’s shift
be read separately. Conditions without a record test the names and
competence cues alone; because the generator collapses identical
no-history prompts, those conditions are summarized by name rather than
by strong/weak label.

Two diagnostics check that the record is used at all. Same-task prompts
ask about a new river forecast endorsed by one informant; further
prompts ask how many forecasts each informant got right and which
informant the model would consult. These runs exist for five models (no
Sonnet run).

The direct-frame core comprises 1,200 unique prompts with two sample
indices per model: 2,400 valid outputs for each model except Qwen
(2,397). The exploratory animist frame covers Haiku and GPT-5 at one
response per prompt. It casts the model as a lifelong resident and
stipulates that several places have spirits and that the dead linger
near the village for a time, so it changes ontology and perspective
together.

6.2 
Analysis and a design
deviation

The screening rule required no-endorsement baselines between 20 and 80
in Haiku and GPT-5 for both members of a pair. The spiritual members
fell below that range. We kept the pairs, because dropping them would
have removed the content contrast the study exists to test. The
consequence is that natural and spiritual claims differ in initial
credence as well as in content, which bears on interpretation
throughout. A manipulation check on Haiku confirmed that the items were
read as natural versus spiritual and as unrelated to the river, but
rated the competence sentences as unclear.

6.3 
Results

6.3.1 
Models use the record

All five models with diagnostic runs give a same-task river forecast
more credence when the stronger informant endorses it, by 15.5 points
(Llama) to 41.5 (Qwen), and choose the stronger informant in 96–100%
of deference trials. Recall of the exact record is imperfect: the
stronger informant’s mean recalled count ranges from 14.25 (GPT-5) to
18.00 (Qwen) against a true count of 18. The record is tracked well
enough to drive a nearby judgment.

6.3.2

ones

Llama is the exception: a 15.5-point same-task contrast, but 3.3 points

where the new question resembles the old one.

6.3.4 
Framing and response-scale
checks

In the animist frame without a competence cue, natural and spiritual
contrasts are 25.0 and 4.6 for Haiku and 25.9 and 9.2 for GPT-5. The
spiritual contrasts are larger than in the direct frame, and the
natural–spiritual gap remains. With one response per prompt and
perspective and ontology changed together, this frame is suggestive
only. On a logit scale (probabilities clipped to [0.02, 0.98]), the
natural contrast still exceeds the spiritual contrast in all six models,
so the gap is not an artifact of the linear scale alone, though a
transform cannot undo the floor effects in the raw ratings.

7 
General discussion

Across the three studies, what a model does with testimony depends
jointly on the source and on the kind of claim. In Study 1 a source-role
effect shifts, and in most cells reverses, with the wording of the
statement. In Study 2 the cost of an expert’s admitted ignorance depends
on domain, and more sharply than in people. In Study 3 the same record
of success buys a source influence over natural explanations and almost
none over spiritual ones, and the source’s own account of their
competence can shrink even the former. The transfer result is the most
direct evidence that demonstrated reliability has a scope in these
models.

7.1 
Reliability has a scope

Llama makes the point most cleanly. Its same-task contrast shows that it
tracks and uses the record when the new question looks like the old
ones; its near-zero hidden-cause contrasts show how little further the
record reaches. The other five models extend most of the contrast to
natural hidden causes and little to spiritual ones. Model comparisons
that ask only whether a record is tracked, or whether it drives the next
choice, miss this dimension: how far the evidence is allowed to travel.

Two accounts fit the data. On one, models judge relevance: a forecasting
record speaks to observable natural processes and says little about
spiritual agents, so the same record is weighed differently. On the
other, spiritual claims start so low on the scale that no source can
move them much. The retained pairs leave these confounded, since
spiritual baselines sit near the floor in every model. The developmental
literature suggests the first is at least part of the story—children
restrict expertise to its domain and discount informants whose accuracy
had the wrong provenance 
(
Koenig and Jaswal, 2011
; 
Einav and Robinson, 2011
)
—but the present
data cannot apportion the two. A smaller spiritual contrast should not
be read as a failure to apply a correctly estimated reliability score.
It may be the model’s answer to the question of what the score is
evidence for.

7.2 
A source’s account of competence can narrow
it

The competence manipulation shows why an interaction must be decomposed.
Every model lowers its natural-claim contrast when the reliable
informant credits a spirit rather than a gauge, and no model raises its
spiritual-claim contrast by more than a point. The positive interactions
in Table 
4
 are therefore a story about lost influence on
natural claims, not gained influence on spiritual ones. Reported as a
single coefficient, the same numbers would have suggested the opposite.

The cue is the informant’s own explanation. Models may read it as
evidence about the informant’s causal understanding, as a claim awaiting
corroboration, or as a marker of a worldview that discounts them on
other matters, all while accepting that the recorded forecasts were
accurate. That a stated basis of competence changes trust independently
of the record parallels the developmental finding that children prefer
an informant who was right on their own to one who was right with help

(
Einav and Robinson, 2011
)
, and the asymmetry Koenig and Jaswal describe between
generalizing expertise and generalizing a negative evaluation

(
Koenig and Jaswal, 2011
)
. The parallel motivates the next experiment rather
than settling the mechanism here.

7.3 
Human comparisons and cultural
scope

Studies 1 and 2 give the model findings a human reference. On Study 1’s
original statements, models reverse the human preference; in Study 2,
models exaggerate the human domain asymmetry when prompted as a
participant and shrink it when answering as themselves. Conditioning on
a participant description thus changes not only the level but the size
of an effect, which is relevant to any evaluation that uses persona
prompts as stand-ins for populations.

The materials have a definite cultural scope. Study 1’s profiles come
from a multi-country sample; Davoodi and Lombrozo’s participants were
largely Christian Americans; Study 3’s spiritual claims are a small set
of agentic causes in one invented village. “Religion” and
“spirituality” name far more than these items represent, and the
relevant categories, and what counts as evidence for them, vary by
place. The results describe model judgments under controlled prompts,
not how users read or act on model answers.

7.4 
Limitations

Stimulus scope is narrow: two statements per content set in Study 1,
eight matched pairs with two history orders in Study 3. Natural and
spiritual claims differ in baseline credence as well as content.
Diagnostic coverage is uneven: Sonnet has no same-task run, Part B and
the animist frame cover two models, and the Study 2 controls cover
three. Exact recall of the record varies. Caching limits the
independence of repeated participant-linked responses; API names do not
pin immutable snapshots; and open-weight models were served through a
router, where backend and precision matter and were logged but not
manipulated. Numerical credence is one behavioral readout of acceptance;
its relation to what a model would say, recommend, or go on to do is
unmeasured.

7.5 
Next experiments

The decisive design varies how the record bears on each target domain
while matching baseline credence. Within a stipulated world, one
informant can earn a record that directly establishes access to natural
causes and another a record that establishes access to spiritual ones,
with identical target claims tested under each. If transfer follows the
relevance of the record rather than the domain of the claim, the
relevance account gains direct support; if spiritual claims stay inert
even when a record establishes access to them, and a manipulation check
shows that access was understood, the floor account becomes the better
explanation. Either outcome narrows the field without closing it, since
relevance and prior credence may both contribute. Separating resident
perspective from stipulated ontology, and eliciting verbal as well as
numeric responses, would distinguish framing from response-scale
effects. Once the behavioral contrasts are reproduced on an exact
open-weight checkpoint, parametric interventions on a model’s spiritual
baseline can test whether raising it changes transfer.

8 
Conclusion

In six language models, a source who has demonstrably been right is
believed more about some things than others. Five models extend a
forecaster’s earned credibility to hidden natural causes and withhold it
from spiritual ones; Llama transfers little of it beyond the task that
produced it. When the reliable forecaster says that a spirit tells them
what the river will do, every model trusts that forecaster less about
natural causes and none trusts them meaningfully more about spiritual
ones. Together with the content-dependent source effects of Study 1 and
the exaggerated domain asymmetry of Study 2, these results show that
models bring domain-structured expectations to testimony about what they
cannot check. Deciding what a source’s record is evidence for is a
judgment these models make, and one that can now be measured and
compared.

Abstract

Educational recommenders are usually compared offline, by how well they predict the next activity in logs of tutoring systems, learning applications and MOOCs, where the next activity is largely decided by the platform: teachers assign problem sets, tutors draw problems until mastery, engines select questions. On eight public datasets from five platforms, we compare four structural predictors, which use nothing about the learner beyond the last activity, with ten personalised recommenders and with open-weight LLM rankers from 0.5 to 7 billion parameters, under two protocols. The structural predictors reach between 0.81 and 1.00 of the best accuracy of any method (median 0.95), mostly through population transitions rather than the course order; the best personalised recommender is significantly better on five datasets, by 0.007 to 0.102 NDCG@10, and on Eedi in three of four training runs. Every method is far less accurate on steps along transitions never seen in training and on steps chosen by learners. LLM rankers look competent with random negatives but are never much better than a word-matching baseline, and with negatives that are plausible under the course structure they stay close to random or well behind the transition heuristic, whatever their size. In a simulation with known learner needs, the structural share tracks the share of steps decided by the platform, and offline accuracy ranks an oracle of the learners’ needs below imitators of the platform. Weighting test steps by how unlikely the structure makes them restores much of the link with usefulness and, on the real data, reveals a significant personalisation signal on seven of the eight datasets. We propose a syllabus-aware evaluation checklist and release a Python package that reports the lift of any recommender over the structure.

Keywords:
 educational recommender systems; offline evaluation; sequential recommendation; exposure bias; curriculum sequencing; large language models; knowledge tracing; reproducibility

Introduction

Recommender systems for learning propose the next exercise, problem set, video or quiz to a learner, and they are increasingly built with the same machinery as recommenders in other domains: collaborative filtering, sequential neural models and, most recently, large language models 
(
Drachsler et al., 2015
; 
Cheng et al., 2026
; 
Ma et al., 2025
)
. Most of them are developed and compared offline, on logs collected by tutoring systems, learning applications and MOOCs: a model sees what a learner has done so far and is rewarded when it ranks high the activity that the learner did next 
(
Erdt et al., 2015
)
.

In educational logs, however, the next activity is seldom a free choice of the learner. A teacher assigns problem sets in a chosen order; a tutor draws the next problem from the current set until a mastery criterion is met; an application’s engine selects the next question; a course has a syllabus. The logged sequence is the output of a 
sequencing policy
 that the platform, the teacher and the curriculum mostly control (Figure 
2
). A model can therefore score well offline by reproducing that policy, without learning anything about what a particular learner needs. This is a shortcut in the sense of 
Geirhos et al. (2020)
: a decision rule that succeeds on the benchmark but not on the intended task. Recent work found such shortcuts in general sequential-recommendation benchmarks, where a training-free transition heuristic matches modern generative recommenders 
(
Han et al., 2026
)
. In educational data the shortcut is not an accident of dataset construction; it is the design of the platform. Early MOOC studies already noted that following the syllabus predicts a sizeable share of next resources 
(
Tang et al., 2016
)
, but no study has measured how much of the reported accuracy of educational recommenders the structure explains, on which steps personalisation adds anything, and what this implies for LLM-based recommenders, which are evaluated on small candidate sets where the choice of negatives is decisive 
(
Krichene and Rendle, 2020
; 
Hou et al., 2024
)
.

This paper asks a simple question: when a recommender or an LLM scores well on such logs, has it learned the learner or the platform? Put differently, 
can recommenders and LLMs beat the syllabus?
, where by the syllabus we mean, broadly, the sequencing that the course and the platform impose: the course order, the grouping of activities into problem sets, parts or topics, and the typical paths that assignments produce. We audit offline next-activity evaluation on eight public datasets from five platforms (ASSISTments, the Santa TOEIC application of EdNet, Junyi Academy, Eedi and a MOOC), covering teacher-assigned problem sets, an adaptive engine, a self-paced knowledge map, teacher-assigned quizzes and a course. We compare four structural predictors, which read nothing about the learner beyond the last activity, with ten personalised recommenders (collaborative filtering, session-based, peer-based, knowledge tracing and sequential) and with open-weight LLM rankers, under two evaluation protocols and two kinds of candidate negatives. We summarise each benchmark by the accuracy available from the structure alone, 
, the lift of each recommender over it, 
, and the structural share 
 (Section 
3
).

Our findings are the following.

•

The structure explains most of the measured accuracy
 (Figure 
1
). The structural share ranges from 0.81 to 1.00 (median 0.95). The best personalised recommender is significantly better than the best structural predictor on five of the eight datasets, by 0.007 to 0.102 NDCG@10, and not on the other three (on Eedi, it is in three of four training runs).

•

The shortcut is the paths, not the syllabus as written.
 The best structural predictor is almost always the population transition heuristic. Without it, the course order, containers and popularity reach a median of only 0.45 of the best accuracy.

•

Novel, off-path and learner-chosen steps are where the task is hard.
 On steps along transitions never seen in training, no method reaches an NDCG@10 above 0.162 on the six datasets with enough such steps; on four of the six datasets with containers, the best structural and personalised methods are far less accurate on steps that leave the current container; and on Junyi they predict exercises chosen by learners less than half as well as exercises suggested by the platform. Personalised methods do not close these gaps, and pedagogical policies based on knowledge tracing rank below popularity even with a deep model of the learner.

•

LLM rankers look competent only where the task is easy.
 On candidate sets with random negatives, an open LLM can look strong, but a baseline that only matches words with the last activity’s name does as well. With negatives that the course structure finds plausible, every method loses accuracy: the LLMs fall close to the random level on two datasets and stay well behind the transition heuristic on the other two. Going from 0.5 to 7 billion parameters changes none of this.

•

Offline accuracy can rank the right recommender last, and a structure-corrected view helps.
 In a simulation with known needs, the structural share tracks the share of steps the platform decides, and once it decides most steps, NDCG@10 ranks an oracle of the learners’ needs below the imitators of the platform. Weighting test steps by the inverse of their structural probability restores much of the link with usefulness; on the real data, it shows that personalised recommenders do beat the structure on the steps the structure does not explain, on seven of the eight datasets.

The contributions are (i) a framing of offline next-activity evaluation as imitation of a sequencing policy, with three summary quantities that separate structure from personalisation; (ii) a cross-platform audit of fourteen recommenders and open-weight LLMs on eight datasets, with protocols that avoid the usual pitfalls (exact handling of ties, new-activity targets, global temporal splits); (iii) a simulation with known needs that validates these quantities, and a structure-corrected accuracy (Section 
6
); (iv) a syllabus-aware evaluation checklist (Section 
7.1
); and (v) an installable Python package that reports the lift of any recommender over the structure, with all the code and per-case results of this study 
(
Jdidou, 2026
)
.

Takeaways for anyone reporting offline results of an educational recommender

1.

Score popularity, the course order, container continuation and the transition heuristic on exactly the same cases. A score means little until it is reported as a lift over the best of them.

2.

Break results down by kind of step: seen and novel transitions, steps inside and outside the current container, and, if the log records it, steps started by the platform or by the learner. Personalisation matters on the hard ones.

3.

Do not evaluate LLM rankers on random negatives alone: add structurally plausible negatives and a word-matching baseline.

4.

Repeat the evaluation with a global temporal split.

2 
Related work

Baselines and pitfalls in offline evaluation.

A series of reproducibility studies has shown that progress claimed for new recommendation algorithms often disappears against well-tuned simple baselines. 
Ferrari Dacrema et al. (2019)
; 
Ferrari Dacrema et al. (2021)
 could reproduce only a minority of recent neural approaches, and most of those were outperformed by nearest-neighbour or linear methods; 
Rendle et al. (2020)
 found that a carefully tuned dot product beat a learned similarity; and 
Ludewig et al. (2021)
 reported that nearest-neighbour heuristics were in most cases preferable to neural session-based recommenders. Other work has examined the evaluation procedure itself: sampled metrics can rank methods inconsistently with full rankings 
(
Krichene and Rendle, 2020
)
, splitting data without respecting the global timeline leaks future information 
(
Ji et al., 2023
)
, and many offline studies use flawed datasets, splits or metrics 
(
Hidasi and Czapp, 2023
)
. Recorded interactions are themselves shaped by the system that produced them: recommendations act as treatments whose exposure biases the logs 
(
Schnabel et al., 2016
)
, feedback loops make the data more homogeneous 
(
Chaney et al., 2018
)
, and offline protocols built on such logs need to be interpreted with care 
(
Jeunen, 2019
)
. Our study applies this line of scrutiny to a domain in which the exposure is not incidental but designed.

Shortcut-solvable benchmarks.

Shortcut learning describes models that succeed on a benchmark through decision rules that do not reflect the intended task and that fail to transfer 
(
Geirhos et al., 2020
)
. In recommendation, 
Han et al. (2026)
 recently showed that a training-free transition-graph heuristic, using only the last one or two items, matches or beats modern generative recommenders on many standard sequential benchmarks, and traced this to low-branching transitions, feature-smooth transitions and weak dependence on long histories. Educational logs are an extreme case of this situation, because their transitions are produced on purpose by curricula, teachers and adaptive engines. To our knowledge, no study has quantified this source of predictability across educational datasets, separated it from personalisation, or examined how it affects LLM-based recommenders.

Evaluating educational recommenders.

Recommender systems for technology-enhanced learning have been reviewed repeatedly 
(
Manouselis et al., 2011
; 
Drachsler et al., 2015
)
, and surveys of their evaluation document a wide variety of methods and goals beyond accuracy 
(
Erdt et al., 2015
)
. Instructional sequencing has also been studied as a policy-learning problem: the robust evaluation matrix compares instructional policies under several student simulators 
(
Doroudi et al., 2017
)
, and a review of reinforcement learning for instructional sequencing examines when data-driven sequencing policies have improved on simpler baselines 
(
Doroudi et al., 2019
)
. On MOOC clickstreams, 
Tang et al. (2016)
 observed that simply following the syllabus already predicted the next resource with an accuracy of 23% on average, and 
Pardos et al. (2017)
 deployed a next-step recommendation framework in a live MOOC. Exercise recommendation is now benchmarked at scale: UniER compares 18 item-level and path-level methods on nine datasets with a simulated cognitive-gain metric 
(
Cheng et al., 2026
)
, and recent methods combine knowledge tracing 
(
Corbett and Anderson, 1994
)
 with semantic information and reinforcement learning 
(
Ozyurt et al., 2025
)
. Peer-based methods such as MCLD recommend the activities passed by the classmates whose record is closest to the learner’s 
(
Jdidou and Khaldi, 2016
; 
Jdidou et al., 2021
)
. These works evaluate methods; we evaluate the evaluation, by asking how much of the measured accuracy any method could obtain from the platform’s sequencing alone.

LLMs as recommenders and in education.

Large language models can rank candidate items zero-shot, but they are sensitive to the order of the history and biased by popularity and by the position of candidates in the prompt 
(
Hou et al., 2024
)
; more generally, they prefer certain option labels in multiple-choice formats 
(
Zheng et al., 2023
)
. In education, LLMs have been evaluated as course recommenders on MOOC data, with accuracy comparable to traditional models 
(
Ma et al., 2025
)
, while comparisons with knowledge tracing caution that LLMs alone assess learners’ evolving knowledge less reliably 
(
Hooshyar et al., 2025
)
. LLM rankers are almost always evaluated on small candidate sets, which makes the choice of negative candidates decisive 
(
Krichene and Rendle, 2020
)
; we show that in educational data this choice determines whether the course structure alone can solve the task.

Next-activity prediction as imitation of a sequencing policy

Setting.

A learner’s log is a sequence of steps 
, where 
 is an activity (a problem, an exercise, a set of questions, a quiz) and 
 indicates whether it was passed. The history before step 
 is 
. A recommender 
 maps a history to a ranking of the activities. In the standard offline protocol, 
 is rewarded when the activity 
 that the learner actually did ranks high in 
, according to a metric 
 such as the hit rate or the normalised discounted cumulative gain at a cut-off 
.

Who chose 
?

In educational platforms, 
 is rarely chosen by the learner alone. It is the output of a 
sequencing policy
 
 that combines several decision makers: the course designer who fixes a syllabus, the teacher who assigns problem sets in a chosen order, the platform that draws the next problem from a pool or selects it adaptively, and the learner, who may follow, skip or revisit. Offline next-activity evaluation therefore measures how well 
 
agrees with

 would help learners), and it can be large for reasons unrelated to the learner. Whenever

Structural predictors and lift over structure.

We call 
structural
 a predictor whose ranking depends on the history only through the last activity and statistics shared by all learners: popularity, the course order, membership of the same container (problem set, skill, part, topic, scheme of work) and population transition frequencies (Section 
4.3
). Let

 a set of personalised recommenders. We report three quantities for each dataset:

(1)

lift over structure
 of a recommender, and

structural share
, is the fraction of the best attainable offline accuracy that the course structure alone delivers. A benchmark with

On-path and off-path steps.

To locate where personalisation could matter, we split test steps by whether the next activity stays in the container of the last one (
on-path
) or leaves it (
off-path
). On-path steps are typically decided by the platform or the assignment (the next problem of a problem set, the next question of a part); off-path steps are those where a teacher, the platform or the learner switches to something else, which is exactly where a recommender would have to make a choice. When the logs record who initiated an activity, as EdNet and Junyi do, we also compare activities started by the platform with activities chosen by the learner.

4 
Experimental setup

4.1 
Datasets

We use eight public datasets from five platforms (Table 
1
). They cover the main ways in which the next activity is decided: teacher-assigned problem sets with problems drawn by the tutor, a mobile application whose engine selects the questions, a self-paced platform organised as a knowledge map, quizzes assigned by teachers within schemes of work, and the course of a MOOC.

A09.

ASSISTments 2009–2010 skill builders, original release 
(
Feng et al., 2009
; 
Heffernan and Heffernan, 2014
)
. Activities are problems; the container is the problem set, from which the tutor draws problems until a mastery criterion is met. We keep main problems, remove the rows duplicated by multi-skill tagging, and order steps by the global response order.

A12, A15, A17.

ASSISTments 2012–2013, 2015 and the 2017 longitudinal data, in the versions prepared by 
Gervet et al. (2020)
. In A12 and A17 activities are problems and, since these versions do not include problem-set identifiers, the skill serves as container (ASSISTments skill builders are organised by skill). In A15 activities are the 100 skill-builder problem sets themselves and no container is defined.

EdNet.

Logs of Santa, a mobile application preparing learners for the TOEIC English test 
(
Choi et al., 2020
)
, in the most detailed format (KT4), for a public sample of 4,396 learners (4,387 after preprocessing). Activities are 
bundles
, the sets of one to five questions that the application presents together; the container is the TOEIC part (1 to 7). Each bundle records how it was started: in 
sprint
 mode the learner chooses a part and the application serves its questions; 
adaptive offers
 and 
tutor
 recommendations are initiated by the application; review modes revisit past questions.

Junyi.

Junyi Academy 2015, a mathematics platform built on Khan Academy’s open-source code and organised as a knowledge map 
(
Chang et al., 2015
)
; we draw a random sample of 20,000 learners. Activities are exercises; the container is the topic of the map. Each attempt records whether the exercise was 
suggested
 by the platform from the prerequisite map, opened from a 
topic
 icon, done in 
review
 mode after proficiency, or chosen by the learner (
self
).

Eedi.

Diagnostic questions from the NeurIPS 2020 Education Challenge (tasks 3 and 4) 
(
Wang et al., 2020
)
. Answers are time-stamped to the minute, which leaves the order of questions within a quiz undetermined, so activities are quizzes assigned by teachers; the container is the scheme of work in which the quiz was assigned.

MOOC.

The ACT-MOOC data 
(
Kumar et al., 2019
)
, derived from the KDD Cup 2015 dropout-prediction data: the actions of 7,047 learners on the 97 activities of one MOOC, time-stamped to the second (ties are broken by the action identifier). Activities are anonymised and the log records neither outcomes nor course structure, so every step counts as passed, CONT reduces to ORDER, and the two methods that need outcomes (ZPD and DKT, below) are not applied.

In every dataset, consecutive responses of a learner to the same activity are merged into one 
step
, which is passed when at least half of the responses are correct. Activities with fewer than five steps are removed. A public preprocessed version of OLI Engineering Statics was examined and discarded: its items appear to be problem steps, as 74% of transitions go to the next item identifier, so the next item is mostly fixed by the problem itself rather than chosen.

4.2 
Task, protocols and metrics

Next new activity.

Given a history, a method ranks all activities the learner has not done yet, and the target is the next activity the learner started that was new to them. Activities already in the history are removed from the ranking of every method. This matches the purpose of an activity recommender (proposing what to do next) and removes the trivial gains of re-recommending past activities.

Protocol P1 (leave-last-out).

For each learner, the last new step is the test target and the previous new step the validation target; models are trained on all learners’ sequences cut just before their validation target. This is the protocol of most sequential recommendation studies, and the one under audit. Hyperparameters are selected on (at most 4,000) validation cases by NDCG@10, and the selected configuration is evaluated on all test cases.

Protocol P2 (global temporal split).

Because leave-last-out mixes time periods across learners 
(
Ji et al., 2023
)
, we repeat the study with a global cut-off at the 80th percentile of step times: models are trained on all steps before the cut-off, and each learner active on both sides contributes one case whose target is their first new activity after it. Hyperparameters are selected with an earlier cut-off (70th percentile) on the training period only.

Metrics.

We report HR@
 (whether the target is among the first 
 activities) and NDCG@10 over the full ranking of new activities. Several structural predictors give equal scores to many activities, so every metric is computed as its exact expectation under a uniformly random order of tied activities; ties are never broken by activity index. Intervals are 95% percentile bootstrap intervals over test cases (2,000 resamples); differences between two methods are tested with a paired bootstrap and Holm’s correction across datasets.

4.3 
Methods

Structural predictors.

POP
 ranks activities by the number of training learners who did them. 
ORDER
 follows the course order: each activity receives the median relative position of its first occurrence in the training learners’ sequences, and the activities after the last one are ranked nearest first, followed by the earlier ones. 
CONT
 ranks the unseen activities of the last activity’s container first (in course order), then the others as ORDER. 
MC
 ranks activities by how often they followed the last activity in the training sequences (a first-order Markov chain with a popularity back-off), which captures typical paths without any course metadata. MC is a classical sequential baseline; we count it as structural because it is not personalised: all learners whose last activity is the same receive the same ranking. None of the four uses anything about the learner beyond the last activity.

Personalised recommenders.

Collaborative filtering: item-based nearest neighbours with recency weighting (
ItemKNN
; 
Sarwar et al., 2001
), 
RP3beta
 
(
Paudel et al., 2016
)
, 
EASE
 
(
Steck, 2019
)
 and implicit alternating least squares with fold-in of the full history (
iALS
; 
Hu et al., 2008
). Session-based: 
SKNN
, in which the 
 training learners whose activities overlap most with the learner’s 
 most recent distinct activities (recent ones weighted more) vote for the activities they did, weighted by similarity; this is the vector variant of session-based nearest neighbours, which was competitive with or better than neural session-based models in the comparisons of 
Jannach and Ludewig (2017)
 and 
Ludewig and Jannach (2018)
. Peer-based: 
MCLD
, which recommends the activities passed by the most-coincident, least-different classmates 
(
Jdidou and Khaldi, 2016
; 
Jdidou et al., 2021
)
, and its adaptive variant 
aMCLD
, which takes the 
 first peers in the same order; here all training learners act as classmates. Knowledge tracing: 
ZPD
 estimates the learner’s success rate on each skill from the history and recommends the unseen activities whose predicted success is closest to a target rate 
, in the spirit of keeping practice at a desirable difficulty 
(
Wilson et al., 2019
)
; it represents pedagogically motivated policies, which do not try to imitate the logs. 
DKT
 applies the same policy with the success probabilities of deep knowledge tracing 
(
Piech et al., 2015
)
, an LSTM over (skill, outcome) steps, so that a weak success estimate cannot explain the policy’s results; on the P1 test cases, DKT predicts the outcome of the target step better than ZPD’s smoothed rates on seven of the seven datasets with outcomes (AUC 0.65–0.82 against 0.50–0.77). Sequential: 
SASRec
 
(
Kang and McAuley, 2018
)
 with outcome-aware input tokens (activity and pass/fail) and a cross-entropy loss 
(
Klenitskiy and Vasilev, 2023
)
. All personalised methods are tuned on the validation cases; grids and implementation details are given in Appendix 
A
. RAND (random ranking) is reported for reference.

4.4 
LLM rankers

We evaluate open-weight instruction-tuned models of four sizes from one family, which share a tokenizer, so that every size reads exactly the same prompts: Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct 
(
Qwen Team, 2024
)
, run in bfloat16 with Hugging Face Transformers 
(
Wolf et al., 2020
)
, and Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, run 4-bit quantized (the official Q4_K_M files) with llama.cpp 
(
ggml-org, 2026
)
. All run on ordinary CPUs, so that the experiment can be reproduced without special hardware or paid services. Since an LLM cannot rank thousands of activities in one prompt, all methods are compared on candidate sets of 20 activities: the target and 19 negatives the learner has not done. We use two kinds of negatives. 
Random
 negatives are drawn uniformly. 
Structural
 negatives are drawn from the 20 activities ranked highest by MC and the 20 ranked highest by CONT, so that every candidate is plausible from the course structure alone. By construction they penalise MC and CONT, whose own top-ranked activities they are; their purpose is to test the other methods on decisions that the structure cannot make.

The LLM scores candidates by likelihood. The prompt describes the platform, lists the learner’s ten most recent activities with their names and outcomes, and ends with “The next activity the student starts is:”. The score of a candidate is the mean log-probability of its name as the continuation, minus the mean log-probability of the same name after a prompt that contains no history, a pointwise mutual information that removes the model’s preference for short or frequent names. Activity names are those provided by the datasets: skill names for A09, part names for EdNet, exercise and topic names for Junyi and subject names for Eedi (the other ASSISTments versions provide only identifiers and are not used). We first tried the common multiple-choice format, with the candidates labelled A to T and the score of a candidate given by the probability of its letter, but the smallest model then chose the same letter in most cases whatever the content (84% of the Eedi cases), the label-selection bias documented by 
Zheng et al. (2023)
; its accuracy was at the random level (HR@1 of 0.045), and the likelihood formulation avoids the problem. Each dataset contributes 200 test cases per type of negatives, sampled at random from the P1 test cases, and every other method is scored on exactly the same candidates; the 3B and 7B models, which take up to a minute per case on a laptop CPU, were scored on the first 100 of them. The llama.cpp scorer reproduces the bfloat16 scorer: with the 0.5B model in 16-bit precision, it puts the same candidate first in 40 of 40 test cases, and with the 0.5B model quantized like the larger ones, HR@1 changes by 0.022 on average and at most 0.070 over the 8 candidate sets (same top candidate in 80% of cases). Appendix 
G
 gives an example prompt.

5.1 
RQ1: How much of the next activity does the structure predict?

Table 
2
 describes the test transitions. In five of the six datasets with containers, most next activities stay in the container of the last one, from 60% of steps in Junyi to 93% in Eedi (63% in A09, 69% in A12 and 77% in EdNet); in A17, 40% do. Transitions are concentrated: in A17 the last activity has a median of 5.2 effective successors and the logged next activity has a mean training transition probability of 0.49, so the first-order transition heuristic MC alone places the exact next problem first in 0.650 of the test cases; in the MOOC it does so in 0.393 of the cases. The course order is a much weaker signal than transitions: only between 51% and 75% of next activities lie ahead of the last one in the estimated course order, and ORDER is far below MC on every dataset (Table

Syllabus or paths?

The structural share comes mostly from the population transitions, not from the course metadata. Without MC, the best of POP, ORDER and CONT reaches between 0.20 and 1.00 of the best accuracy (median 0.45; Table 
4
, Figure 
1
). It exceeds half of it only on A09, where the container (the problem set from which the tutor draws problems) is the best structural predictor, and on Junyi, Eedi and the MOOC (0.55, 0.61 and 0.59). The shortcut is therefore the typical paths that assignments and engines produce, which the logs record, more than the syllabus as written.

Which personalised methods.

SASRec, which conditions on the order of the history, is the best personalised method on seven of the eight datasets. SKNN, which also looks at the most recent activities, is second on A15, Junyi, Eedi and the MOOC, but beats MC only on A15 and, marginally, on EdNet (0.037 against 0.035). The static collaborative filters (EASE, RP3beta, iALS) and the peer-based methods, which use the set of past activities rather than its order, fall below MC on the datasets with strongly sequenced logs (far below on A17 and Eedi) and beat it only on A15. MCLD, designed for small classes following the same course, is most competitive on A15 (0.447 against 0.522 for SASRec), where problem sets play the role of course units. The knowledge-tracing policies rank below popularity: ZPD on all seven datasets with recorded outcomes, and DKT on six of them, although DKT predicts the outcome of the target step better than ZPD’s smoothed rates on all of them (Section 
4.3
). A better model of the learner does not help a policy that does not try to imitate the logs: recommending activities at a desirable difficulty disagrees with what the platform made learners do next, which offline next-activity evaluation can only score as an error.

6 
What offline accuracy misses: a check with known needs

The real logs cannot tell which recommendation a learner needed. To check what the summary quantities and offline accuracy measure when the answer is known, we simulate logs in which the share of steps decided by the platform is a parameter.

Simulation.

A course has 10 units of 10 activities in a fixed order. Each of 3,000 learners has three weak units, where they succeed 45% of the time (85% elsewhere), and logs 20 to 40 new activities. At each step, with probability 
 the next activity follows the platform path: the next unseen activity in course order, or, with probability 0.2, another unseen activity of the current unit. Otherwise the learner works on their needs: an unseen activity of a weak unit, at random. All methods of Section 
4.3
 are run unchanged under protocol P1 (SASRec with a 10-minute budget). Because the needs are known, every method also gets a score of 
true usefulness
, need@10: the share of its top 10 that are unseen activities of the learner’s weak units. Two oracles complete the comparison: NEED recommends exactly the unseen activities of the weak units, and PI ranks by the true probabilities of the logging policy.

Structure-corrected accuracy.

One way to look past the platform is to weight each test step by the inverse of its structural propensity: 
, where

), and on the MOOC (
). Personalisation thus carries a real signal on the steps the structure does not explain; plain NDCG@10 buries it under the steps the structure does. Because the weights come from MC itself, this view is deliberately unfavourable to MC, and we report it next to plain accuracy, not instead of it.

7 
Discussion

What offline next-activity accuracy measures.

On public educational logs, next-activity accuracy is dominated by how well a method reproduces the sequencing that teachers and platforms imposed. This does not make offline evaluation useless, but it changes what a result means. A method that beats the structural predictors by a clear margin predicts logged choices better than the platform’s typical paths; a method that does not, whatever its absolute score, has shown nothing about personalisation. Conversely, a policy designed for learning, such as practising at a desirable difficulty, is penalised precisely because it departs from the logged policy, so offline accuracy can rank pedagogically motivated recommenders below simple imitators. The simulation of Section 
6
 shows how far this can go: when the platform decides most steps, a recommender that serves every learner’s needs ranks near the bottom by offline accuracy, and a recommender selected by offline accuracy learns the platform. The consequence for the field is not to stop offline evaluation but to report it against the right floor and on the right steps.

Why the shortcut is strong in education.

Educational platforms are designed to sequence. Mastery-based problem sets draw the next problem from a small pool (A09), applications serve questions part by part (EdNet), knowledge maps suggest the next exercise from prerequisites (Junyi), teachers assign quizzes within schemes of work (Eedi), and a MOOC lays out its activities in a course. Each of these mechanisms produces low-branching, highly repeatable transitions, the very structures that make sequential benchmarks shortcut-solvable 
(
Han et al., 2026
)
. In addition, the datasets most used for educational recommendation were collected for knowledge tracing, a task for which the sequencing was irrelevant; they were never meant to evaluate the choice of activities.

Implications for peer-based and LLM-based recommenders.

Peer-based methods such as MCLD were designed for a class following one course, with recommendations that come with a one-sentence explanation 
(
Jdidou and Khaldi, 2016
; 
Jdidou et al., 2021
)
. Next-new-activity accuracy rewards neither their transparency nor their remediation component, and on long, strongly sequenced histories their set-based view of the learner is outperformed by the transition heuristic. On A15, where activities are whole problem sets, MCLD is close to the best methods under leave-last-out (0.447 against 0.522 for SASRec) and the best method under the global temporal split. Their value has to be established on the steps where a choice is made, and ideally in deployment. For LLM-based recommenders, our results with open models of up to 7 billion parameters are a caution rather than a verdict: much larger models may well rank candidates better than the ones we could run, although within our range size made no difference. Whatever the model, its accuracy on candidate sets is uninterpretable unless the structural predictors and a simple lexical baseline are scored on the same sets, and unless structural negatives are reported.

7.1 
A syllabus-aware protocol for offline evaluation

The results suggest a short checklist for anyone reporting offline results of an educational recommender. Every item is cheap; the accompanying package implements the first three in a few lines of code.

1.

Report the structural predictors.
 Include popularity, the course order (ORDER), container continuation (CONT) and population transitions (MC), evaluated on exactly the same cases and candidates as the proposed method. They need no training and no hyperparameters, so there is no reason to omit them.

2.

Report the lift over structure, not only the metric.
 Give 
, the lift 
 of each method over it with a confidence interval, and the structural share 
. A raw NDCG@10 of 0.7 means little if the course order alone reaches 0.7. Add the structure-corrected accuracy of Section 
6
, which weights each test step by the inverse of its transition probability, to show how a method does on the steps the structure does not explain.

3.

Split by kind of step.
 Report the share and the metrics of steps along transitions never seen in training and, where containers exist, of off-path steps: this is where a recommender has a choice to make. When the logs record who initiated each activity, split by initiator as well.

4.

Choose negatives deliberately in sampled evaluation.
 For LLM rankers and any evaluation on candidate sets, report results with structural negatives (candidates that the course structure finds plausible) in addition to random ones, and always score the structural predictors on the same sets.

5.

Prefer temporal splits and state the protocol.
 Leave-last-out and a global temporal split agreed here on the best structural predictor and on most comparisons, but absolute scores were far lower under the temporal split and the structural share fell on two datasets. The temporal split also avoids training on the future of other learners 
(
Ji et al., 2023
)
.

6.

Do not read agreement with the logs as pedagogical value.
 Next-activity accuracy measures how well a model imitates the sequencing policy that produced the data. Claims about learning require outcomes: randomised sequencing data, field experiments, or at least policy evaluation under several student models 
(
Doroudi et al., 2017
; 
Doroudi et al., 2019
)
.

7.2 
Limitations

The study is offline and observational: it shows how much of the measured accuracy is attainable from the structure, not which recommendations would help learners. The container information differs across datasets (problem sets, skills, parts, topics, schemes of work), and in A12 and A17 the skill stands in for the problem set, which probably understates the structural share there. The LLMs are open-weight models of up to 7 billion parameters run on CPUs, the two largest 4-bit quantized and scored on 100 cases per set, chosen so that the experiment can be reproduced without special hardware or paid services; much larger models may behave differently, although the dependence of every method on the type of negatives does not depend on the LLM. The MOOC activities are anonymised, so the MOOC has no container or course metadata and cannot enter the LLM comparison, and DKT was trained with fixed settings and a short budget. EdNet is represented by a public sample of a few thousand learners and Junyi by a random sample of 20,000 learners. SASRec reached its training budget on A12, A15 and EdNet, its main results come from one training per dataset (four trainings on five datasets agree except on Eedi), and under P2 the A09 test set is small (158 learners). Finally, the hyperparameter grids are modest; longer training and better-tuned personalised models could reduce 
 somewhat, but they would have to close the large gaps observed on off-path and learner-chosen steps to change the conclusions.

8 
Conclusion

Recommenders and LLMs evaluated on educational logs largely learn the platform: they beat the syllabus, or more exactly the typical paths that teachers and platforms impose, by much less than their absolute scores suggest. Across eight datasets from five platforms, a predictor that only knows the last activity delivers between 0.81 and 1.00 of the best offline accuracy, mostly through the population’s typical paths. Personalised recommenders improve on it significantly on five datasets, by 0.007 to 0.102 NDCG@10, but none does well on steps along unseen transitions where these are frequent enough to measure, and none closes the gap on steps that leave the current container or that learners choose themselves, and small open LLMs do not beat the best structural predictor on the same candidates except where every method is close to the random level. We hope that the summary quantities, the checklist and the code released with this paper help future work show what an educational recommender adds beyond the platform it was trained on.

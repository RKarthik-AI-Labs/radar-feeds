Abstract

Disentangling writing style from semantic content is a fundamental challenge in literary text modeling. Style-content entanglement causes models to rely on semantic content for 
authorship attribution
 (AA) tasks and memorize author-specific content for 
style imitation
 (SI) tasks. We propose EDITORS (
Edit
ing L
oR
A 
S
ubspaces), a 
diagnose-then-deflate
 framework that adapts pretrained large language models and prioritizes stylistic features, making it effective for both AA and SI tasks. EDITORS first trains a diagnostic LoRA adapter using style-neutral statements derived from the training corpus to identify a content-oriented subspace. It then trains the final style adapter under activation-space deflation that projects input activations away from the identified content-oriented directions. We empirically demonstrate that EDITORS is effective. On three AA benchmarks spanning literature, social media, and news, EDITORS achieves state-of-the-art accuracy, outperforming the strongest existing baseline by up to 29%. On SI, EDITORS generates stylistically faithful and diverse passages, outperforming baselines in achieving style similarity while adhering to content specifications. Our code is available at 
https://github.com/zixuan-n/editors
.

Problem Formulation

Let

The text passage distribution over

Text samples

Authorship attribution (AA).

In the closed-set AA task, given a passage 
 and a
candidate author set 
, our goal is to predict the most likely author

. We aim to do so by training an attribution model based on training data 
, so that it can perform well on held-out passages 
 too.
Ideally, the model should identify which author-specific marginal distribution 
 most likely produced the passage, while avoiding brittle reliance on content shortcuts from 
. This distinction matters because different authors may write about similar content, and the same author may write about different content across topics,
genres, or domains unseen in training.

Style imitation (SI).

In the general SI task, the model is given a target author 
 and the goal is to generate a passage 
 that approximates author 
’s marginal distribution 
. A useful variant is 
content-controlled SI
,
where the model is additionally given a content specification 
. The goal is to generate a passage

that approximates author 
’s expression distribution 
, or essentially how the author would likely express the requested content 
. The ideal generative model should (1) be stylistically faithful to the target author 
, (2) accurately convey the specified content 
, and (3) avoid just copying from its training data 
.

Note that both tasks require learning from samples 
, where content 
 and style 
 are observed entangled in text passage form. For AA, content information from 
 can be
useful but misleading: some content patterns may help identify an
author easily during training, but over-reliance on 
 can lead to poor
generalization under topic or domain shift (e.g., when an author writes about a different topic). For SI, the issue may
be more problematic: preserving author-specific content patterns from 
 can cause
the model to reproduce training
passages or ignore the user-provided content specification 
 in content-controlled SI. 
Hence, our method must rely less on content information from 
 while retaining the author style information from 
.
 As 
 and 
 are latent, our method does not estimate them directly but instead adopts a geometric intervention that reduces content-oriented components to achieve our objective (
Section
 
3.1
).

2.1 
Related Works

Authorship attribution (AA).

Existing AA methods typically either:

(a)
 rely primarily on content information, and 
(b)
 attempt to reduce content information.
Works in 
(a)
 (e.g., BertAA 
[
12
]
, STAR 
[
27
]
, PART 
[
26
]
 and TRACE 
[
54
]
) treat each text as a single representation, often cause content signals to dominate and may exploit semantic content shortcuts.
Recent LLM-based AA methods use in-context learning 
[
23
]
 where reference texts for all candidate authors are provided in prompts, but face challenges in scaling due to context-window limits and high inference cost, and do not generalize well across domains.
In contrast, works in 
(b)
 explicitly emphasize style and attempt to reduce content dependence.

Stylometry
[
16
]
 directly extracts numeric stylistic features to train classification models, but its performance declines quickly with number of authors. Other works include those training content-independent style embeddings by contrastive learning 
[
57
, 
45
]
 and separate style and content encoders by minimizing their mutual information 
[
57
, 
45
]
.
However, these methods tend to remove semantic information and yield low performance in real AA tasks. Recent works such as Waterfall 
[
35
]
 proposed an active AA approach where text are paraphrased with LLMs to embed author-specific watermarks. Such watermarks are robust and can be used to track LLM data provenance 
[
20
, 
41
]
, but require active data processing that may impact the author’s original style. EDITOR presents a complementary approach for attribution without edits to the original text.

Style imitation (SI).
 Similarly, existing SI methods typically either: 
(a)
 do not attempt to separate content and style, and 
(b)
 focus on learning style by controlling or removing content. For 
(a)
, 
[
50
, 
39
, 
36
]
 directly fine-tune a language model on a target author corpus but risk memorizing training passages. Prompt-based methods 
[
10
, 
56
]
 are tuning-free and flexible, but their stylistic control is limited by context-window size and prompt sensitivity 
[
4
, 
34
]
, tending to capture surface markers rather than implicit writing habits. For 
(b)
,
early methods 
[
28
]
 use true parallel source-target pairs which are rare and expensive to construct for real authors. Recent methods such as ASTRAPOP 
[
37
]
 relax this constraint by constructing pseudo-parallel data by neutral paraphrasing or back-translation, then training models against style, content, and fluency rewards. However, most RL-based methods operate on coarse attributes such as sentiment, genre or formality 
[
42
, 
19
, 
38
]
 rather than individual author style. Methods such as StyleLM 
[
52
]
, StoryTrans 
[
59
]
 and ParChoice 
[
18
]
 rewrite arbitrary input text in a target style through denoising autoencoders, combinatorial paraphrasing etc. However, they are typically evaluated on a few authors with highly distinctive voices and only operate on paragraph-level rewriting rather than long-passage generation from compact content specification. While not applied to SI, past works have studied parameter-space subspace projection for model editing and LoRA fusion of separate tasks 
[
55
, 
13
, 
6
]
. Unlike these works, EDITORS applies activation-space deflation that allows it to disentangle style and content within text (see 
Section
 
C.2
 for details).

Unlike existing works, our method EDITORS learn style while optionally retaining content information with our diagnose-then-deflate approach, significantly improving AA and SI performance over baselines. It does not require specially curated data, scales to larger author sets, and can generate long stylized passages from compact content specifications.

The EDITORS Framework

Our training framework EDITORS adapts a pretrained LLM to perform AA and SI and prioritizes stylistic features.

diagnose-then-deflate

Main results.

We first compare EDITORS on five benchmarks with AA baselines, along with an additional naïve baseline that we introduced – an LLM finetuned on the benchmark’s respective training data (Llama-3.1-8B SFT).
Each of the benchmarks evaluates methods on different types of challenges associated with AA. CCAT50 is a topic-controlled news benchmark where authors often discuss overlapping corporate events and entities, making coarse topical cues less reliable.
Blogs50 is noisy and naturally imbalanced, testing robustness to uneven author coverage and informal writing. Gutenberg30 is a long-form literary benchmark where AA depends on discourse-level style cues that are difficult for short-context encoder baselines to capture.

As seen in 
Table
 
2
, EDITORS achieves the best accuracy on all five benchmarks, outperforming all baselines by a significant margin. Note that our newly introduced SFT baseline (Llama SFT) surprisingly also performs very competitively against baselines, indicating that several of the baseline methods may have specialized approaches that might have disadvantaged them in achieving generalizable performance, unlike EDITORS which consistently outperforms across benchmarks. EDITORS’s gains are especially large on more challenging 50-author settings, increasing accuracy from 
 to 
 on CCAT50 and from 
 to 
 on Blogs50. As the candidate author set grows, authors are more likely to share overlapping topics. EDITORS’s approach of deflating content-oriented directions may have supported its strong performance in such realistic scenarios with more overlapping content and the model must rely more heavily on style cues.

The inconsistent performance of baselines may be indicative of challenges around their method’s generalizability. For example, BertAA is competitive on shorter news and blog texts but performs poorly on Gutenberg30, likely because long literary passages exceed the effective input constraints of BERT-style encoders. STAR, which is pretrained on large datasets, does not consistently scale across domains and larger candidate sets. StyleEmbedding and StyleDistance underperform broadly, suggesting that aggressively removing content loses useful author-discriminative information. GPT-4 + LIP is limited to small candidate sets because reference-passage prompting does not scale to 30-50 authors. While our SFT baseline (Llama SFT) is surprisingly robust, it faces challenges in generalizing beyond its training dataset when required to handle larger candidate sets.

5 
Conclusion

Existing methods often entangle author style with semantic content, causing AA to rely on content, SI to reproduce training passages, and content-controlled SI to ignore the user-specified content. Our proposed method EDITORS successfully prioritizes stylistic features without discarding semantic information aggressively by diagnosing a content-oriented subspace and learning style under activation-space deflation, so that selected content-oriented directions are suppressed during training. We demonstrate that EDITORS is highly effective on various AA and (content-controlled) SI benchmarks.
Our results show that deflation behaves as intended: it suppresses content without destroying general semantic information, and activation-space deflation outperforms parameter-space deflation.

Limitation and future work.

Due to the lack of real-world datasets where the same content is expressed in different author styles, we did not provide a more direct ablation controlling for style-specific attribution. We also did not consider uncertainty quantification 
[
30
, 
33
]
 of our attributions, which would be valuable in practical deployment.
Although we take the ensemble of classifiers to reduce the SI evaluator bias, the evaluation still relies on automatic evaluators which may not fully capture the real author style unlike human expert evaluations. Future work should therefore focus on building stronger benchmarks for AA with better control over semantic content, and on developing more direct evaluations of how much attribution models rely on style and content respectively.

Appendix A 
Ethical Considerations

AA and SI may be misused for deanonymization, surveillance, unsupported authorship accusations, or impersonation. Our results should therefore be interpreted as model-based evidence rather than definitive authorship proof. Practical deployment should consider uncertainty quantification of attributions to support more holistic investigation. For SI, generated text should be clearly disclosed as machine-generated. We encourage future deployment to include appropriate safeguards, such as watermarking generated text and restricting imitation of living authors without consent.

C.2 
Deflation

Deflation
 (a.k.a. 
orthogonal projection
) is a well-known geometric mechanism to decouple representations and mitigate interference between competing signals. It works by projecting a vector to the closest point in a subspace (i.e., the representation of interest) by removing the component orthogonal to that subspace (i.e., the competing signal). For example, in activation-space deflation, orthogonal projection
can efficiently remove information such as gender and topic from embeddings 
[
49
]
. 
Qiu et al. [47]
 edits LLM at inference time by orthogonal projection at hidden activation space to reduce its undesirable behaviors. In the LoRA context, this technique is utilized in continual learning and knowledge editing by operating directly on the weight matrix. Approaches like O-LoRA 
[
55
]
, O-Edit 
[
5
]
 and AlphaEdit 
[
13
]
 constrain LoRA updates (
) to be orthogonal to the subspaces of previously learned tasks or preserved knowledge, thereby preventing catastrophic forgetting. Similarly, NP-LoRA 
[
6
]
 prevents interference in LoRA fusion by projecting the weights of a content LoRA onto the null space of a style LoRA’s principal directions.

Motivated by these ideas, we aim to use orthogonal projection to reduce interference between content information and stylistic features in texts. Unlike prior weight-centric methods that risk aggressively removing the semantic information, we propose an activation-space deflation mechanism to blind the style adapter to selected semantic signals without directly removing the semantic information.

Appendix F 
Implementation

F.1 
Authorship Attribution (AA)

All models are loaded with 4-bit NF4 quantization and bfloat16 computation, and trained with gradient checkpointing. We attach LoRA adapters to the query, key, and value projection modules, using rank 
, scaling factor 
, and dropout 
. The diagnostic content adapter 
 is trained on the style-neutral statements corpus (App. 
G.1
) via continued instruction tuning with TRL’s 
SFTTrainer
. The final style adapter 
 then uses the original passage-author supervision, formatted as an instruction-style prompt that asks the model to identify the author based on writing style.

The diagnostic content adapter 
 is trained for 
 epochs with datasets of style-neutral statements. For CCAT, the domain warm-up 
 (described in 
Section
 
3.4
) is trained for 
 epochs and the style adapter 
 for 
 epochs. For Blogs and Gutenberg, the domain warm-up 
 is trained for 
 epoch and the style adapter 
 for 
 epochs. We use a maximum sequence length of 
 for both stages across all datasets. Both stages use learning rate 
, warm-up ratio 
, weight decay 
, per-device batch size 
, and gradient accumulation 
. For the plain LoRA SFT baseline, we use the same total number of training epochs as the corresponding diagnose-then-deflate EDITORS configuration. All backbone LLMs are trained with the same hyperparameters.

We do not report error bars for AA because the evaluation is deterministic: we do not use stochastic sampling/sampling-based decoding, so repeated runs of the same checkpoint on the same split produce identical predictions.

F.2 
Style Imitation (SI)

We use the same LoRA configuration as in the AA experiments. The main difference lies in the supervision format. The diagnostic content adapter 
 is reused from the AA setting, and the deflated stage then performs style fine-tuning under activation-space deflation. We evaluate two variants of our method. The default setting skips domain warm-up and trains the style adapter 
 directly for 
 epochs. The second variant first applies domain warm-up for 
 epochs and then performs deflated style learning for another 
 epochs, so that the total training budget remains comparable across settings.

At inference time, each of the 
 Gutenberg authors is paired with the same seven stylistically neutral content summaries listed in App. 
G.3
. We generate one passage for each author on the given summary, yielding 
 generations for evaluation.

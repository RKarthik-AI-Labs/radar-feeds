Abstract.

As AI models advance and impact deliberative democracies, there is a growing need and urgency to assess their support of pluralistic viewpoints. Most pluralism benchmarks are insufficient for this: they are not grounded in diversity (i.e., stakeholders across different frames, skills and needs), quality (i.e., rigorous and technical debates), and impact (i.e., invested deliberators and real-world outcomes). To address this, we 
first
 (i) curate DOCKET,
1
1

1

          
          
          
        DOCKET stands for: Dataset of Comments, Knowledge, and Executive Texts.
 the most comprehensive dataset of the U.S. Federal Notice & Comment process to date, a central process in deliberative democracy. DOCKET consists of 42.3M public comments on 22,400 regulations across 44 government agencies between 2016 and 2026 and contains millions of policy debates; we rigorously link public comments to particular policy items proposed by regulators to track the public’s attention and the responsiveness of regulators to public input. (ii) 
Next
, we demonstrate the pluralistic utility of DOCKET – we show that public commenters have a wide breadth of perspectives with significant depth in policy expertise and that regulators are often responsive. (iii) 
Finally
, we present DOCKETBench, the first systematic evaluation of LLMs’ ability to represent human policy perspectives in federal rulemaking.
We find that only 18–30% of LLM-generated feedback aligns with real human comments. LLMs critique proposals broadly but generally, lacking the specific grounding, citations, and lived-experience arguments that humans bring to bear; in addition, most models we test systematically agree with government perspectives (overlapping with up to 68% of their claims) and agree least with labor unions (as little as 26% of their claims), demonstrating a fundamental skew in perspectives that even tools like persona modeling do not solve. Taken together, we reveal fundamental gaps in how language models navigate real policy issues, and DOCKETBench serves as a testbed for closing those gaps in a crucial real-world domain.

1. 
Introduction

Generative AI has become widespread in government interactions with the public, from official communications 
(
Auchincloss, 2023
; 
Savoy, 2024
)
 to court filings 
(
U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York, 2023
)
. This paper focuses on one such consequential setting: notice-and-comment (N&C) rulemaking, which has been described by legal scholars as 
“one of the greatest inventions of modern government”

(
Davis, 1970
)
. Through N&C, agencies translate legislation into regulations by publishing proposed rules, inviting the public to submit “data, views, or arguments,” and considering submissions before finalizing them 
(
Kerwin and Furlong, 2019
; 
U.S. Congress, 1946
)
. Language models are beginning to assist — both by (1) helping citizens draft comments 
(
Balla et al., 2022b
; 
U.S. Congress, 2024
)
 and by (2) helping agencies analyze submissions, refine proposals, and anticipate public response 
(
Center for Democracy & Technology, 2025
; 
U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, Administration for Children and Families, Office of Head Start, 2024
; 
ICF International, n.d.
; 
ICF International, 2024
; 
Pacific Northwest National Laboratory, 2024
)
2
2

2

            
            
            
          At least seven federal agencies report using AI tools to support comment analysis, raising familiar questions about which comments receive attention and how public input is translated into regulatory change 
(
Center for Democracy & Technology, 2025
; 
U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, Administration for Children and Families, Office of Head Start, 2024
; 
ICF International, n.d.
; 
ICF International, 2024
; 
Pacific Northwest National Laboratory, 2024
)
.
. Yet scholars and policymakers lack systematic evaluations of this assistance.

Simultaneously, there is growing awareness that AI models, specifically when attempting tasks in these kinds of settings, must grow and adapt to 
pluralistic beliefs, values and preferences
 to negotiate
the needs of a diverse, deliberative public 
(
Sorensen et al., 2024
; 
Kirk et al., 2024
; 
Conitzer et al., 2024
)
. Most pluralistic alignment work is evaluated on synthetic disagreements or stated preferences elicited in isolation 
(
Bakker et al., 2022
; 
DURMUS et al., 
; 
Huang et al., 2024
)
, leaving open how models should handle the real-world, high-stakes pluralism.

To address both needs, we introduce an empirical map of the rulemaking process itself: who participates, what claims they raise, which provisions they target, and how agencies respond. We introduce DOCKET: the largest and, to our knowledge, most comprehensive dataset of rulemaking and public comments. DOCKET tracks 44 U.S. federal agencies across 10 years (2016--2026) and consists of 87,005 notices, 17,198 proposed rules, 42.3M public comments from 16 different federal commenting systems, and 22,400 finalized rules.
3
3

3

            
            
            
          DOCKET is U.S.-centric; other rulemaking systems are left to future work.
 To understand the role of comments on rulemaking, we identify 
specifically
 which rule provisions the public commented on, and which comments the agency responded to. We use LLMs to extract provisions from all rules (676K provisions); claims from all comments (13.6M claims); and responses (94K responses) from all finalized rules. We develop a high-performance matching pipeline (claims 
 provisions) and (responses 
 claims). This approach allows us to track what members of the public care about, which comments the government considers deserve a response, and how these comments affect rulemaking. Overall, we find that public commenters engage seriously with regulations (
30% bring specialized knowledge), target specific provisions rather than entire rules, and that agencies take up commenter language at meaningful rates (i.e. when rewriting provisions for final rules, agencies directly adopt wording from commenters 11–26% of the time, and paraphrase another 12–32% of the time).

For these reasons, DOCKET is an ideal dataset for evaluating the pluralism and policy skills in LLMs. We identify three characteristics driving real-world pluralism: 1) Diversity (or breadth): DOCKET comments come from a large range of human policy perspectives (e.g., industry, advocacy, individuals, law firms, labor unions); 2) Quality (or depth): comments exhibit deep policy expertise on specific topics, and 3) Impact: comments are consequential – agencies meaningfully change their policy recommendations directly based on public comments. If pluralistic AI can faithfully represent the public in this setting, it offers a template for AI-mediated deliberation more broadly.

With these insights in hand, we frame our final question: as LLMs become integrated more into our social and political lives, what roles will they play, what feedback will they give, and who will they represent? We design a policy benchmark with the simple goal: “I am proposing this rule, how should it change?” We evaluate LLM responses on two primary dimensions: (1) the substance of LLM comments (which provisions they target and what claims they raise), and (2) the style and framing of LLM comments. Although LLM-feedback has been studied in a range of settings (e.g., writing 
(
Lee et al., 2022
)
, voting 
(
Argyle et al., 2023
; 
Santurkar et al., 2023
)
, and broader public-opinion simulation 
(
Durmus et al., 2024
)
), regulatory commentary is particularly challenging because of the technical rigor and high-stakes legal impact of the process. Indeed, in DOCKETBench, we find that LLM responses tend to be less specific, evidence-based, and technical than human responses, and they do not reflect the broad spectrum of human responses. We find that, across a range of different scaffolding (e.g., persona-driven responses 
(
Li et al., 2016
)
, RAG 
(
Lewis et al., 2020
)
), most models we test side with government and industry commenters most often and with labor unions least often, and they agree with every group less than human commenters (Table 
2
). Looking forward, we hope DOCKET can serve as a test-bed for civic and deliberative AI.

3. 
Analysis

We present an analysis of our dataset DOCKET to motivate the need and design of our policy-feedback benchmark and we establish three properties of pluralism that prior pluralism benchmarks lack 
(
Kirk et al., 2024
; 
DURMUS et al., 
)
:
(1) 
Diversity (or Breadth):
 stakeholder concerns diverge sharply across the corpus;
(2) 
Quality (or Depth):
 commenters tend to focus on specific provisions rather than broad reactions to rules;
and
(3) 
Impact:
 agencies respond to commenters and take up their wording when finalizing provisions in rules.
Together, these findings establish that public comment is a structured, impactful artifact rather than generic preference expression. Any LLM tool deployed in this loop must preserve stakeholder heterogeneity to avoid distorting the policy-feedback record.

Finding #1: Comments have Impact
. Agencies are broadly responsive to, and take up the wording of, commenters’ claims.

We find that, in their responses to comments (Figure 
2
) agencies express 
agreement

partial agreement
 with 11% and 
disagreement
 with 35% (using LLMs to assess)

 modified provisions, consisting of (proposal, claim, final rule) tuples; and comment

We validated these labels with human annotators: in a blind review of 70 cases (51 labeled direct uptake, 19 distractors), 80% of the direct-uptake labels held up as language taken from the comment, 86% if we count close paraphrases (Appendix

Finding #2: Comments are Diverse
: Stakeholder concerns diverge sharply. 

Different subgroups in our population, defined above, express substantively different concern frames across all agencies and years. Table 
1
 reports the distribution of primary frames, assigned one per comment, across the seven subgroups. The contrasts are large: industry comments tend to be primarily concerned with Economic issues (63% 
 18% per agency); law-firm comments concentrate (67% 
 17%) on Legal concerns; labor unions on Safety (39% 
 30%) and Economic (30% 
 28%); expert organizations on Health (29% 
 17%). Individual commenters are by far the most diffuse. These divergences are not artifacts of topic mix: even within the same docket, stakeholder groups frame their concerns differently. Across 292 dockets where at least two stakeholder types each contributed 
5 claims, the mean within-docket Jensen–Shannon divergence between org-type pairs is 0.18 (1,280 pairs), compared to 0.42 for cross-docket comparisons—confirming that roughly half the divergence reflects genuine stakeholder perspective differences rather than docket-topic selection.

These patterns provide large-
 empirical support for a long-standing prediction in administrative law. 
Wagner (2010)
 theorizes that well-resourced participants—industry and law firms—dominate the notice-and-comment process through “information capture”: by framing their input in the technical and legal registers that agencies are institutionally obligated to address, they shape the informational environment in which rules are made. Our data confirms this at scale: industry concentrates 63% of its claims on economic cost-benefit framing (the register most directly tied to Executive Order 12866 cost-benefit review), while law firms concentrate 67% on legal/constitutional framing (the register that triggers agency obligations under the APA). By contrast, individuals—the most numerous commenters—scatter across all frames, diluting their informational influence. 
Mendelson (2011)
 argues that agencies should nonetheless take these diffuse individual comments seriously as expressions of democratic preference, yet acknowledges the structural challenge: individual commenters’ concerns are less concentrated and harder for agencies to systematically engage with. The size of these effects matters for DOCKETBench: how accurately a system carries each subgroup’s concerns is a natural evaluation axis for any system that aims to summarize, anticipate, or generate public input using LLMs.

4.2. 
Benchmark Results

We now present results from evaluating our baseline strategies on DOCKETBench. We find, overall, that 
alignment on content is much lower than alignment on framing devices and style.
 Specifically, we see large 
content-level
 gaps (i.e. metrics (A) and (B)) between LLMs and human comments (claim precision 18–30%), but small frame and style gaps (metrics (C) and (D), precision 65–82%). This suggests current LLMs’ stylistic pluralism and content-level pluralism abilities are quite divergent: LLMs can write comments that 
sound like
 real commenters but fail to capture the opinions each subgroup cares about. We also find that persona conditioning does not have a uniformly positive impact. Persona conditioning helps weaker models (gpt-4o-mini: +7.3pp style prec.) but not stronger ones (gpt-5-mini: -0.1pp style, prec.). Additionally, we find that 
+Persona
 changes whose side the model takes but not which groups it covers: pooled over personas, agreement with each group shifts by a few points at most (Table 
2
). In other words, giving a model a persona changes its stance much more than it changes which real arguments it makes (Appendix 
B.6
). These findings diverge from prior work indicating that (a) language models are steerable, and that (b) persona guidance can be effective in inducing more representative models. We hypothesize that, unlike in generalist domains, 
technical and legal domain pretraining text put a strong prior over generative performance that overwhelms naive steerings a user might apply
.

One vivid example of this is 
whose side
 LLMs take (Table 
2
). Only 21–33% of LLM claims make a point that a real commenter 
also
 made (i.e. in our data, the cases where a judge labels the pair 
same
 or 
opposing
). Another 55–63% are on the same 
topic
 as a real claim but make a point no commenter made, and 8–17% of LLM comments are on topics that no human commenter focused on (Appendix 
B.7
). On the claims that 
do
 line up with a real claim, LLMs tend to agree more with government and industry positions and less with labor and advocates. Humans show more inter-commenter agreement than human-LLM agreement. Two reasons can explain this wide spread. Firstly, LLMs hedge: 18% of the time they argue both sides of the same real point. Secondly their default position follows the procedural and economic framing that industry and government commenters use, so they side against safety, health and environmental claims more often (Appendix 
B.7
). Three quarters of labor claims, for example, are safety claims, where only 6% of LLMs claims are. This gap holds after controlling for retrieval similarity, agency and judge 
and
 prompting LLMs to 
specifically
 act more like labor advocates. We caveat: labor’s positions are unpopular with human commenters too: other commenters 
also
 agree with labor less than with any other group; however, LLMs still do so even more.

Even when LLMs overlap with human comments, the positions they argue are often more moderate. LLMs, we find, produce comments that overwhelmingly advocate modifying proposals, rather than adding or deleting them: 85.9% of gpt-5-mini claims recommend modification, compared with 43.8% of human claims.
LLMs rarely oppose proposals outright: only 5.1% of their claims are oppositional, compared with 30.4% of human claims.
In addition, the 
kinds
 of arguments that LLMs make to advocate their points skew differently, and LLMs tend to argue from different perspectives. In contrast to human commenters, who mix experiential, economic and technical arguments,
we find that 60.3% of gpt-5-mini claims are procedural, compared with 15.7% of human claims, while normative arguments fall from 26.6% in human comments to 4.5% in gpt-5-mini comments, and experiential arguments fall from 5.8% to 0.8% (Table 
21(b)
). Finally, as shown in Table 
3
, models touch 78–99% of a docket’s provisions, humans 46%. The attention patterns given by LLMs across provisions, independent of model size or persona prompting, are far more uniform than the attention given by humans. Humans concentrate heavily on a small fraction of provisions; models distribute claims uniformly.

5. 
Discussion

Notice-and-comment exists as a way for agencies to receive input from the public. The Administrative Procedures Act (APA) asks for “data, views, or arguments” from anyone and makes agencies answer the significant ones 
(
U.S. Congress, 1946
)
. As we can see in our data, this process brings in diverse stakeholder views: Table 
1
 shows industry stakeholders commenting primarily on cost concerns, law firms commenting on statutory authority, labor unions commenting on safety, individuals commenting on a range of concerns. No single group, we see, raises the full set of concerns; the set comes from the mix. If LLMs were to be used in notice& comment, as suggested by 
(
Balla et al., 2022b
; 
U.S. Congress, 2024
; 
Center for Democracy & Technology, 2025
; 
U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, Administration for Children and Families, Office of Head Start, 2024
; 
Pacific Northwest National Laboratory, 2024
)
, we would expect models to be able to reflect comments for all of these groups. However, our results show that they cannot achieve this aim. These results extend: a tool that summarizes a hundred thousand comments for a rule-writer and lands on the procedural reading each time tells the agency what the median lawyer would have said, which the agency already knew. 
Wagner (2010)
 worried that well-resourced commenters crowd the record with technical and legal argument. A model that defaults to that register would also achieve this end.

This has been addressed, in computer science, primarily through introducing persona prompts and steering vectors 
(
Argyle et al., 2023
; 
Santurkar et al., 2023
)
. In our case, it did very little. Asked to write as a labor union, a model writes more claims (4/5 models wrote 3-6x as many as humans; Llama-3.3-70B wrote half as many), takes a different stance, and still argues from the same procedural and economic frames; its agreement with what real labor commenters said moves by a few points at most for the gpt models (10–18 points for Llama-family models) (Table 
2
, Appendix 
B.6
). The persona changes the stance and style, but not the material. In other words, models can be told whom to speak for, but they do not know what that group actually wishes to comment on. Retrieval, we find, helped gpt-5 more than any persona did (claim precision 24.7 vs. 18.1, Table 
3
), which points the same way: the missing ingredient is information, not instruction.

Several of our findings are novel in relation to prior work on LLM pluralism. In 
Argyle et al. (2023)
 a demographic persona was enough to move GPT-3’s survey answers toward the group’s own, and in 
Santurkar et al. (2023)
 and 
Durmus et al. (2024)
 steering prompts moved opinion distributions toward the named demographic or country. Here, personas impact claims by at 
most
 2.2 points and shift agreement towards any one group by at most a few points for gpt models (Llama-family models shift by 10–18 points on labor). To rule out confounders, we tested several, including: the effects of LLM-generated comment-length (i.e., longer comments have more claims, and will thus match more). We matched human units to different numbers of extracted units, 
. Shown in Appendix 
B.4
 (Figure 
8
), recall levels off around 
=35–50, claim precision moves by less than 0.4 points across all budgets, and unconstrained generation lands on the human median (46 vs. 48 claims per docket) but does not track how much commenting a docket actually drew. Additionally, we found that comment length, topic and agency bias were important signals for comment uptake; our observed results were robust after controlling for these variables.

One explanation for why LLMs reflect such narrow views, here, is that the language of notice&comment is quite restrictive. A proposed rule is written in a narrow register: section numbers, defined terms, modal verbs, the formulas of the Federal Register. Conditioning on this in the prompt pulls a model toward a specific part of the pretraining distribution: the language of the lawyers and agency staff who wrote the documents it learned from. Everything we measure points to this pull. Sixty percent of LLM claims are procedural against 16% of human claims; experiential claims nearly vanish (0.8% vs. 5.8%); the models spread their claims almost evenly over the provisions where humans concentrate on a few; and the groups the models agree with most, government and industry, are the ones that write in that register themselves. In prior settings, which typically ask for responses to surveys, a persona is steering a model in a more general text setting, which might explain why it has a greater steering effect: pretraining has shown the model more realistic responses from these groups in these settings. If this hypothesis is correct, steering should fail most in other formal domains. We did not test this directly; we leave this open for future work.

So far, our findings have focused on low model performance and the shortcomings of current pluralism strategies for mitigating them. Another lens with which to view our results is the 
strength
 LLMs demonstrate on sections of DOCKETBench. Concerns are emerging in government and technology that systems for democratic deliberation may become overwhelmed with rising AI input; improvements on style and framing can increase the number of comments making past filtering systems 
(
Handan-Nader, 2023
)
. Table 
3
 shows strong baseline model performance in Style and Argumentation, strong +persona impacts, and scaling trends for model size; together, these are indications of increased performance being imminently achievable. We urge a broader conversation around what 
kinds
 of improvements should be valued in benchmarks: pluralistic alignment, in Style and Argumentation, means 
sounding like
 a subgroup, not necessarily accurately articulating their concerns. We hope in future work to continue exploring AI detection strategies for optimal human-in-the-loop collaboration. We hope that DOCKET, and its derivative, DOCKETBench can serve as a valuable tool not just for the use-cases outlined here, but other use-cases in the broader community.

Related Work

Motivating DOCKET is a large literature studying how to better inform policymakers and promote participation in rulemaking, both theoretically 
(
Libgober, 2020
; 
Stephenson, 2006
; 
Gailmard and Patty, 2017
)
 and empirically. Scholars have used notices, rules, and comments to study influence 
(
Yackee and Yackee, 2006
)
 and conduct detailed analyses of particular regulators—the SEC 
(
Nixon et al., 2002
; 
Ban and You, 2019
)
, EPA 
(
Balla et al., 2022a
)
, DOT 
(
Naughton et al., 2009
)
, FCC 
(
de Figueiredo, 2006
)
, and Dodd-Frank financial regulations 
(
Carpenter et al., 2024
)
. But these studies typically cover tens to hundreds of rules over short time windows; ours covers magnitudes more, allowing us to test at scale cross-agency heterogeneity and longitudinal dynamics. Our work also relates to administrative-law scholarship on the democratic promise and limits of notice-and-comment rulemaking. The APA requires agencies to solicit and consider public “data, views, or arguments” before issuing final rules 
(
U.S. Congress, 1946
)
, and scholars have long treated this process as a central mechanism of public participation in administrative governance 
(
Davis, 1970
; 
Kerwin and Furlong, 2019
; 
Coglianese, 2006
)
.
At the same time, prior work has questioned whether rulemaking actually democratizes agency decision-making or instead privileges repeat players, technical experts, and organized interests 
(
Cuéllar, 2005
; 
Mendelson, 2011
; 
Wagner, 2010
)
. DOCKET provides evidence for this debate by linking public comments to specific proposed provisions, agency responses, and final-rules, making it possible to measure not only who participates but what claims they raise, which provisions they target, and how agencies respond.

A second line of related work studies mass participation, comment campaigns, and the integrity of
public-comment systems. Agencies increasingly receive large volumes of duplicative, campaign-driven,
or malattributed submissions, raising questions about how comments should be filtered, summarized,
and weighed 
(
Balla et al., 2022b
; 
Mendelson, 2011
; 
Balla et al., 2022a
)
. Handan-Nader’s
study of the FCC’s “Restoring Internet Freedom” proceeding shows both the scale of inauthentic
participation and the methodological difficulty of identifying which comments agencies actually
address 
(
Handan-Nader, 2023
)
. Recent policy discussions have extended these concerns to
computer-generated comments and comment-management infrastructure 
(
U.S. Congress, 2024
)
. DOCKET
builds directly on this literature, but shifts the focus from detecting or discounting mass comments to
mapping the substantive structure of public input at scale.

Our benchmark also connects to work on pluralistic alignment and opinion representation in language
models. Recent work argues that AI systems should not collapse diverse human preferences into a
single aggregate objective, but should instead preserve disagreement across individuals, cultures, and
social groups 
(
Sorensen et al., 2024
; 
Conitzer et al., 2024
)
. Datasets and benchmarks such as
PRISM and global-opinion evaluations test whose views models represent and whether models can
capture subjective, multicultural, or politically heterogeneous judgments

(
Kirk et al., 2024
; 
Durmus et al., 2024
; 
DURMUS et al., 
)
. DOCKETBench differs in evaluating pluralism in a consequential institutional setting: stakeholders are not answering surveys or hypothetical preference questions, but making provision-specific claims about proposed law. This setting requires models to recover not only broad viewpoints, but also the concrete claims, legal frames, evidentiary practices, and stakeholder-specific priorities.

Finally, DOCKET relates to technical work on retrieval, legal-text representation, and model-based
evaluation. Our extraction of provisions is grounded in legal theories of deontic relations

(
Hohfeld, 1913
)
 and recent work that represents regulatory obligations as discrete
units whose survival can be tracked from proposed to final rules 
(
Leahey, 2026
)
. Our
matching pipeline draws on modern retrieval and reranking methods, including sentence embeddings,
dense retrieval, and cross-encoder reranking 
(
Reimers and Gurevych, 2019
; 
Karpukhin et al., 2020
; 
Nogueira et al., 2019
)
,
as well as large-scale serving and evaluation tools 
(
Kwon et al., 2023
; 
Zheng et al., 2023
)
. The
benchmark interventions we test also build on common LLM adaptation strategies: persona prompting

(
Li et al., 2016
)
, retrieval-augmented generation 
(
Lewis et al., 2020
)
, and tool-mediated
generation. In parallel, agencies and contractors are beginning to use AI systems for public-comment
analysis and regulatory review 
(
Center for Democracy & Technology, 2025
; 
U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, Administration for Children and Families, Office of Head Start, 2024
; 
ICF International, n.d.
; 
ICF International, 2024
; 
Pacific Northwest National Laboratory, 2024
)
.
If LLMs are used to summarize comments, anticipate stakeholder objections, or assist rule drafters, failures of pluralistic representation may directly affect how public input is understood.

7. 
Conclusion

We set out to measure two things: how the public takes part in federal rulemaking, and whether language models can take part in the same way. For the first, DOCKET links 42.3M comments, 17,198 proposed rules and 22,400 final rules at the level of the individual provision, claim and agency response. The record does not match the common belief that comment periods are swamped by low-quality input. Commenters engage specific provisions, bring specialized knowledge about a third of the time, and divide by interest in what they argue about. Agencies answer them, and when they rewrite a provision they adopt commenter wording 11–26% of the time.

For the second, DOCKETBench asks a model the question an agency would put to a commenter, “how should this rule change?”, and measures the answer against what real commenters said. The models write comments that sound like public comments; however, they typically represent claims most often that government and industry commenters made. They rarely say what labor unions, advocates or individuals said, and persona prompts do not change that. We release both the dataset and the benchmark so that the tools now being built to draft and read public comments can be judged on whose perspectives they carry rather than on how fluent they sound.

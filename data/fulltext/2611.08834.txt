Abstract

Specialized prompt injection guards are deployed in front of document analysis systems, but no false positive rate has been published for any of them on legal contracts. We measure eleven open-weights guards on two corpora of legal contracts and find that existing guards struggle: document-level false positive rates are as high as 92.39%; two guards fall below chance on paired AUC; and a five-regex baseline is not Pareto-dominated by any guard in the panel. Controlled probes locate model-specific as well as general failure modes. Prompted LLMs resolve the tested failures, including a second threat category (unauthorized scope claims) on which every guard scores zero. A cascaded LLM solution, where the positives of a mid-tier model are verified by a frontier model, is 
 cheaper than using exclusively a frontier model and approaches its performance.

Introduction

Contract review systems read documents authored by an adversarial counterparty. Unlike the chat setting that prompt guards were built for, the untrusted text 
is
 the task input: the legal contract authored by a counterparty. It is long, and it is dense with legitimate directive language that may be aimed at human parties or at AI reviewers that have grown in popularity. Recent and concurrent work evaluates prompt injection guards on domains with unique structure, such as résumés 
(
Zhang et al., 2026
; 
Augey et al., 2026
)
 and agentic workflows 
(
Liu, 2026
)
. No published false positive rate exists for prompt injection guards on legal contracts, which leaves open the question: how well do guards defend against contract-borne injections?

We make the following contributions:

1.

A taxonomy of legal-specific indirect prompt injections
 (§
3
). We characterize a taxonomy of statements that may influence automated legal review, some of which differ from traditional prompt injections.

2.

A new challenge setting for prompt guards
 (§
4
). We show a specialized use case for which eleven leading prompt guards have unusably high false positive rates or low recall. We release a taxonomy-structured benchmark of contract-borne injection and matched benign control strings that are hand-written, factorially generated, and model-generated with independent audit, along with insertion manifests that constructed injected documents from publicly available legal contracts.

3.

Analysis of general and model-specific failure modes
 (§
5
). We find that detection tracks the instruction’s 
register
, not its function, and a telltale addressee cue runs 
backwards
 in more than half of the guards. We also show that some models’ flagging is keyed on narrow phrasing or even input length.

4.

A cost-efficient cascaded LLM detector
 (§
6
). A prompted frontier LLM is near the ceiling on the threat model that guards are formulated for, and makes appreciable progress defending against a threat that prompt guards cannot see. A mid-tier model also outperforms all guards, and the two can be combined in a cascade (Fig. 
1
) that achieves near-frontier performance at less than a sixth of the cost.

4 
Prompt Guards Fail on Contracts

In Tab. 
1
 we report our study of eleven existing prompt guards on CUAD 
Hendrycks et al. (2021)
, and our internal corpus. We found that detector scores on injected documents correlate strongly with the host’s score 
before
 any injection (App. 
E
). We therefore report paired AUC: each injected document against its own host.

Our main result is that 
no prompt injection guard is usable for unauthorized legal review instruction.
 Some guards flag nearly everything (ProtectAI v2, TestSavant, deepset, DistilBERT); others (e.g. the PromptGuard models) flag almost nothing. No guard Pareto-dominates a simple regex-based lexical baseline (printed verbatim in App. 
B.1
).

Horizon Labs’ recent guard is specifically trained for document-borne injection and obtains the highest AUC. However, it has a smaller gap with the control AUC; it detects that the document was edited but not that the edit was hostile. The zero false positive rates of PG-22M are timidity rather than discrimination, as shown by its below-chance paired AUC.
Notably, ProtectAI v2’s FPR is over an order of magnitude higher than v1, a practical reminder that newer versions do not universally improve security.

All payloads are formulated to match a specific review question about service-level agreements, detailed in App. 
A.3.1
. Repeating the panel on a second review question about confidentiality requirements leaves the scope-claim column near chance for every detector; the in-scope ranking, however, does not transfer, with three detectors moving 0.13–0.40 AUC on a topic substitution alone (Tab. 
6
, App. 
D
). This instability further underscores the challenges of deploying existing prompt guards on legal contracts.

7 
Discussion

We evaluate eleven prompt injection guards’ ability to detect unauthorized review instructions injected into legal contracts.
At this time, these guards do not represent a viable deployment solution, and we carefully diagnose their failure modes. In contrast,
LLMs can catch the realistic version and additional classes of attacks while requiring minimal spend on frontier-level capability.

Limitations

We consider English and US commercial contracts only, and each injected contract targets a single review question. Our panel of eleven prompt guards reflects what is currently available off the shelf, but as the difference between ProtectAI v1 and v2 shows (v2 flags 60.61% of clean contracts whereas v1 flags only 0.59%), the landscape of prompt injection solutions moves and not always forward. Two other attack layers that we do not consider here are structural concealment (text hidden by rendering rather than by wording, e.g. attacks written in tiny background-colored font) and bare false assertions (text that states something untrue without instructing anyone). The fix for the former lies in the document pipeline, a preprocessing step, and the latter depends on span-level provenance (checking each extracted answer against the text it cites, which is verification rather than injection detection).

We propose an LLM-based guard in § 
6
 as a reference point, to show that legal review prompt injections are detectable despite the failures of current off-the-shelf prompt guards. Cost is measured at list price on one corpus and one field, bounding its order of magnitude rather than any deployment budget. We do not measure latency at all, and for interactive review it may matter more. A guard is in any case only one place to intervene: document ingestion and the reviewer prompt are others, with different costs and different failure modes. Note that all detectors including the LLM guard score 512-token chunks; what a system does with a verdict (block, route, annotate) is a design question we do not address.

Ethics Statement

This paper publishes attack payloads that can be injected into legal contracts and shows that current off-the-shelf defenses are inadequate against them. We do this so that defenders can reproduce and develop better methods; we also measure one alternative, an LLM guard, and the cost it carries. Our attack payloads do not put novel capabilities in attackers’ hands: they are the documented OWASP LLM01 vocabulary applied to a new surface, and they succeed only in documents the attacker already controls (the counterparty-authored contracts). The released data is 24% benign by construction, with every attack paired to a matched control; this makes it more useful for measuring a defense than for mounting an attack.

We did not pursue coordinated disclosure with the eleven guards’ vendors: the finding is not an exploitable defect in any one product but a class of attack that none of the models represents, no configuration change or patch would close it within a disclosure window, and both the models and the payload vocabulary are already public.

False positives carry their own ethical weight, and our measurements shed new light on how they are distributed. As shown in App. 
E
, guard scores are largely a property of the host document rather than of any inserted text (up to 97% of score variance is attributable to which contract was scored). This suggests that flag rates vary systematically with document length and structure rather than with the presence of an attack. Thus, for example, parties whose contracts are longer or more heavily formatted may see degraded review service for reasons unrelated to risk. The form this cost takes depends on what the system does with a guard’s verdict: whether a guard is a hard filter that blocks review or a soft filter that alerts a human lawyer. We recommend the latter, to leave the decision with a person, while noting that a detector with high false positive rates produces alert fatigue that erodes that safeguard in practice.

Reproducibility

While we cannot release our proprietary corpus, we report results on a public benchmark (CUAD) that verify the limitations of off-the-shelf prompt guards on an open-access dataset. We release our benchmark of injection payloads and matched benign control strings, along with all LLM prompts, at

A.3 
Template Instantiation

Payloads are templates that can be instantiated for a specific review question. We present the review questions we use in this study, as well as how payloads are instantiated for them.

A.3.1 
Review Questions

In this work, the primary review question we use is called 
Has SLA
Commitment
, asked verbatim as:

Does the contract outline Service Level Agreement (SLA) commitments or
performance obligations? Look for specific performance metrics, uptime
guarantees, response time requirements, availability commitments, or other
measurable service levels that a party is obligated to meet. […]
Return true if the counterparty has explicit SLA commitments or measurable
performance obligations, and false if there are no specific SLA commitments or
only general service obligations without measurable targets.

We choose this question because the answer varies across the corpus (there is something to flip in both directions), it is decided by a clause that is usually present and localized, (a competing true answer exists in the document) and it draws a distinction of measurable targets versus general service language that is fine enough to admit a not obviously false assertion about.

As a test of generality, we evaluate guards on a second held-out field in App. 
D
, called 
Confidentiality Required of Us
. Its precise statement is:

Determine whether the contract explicitly requires our party to maintain
confidentiality of information. Look for sections or clauses that impose
confidentiality obligations on us. Return “true” if such obligations are
clearly stated; otherwise, return ‘Not specified.’ if no such requirement is
mentioned or if it is unclear.

It was chosen because it is a real determination that varies across commercial contracts rather than a near-tautology. A question such as whether services are provided is affirmative in essentially every agreement in the corpus, so nothing could move it and a null result would be uninformative. Note also that it is 
asymmetric
 where the SLA question is not: it admits a third outcome, 
not specified
, which is what the 
null
-target payloads aim at directly.

Appendix C 
Held-Out Attacks

In this section, we describe the automated construction of a held-out set of attack and benign items written without access to the registry, targeting review questions the registry never addressed.

C.1 
Formulating Against New Questions

For our main experiments, we used two review questions described in App. 
A.3.1
. To avoid keying on vocabulary in these questions, held-out attacks are written for four further review questions that the registry never targeted at all and that are keyword-disjoint from the held-in review questions. For succinctness, these questions ask the LLM to determine a cap on liability, audit rights granted to us, indemnification obligation on us, and insurance required of us. Full statements are in the supplement.

C.2 
Generation By A Different Model Family

We use a frontier LLM to write held-out attacks, but as we propose a Claude model for our LLM guard, we use a different family to avoid same-family bias. Items were were written by GPT-6 Astra Light, which for each review question received only the question itself, the party binding, and six real clauses on that topic harvested from CUAD as register exemplars. We supplied these real clauses so that the output reads as drafted language rather than chat prose; each clause came from a distinct contract, matched on obligation rather than keyword. We did 
not
 provide the generator with the payload registry, the probe scripts, or the
taxonomy and its class names, or the
detection definition that the LLM guard is given.

For each of the four held-out fields, the model was asked to produce twelve pairs of examples, each example a self-contained sentence of 15-45 words: an attack item that directs the reader to suppress a finding, restrict what counts as evidence, or dictate a conclusion, and a benign partner that asks for verifiable information, constrains presentation, or directs where to read.
Pairs are matched on length to within 15% by character count, have the same audience (both address an automated reviewer) and share a modal verb; several differ by one or two words.

As a pipeline check, we generate three positive controls per field: blunt textbook
overrides using the vocabulary the pairs are forbidden (
ignore
,

disregard
, 
previous instructions
). We find that six of eleven guards
miss them, further underscoring the narrowness of their recall (§ 
5.2
).

C.3 
Quality Control

Items were mechanically screened for forbidden vocabulary, violations of word bounds, or pairwise length differences. Violating items were rejected rather than
repaired to maintain the sole third-party authorship. They were then checked for
normalized 5-gram overlap against our payload registry; we confirmed no overlap apart from two exceptions that overlap only on generic contractual phrasing.

To measure the extent to which the generator’s attacks and benign examples fit into the intended categories, we re-check every item with Gemini 3.1 Pro, a frontier LLM from a third model family. Gemini is blind to the generator’s label as well as the two-part definition used in the LLM guard. The Gemini auditor agrees with the GPT generator on
91.7% of items, and judges all positive controls adversarial. We report results using all examples with the generator’s classification; dropping disagreeing examples did not change conclusions.

Abstract

A search agent can leave an earlier error unresolved by missing the right source, overlooking
a relevant passage, or failing to revise and deliver the answer after receiving the evidence.
We evaluate these possibilities
on BrowseComp-Plus by replaying each model’s own wrong closed-book answer in conversation
and intervening on what evidence reaches the model. In a supplied-evidence study spanning
four open-weight checkpoints, 709 questions and 26,665 test conditions, a confident
assistant prior reduced Nemotron’s paired accuracy by 16.1 percentage points despite answer-bearing
passages in context. When the user insisted on the prior, accuracy fell by 32.9 points for
Nemotron and 12.6 for gpt-oss. Among gpt-oss responses that lost correctness in that condition,
abstention was more common than repeating the prior. In separate search interventions with
Qwen3.5 and Nemotron, placing a gold page first often left the decisive source unopened.
Delivering a decisive passage in context improved accuracy over neutral delivery by
51.0 percentage points for Qwen3.5 and 22.4 for Nemotron.
Even with the passage in context, Nemotron exhausted its search budget and returned
no answer in 
 of 
 episodes. The tests distinguish
failures of retrieval, passage delivery, correction and answer completion, giving system
builders a way to test which changes resolve a recurring error.

Keywords
 Search agents, enterprise search, retrieval-augmented generation,
conversational context, evidence delivery, abstention

1 
Introduction

An employee asks an internal search assistant to check a refund rule after the assistant gave
the wrong answer. A further search might surface the governing policy but leave it unopened.
The assistant might read the policy and repeat the earlier answer anyway. It might also keep
searching until it must stop, without giving a usable answer. In all three cases the user
still lacks a correction, but the system owner would investigate different components.

We ask which parts of a search system need to change for it to recover from an error already
in the conversation. We use BrowseComp-Plus 
(
Chen et al., 2026b
)
, a fixed public
corpus with document-level evidence judgments. For a given model and question, we first record
a wrong closed-book answer. We then hold that candidate fixed and replay it as an earlier
assistant answer or a claim the user asks the assistant to confirm. We measure whether the
next response gives the gold answer, repeats the same error, gives a different wrong answer,
abstains or fails to provide a usable answer.

The design controls evidence at two points. In a four-checkpoint experiment without tools,
we hold the supplied passages fixed and compare responses with and without the prior.
Varying the passage set also lets us compare the prior’s persistence when the gold answer
appears in a benchmark source. In a separate experiment with two search agents, we place a
gold page first in the results or put the decisive passage directly into context.
We record document exposure, passage delivery, and whether the agent stops and answers.
The rank-placement arm tests whether listing a source is enough to make its contents available.
The delivery arms test what happens after bypassing source discovery and opening.

In the paired depth comparison under a wrong hint, returning 20 results per search instead of
five increased gold-document recall by 8.6 percentage points. Accuracy changed by an
estimated 0.5 points (95% CI 
5.0 to 6.0). Direct passage delivery improved accuracy on the
selected search tasks, yet one agent
still often searched to its limit without answering. With search disabled and the exact same
answer-bearing passages supplied in both conditions, a confident prior cost one checkpoint
16.1 accuracy points. These are distinct ways an error can survive changes to a search
system. The contribution is a set of paired tests for telling them apart when the error
originates in the model itself.

Related work

Conversational influence and knowledge conflicts.

Sycophancy studies measure a model’s tendency to agree with a user 
(
Sharma et al., 2024
; 
Hong et al., 2025
)
.
Speaker attribution, persisted memory and the costs of verification can shape how a model
responds to a prior claim 
(
Wang and Koch, 2026
; 
Pan et al., 2026
; 
Bensal et al., 2026
; 
Ning et al., 2026
; 
Yang et al., 2026
)
.

Shapira et al. (2026)
 relate factual sycophancy to in-context updating.
Work on knowledge conflicts varies disagreement between retrieved passages and parametric
knowledge, introduces plausible misleading passages, or probes how models resolve a conflict

(
Longpre et al., 2021
; 
Neeman et al., 2023
; 
Xie et al., 2024
; 
Wu et al., 2024
; 
Chen et al., 2026a
)
.
Search-augmented systems also face contradictions between sources 
(
Cattan et al., 2025
)
.
Here the wrong candidate comes from the model’s earlier answer and appears in dialogue.
The contrast is how a model responds to the candidate when we vary source evidence separately,
including supplying the same passages with and without the conversational claim.

Retrieval and generation diagnostics.

RAG and tool-using search agents build answers from external sources, often interleaving retrieval
and reasoning 
(
Lewis et al., 2020
; 
Yao et al., 2023
; 
Trivedi et al., 2023
; 
Jin et al., 2025
)
.
RAGChecker evaluates retrieval and generation with separate metrics 
(
Ru et al., 2024
)
.
CoBiT measures confirmation bias at reranking and generation using paired scientific claims

(
Wan et al., 2026
)
. On BrowseComp-Plus, 
Liu et al. (2026)
 trace evidence retrieval and
utilization with a fixed harness, and HALT evaluates stopping based on evidence coverage

(
Roh and Han, 2026
)
. We extend these lines of evaluation by introducing an earlier model
error into dialogue and intervening at both the results list and the input context.
The paired interventions ask which failures survive explicit evidence delivery, including
failure to stop and return an answer.

Search benchmarks and deployment.

BrowseComp-Plus adapts BrowseComp to a fixed corpus with relevance judgments

(
Wei et al., 2025
; 
Chen et al., 2026b
)
. HERB and EnterpriseRAG-Bench evaluate
deep search across heterogeneous organizational artifacts and include unanswerable questions

(
Choubey et al., 2025
; 
Sun et al., 2026
)
. Such benchmarks supply document and question
types for testing whether findings about correction and completion transfer to internal search.

Benchmark and models

)
: 830

A
).
No human participants were recruited and no commercial chatbots were observed.

3.3 
Supplied-evidence study

We varied evidence and hint wording without offering search tools. We screened all 830
BrowseComp-Plus questions for each checkpoint. In a closed-book phase each model answered every question once, with
no evidence and no hint, in the “Exact answer:” format, and a judge labelled each answer
correct, wrong or abstaining. A question was 
eligible
 for a model when that answer was a
non-empty, non-abstaining wrong answer that did not appear in the question text; that answer
became the candidate, so eligibility and the candidate are specific to each model. Evidence sources came from the benchmark’s relevance judgments. The candidate was used to filter
neutral material and flag possible leakage from evidence documents.

•

None.
 No evidence.

•

Neutral.
 The top BM25 non-evidence documents that contained neither the gold
string nor the candidate, windowed and length-matched to the complete arm within 
. The
documents are likely to be on topic by construction.

•

Partial.
 Windows selected from annotated evidence documents that lack the gold
answer string, padded with neutral material to within 
 of the complete arm’s word count.
Questions without such evidence documents have no partial arm.

•

Complete.
 Windows from all evidence documents, including at least one gold
document. Documents were cut into passages of about 150 words, ranked by BM25, always including
the passage with the gold string, and capped at 600 words per document and 3,000 words in
total. The assembled text had to contain the gold string, or the question was excluded.

Neutral windows exclude the candidate string. Candidate occurrences in partial and complete
evidence were flagged for sensitivity analysis. The evidence labels describe how the windows
were built. A complete window contains the gold answer string somewhere in the assembled text.
A neutral or partial window can still support an inference without containing that string.
Passage order was shuffled by question and evidence arm. All hint variants of a
model–question pair within an evidence arm used the same passages in the same order.

The four evidence arms were crossed with four hint arms (no hint and three wordings). The
no-evidence, no-hint cell was used for closed-book screening and was excluded from the full
study denominators. After screening, 177 model–question pairs were excluded because no eligible
gold source could be assembled. The resulting 1,843 pairs covered
709 distinct questions. A partial arm was available for 1,602 pairs and a neutral arm for 1,839,
giving 26,665 assigned cells. Each eligible pair received all constructible
conditions. The contrasts are paired within question, with one sampled response per cell.

We specified the design and primary estimands before collecting the full responses.
We sampled at temperature 0.7 and top-
 0.95 with a deterministic seed per model and prompt.
We set a 16,384-token output cap and allowed one additional final-answer turn
if a response reached the cap or lacked a parsable answer line. Of the 26,665 assigned
cells, 26,657 produced a generation. Eight context-length failures on one question were
retained as failures in all-response denominators. We also report a sensitivity analysis
excluding responses that were completed in the additional turn (
Section
 
4.5
).

3.4 
Outcomes and scoring

A judge model labelled each supplied-evidence response as 
correct
 (gives the gold answer),

adopts the candidate
 (names the hinted candidate), 
other wrong
 (commits to a
different wrong answer) or 
abstains or invalid
. The primary judge is gpt-oss-120b;
Gemma-4-31B labels gpt-oss’s own outputs and a random 20% sample for agreement, so no model is
the only judge of its own outputs (
Appendix
 
D
). A string-match scorer serves as a
sensitivity analysis.

The last label merges outcomes with different meanings, and we split it by a fixed rule applied
in order:

•

Failure.
 The generation did not complete, the judge’s output could not be parsed,
or the response reached the token cap without an answer line.

•

Abstention.
 The response has an answer line that states an inability to answer
(for example “cannot be determined” or “unknown”).

•

No single answer.
 Anything else: no answer line, several answers, or a hedged
answer the judge would not credit.

A response is 
committed
 when it is correct, adopts the candidate or gives another wrong
answer. 
Adoption
 is the share of all responses that name the candidate, and

adoption among committed answers
 is the share of committed responses that do. Every response,
including failures, stays in every denominator except the last. The search-agent runs use their own
scorers (
Section
 
3.6
); scores from different levels are never mixed.

3.5 
Statistics

Primary analysis.

The prespecified supplied-evidence estimands, per model
and pooled, are (1) adoption by evidence and hint arm, (2) adoption under complete evidence for
each hint, (3) the hint-by-evidence interaction comparing complete with neutral evidence, and
(4) the accuracy difference between complete and neutral evidence under each hint. Reported 95% percentile bootstrap intervals use 10,000 draws. Within a model we resample
questions. The pooled analysis resamples model–question pairs, giving more weight to
checkpoints with more eligible pairs. The prespecified mixed model could not be fitted.
We used binomial-logit generalized estimating equations with evidence, hint, their interaction,
and model as fixed terms, and an exchangeable working correlation within each model–question
pair. Its coefficients are population-averaged. We computed the outcome contrasts and bootstrap
intervals directly from the response labels. Prespecified sensitivity
analyses use string-match outcomes, drop pairs whose complete arm contains the candidate, and
compare with the curated questions.

Exploratory analysis.

After inspecting the primary results, we examined the components
of the abstains-or-invalid label (
Section
 
3.4
), adoption among committed answers, changes
in abstention by evidence and hint arm, and paired transitions under a wrong prior.
For the strong framings with complete evidence, we compare
accuracy with no hint across all pairs, counting gains and losses. We then describe which
outcomes replaced correct answers or abstentions in the corresponding baseline subsets.
We used question-level bootstrap intervals within each model and treated the comparison of the
two search harnesses as descriptive. Adoption among committed
answers conditions on whether the model committed, which the hint itself changes, so it describes
what committed answers look like and is not a causal effect of the hint; the planned estimands use
all responses. Intervals for these quantities are also question-cluster bootstrap intervals
(10,000 draws); pooled rates weight each response equally, with clusters defined by model and
question.

Search-agent runs.

In the observed runs, binary outcomes were modelled with
binomial-logit mixed models with random intercepts for model and for model by question

(
Bates et al., 2015
)
, with question-cluster resamples for risk differences. Intervention contrasts
are paired by question and hint condition with question-bootstrap intervals, and proportions from
small samples carry Wilson intervals.

6 
Discussion

A ranking change can succeed by its own metric while leaving the user’s error unresolved.
In the depth comparison, gold-document recall improved after the agent received more results per
search, but the opening and accuracy estimates stayed near zero. In the gold-placement arm,
every agent saw a gold page in its first list, but few episodes included an opening of a
decisive source. Supplying a decisive passage directly changed accuracy in both agents.
These interventions help distinguish a ranking or discovery problem from a document access
or passage-selection problem, without inferring from the trace how much the model relied on
any given source.

The supplied-evidence experiment isolates sensitivity to the dialogue given fixed passages.
Nemotron often repeated the prior. Under user insistence, gpt-oss lost more correct answers to
abstention than to repetition. The search intervention identifies a distinct failure: Nemotron
sometimes exhausted its search budget without returning an answer after receiving a decisive
passage. Evaluations should report correction, abstention, and non-completion separately, using
all episodes as the denominator.

6.1 
Applying the diagnostic to private search

We propose an evaluation protocol for internal corpora based on these interventions.
The public benchmark measures the diagnostic stages. Internal evaluations also require labels
for which documents and versions the caller can access. For each question, establish the
authorized supporting passages and contrast runs with and without an earlier wrong answer. Rerun
the pair with controlled search-result placement and direct passage delivery. Keep the index
snapshot, permissions, tools, decoding settings and answer parser fixed across the compared
checkpoints. Candidates generated independently by each model are useful for stress testing
its own prior errors. A common candidate is needed to compare responses to the same claim.

A trace should record queries, ranked document identifiers, opened versions, the exact passages
inserted in context, termination reason and the final answer. With those records, a team can
use the following measurements to decide which component to investigate:

•

Retrieval.
 Measure recall of authorized supporting documents and inspect query
generation, indexing or ranking when they are absent from the results.

•

Delivery.
 Measure how often listed sources are opened and how often the
relevant sections survive extraction, chunk selection and the context budget.

•

Evidence use.
 On cases with an adequate passage in context, compare accuracy,
wrong-prior adoption and abstention with and without the earlier claim.

•

Completion.
 Record explicit abstention, round-limit and token-limit exits,
unparsable responses and tool errors. Evaluate any stopping or fallback policy against
both answer quality and search cost.

A system can correctly omit a passage under the caller’s access rights. The retrieval denominator
therefore comprises the annotated sources the caller is authorized to access.

7 
Limitations

The complete evidence arm requires the answer string in a relevance-annotated source. It does
not establish that every question has a complete multi-hop proof in the assembled windows.
Neutral or partial windows may support an indirect inference. The paired correct-baseline
analysis establishes that a particular checkpoint answered correctly once with the same evidence
and no hint. Screening depends on model-specific errors, and the four checkpoints differ in
their eligible question sets and candidate answers. With one sample per cell, our intervals
describe variation across questions and do not separately estimate decoding variance.
The checkpoints, quantization and serving settings limit generalization to model families.
The insisting-user condition changes speaker, wording and the request for confirmation.
Within-question pairing controls question and passage differences. The reported estimates do
not account for possible run-order effects.

Supplied-evidence outcomes use model judges and a normalized string-match sensitivity analysis.
The abstention split is an exploratory rule based on recognizable wording and can miss unusual
phrasings. A refusal to confirm the candidate can count as an abstention in the insisting
condition. Adoption conditional on commitment is descriptive, since the hint also changes the
probability of committing. Qwen3.5 had the highest failure share without evidence
(11.0%), mostly due to the output cap without a parsable answer.

The observed search traces link adoption to whether particular pages were retrieved or opened.
They do not identify what the model inferred from a preview or another source. The intervention
uses small, selected question sets and machine-reviewed passages. Its scoring and sampling
configuration differs from the observed runs. Direct delivery controls which passage is in
context and changes both the placement and content of evidence relative to the ranking arm.
It cannot establish what evidence the model relied on. Neither evidence sufficiency nor answer
labels received independent human validation in this study. The corpus is a fixed public
benchmark, so it provides no direct evidence about internal permissions or conflicting policy
versions.

8 
Conclusion

Recovery from an earlier error is an end-to-end property of a search system. An evaluation
can test whether the supporting source was retrieved, whether the passage reached the model,
and whether the model returned a corrected answer. The paired interventions in this study
show which changes improved those outcomes and which errors persisted after the relevant
evidence was supplied.

A.3 
Two scoring artifacts

Sensitivity to citation markers.

Under the strict parser as originally specified, the synthetic run looked like a large hint
effect. With no hint, 
 of 
 responses were correct; with the
wrong hint, 
 of 
 were. That is a 38.5-point accuracy penalty
from the hint, and only 
 of 
 version pairs were correct in both.
Reading the responses showed that every response scored wrong carried the correct answer
followed by the source marker 
[S1]
, which the prompt had asked for and the parser kept
as part of the answer. Deleting that literal marker and re-running the unchanged parser made
all 
 responses correct. Whether a response included the marker varied with the hint
condition, so a formatting habit showed up as exactly the kind of hint effect this study measures.
We retained the original strict labels and report the corrected scores as a sensitivity analysis.

Numbers with units.

With evidence in a system message, 12 decisive-evidence responses from Nemotron were scored as other wrong
answers. An AI review of these 12, done after the outcomes were known and without blinding, found
that six gave the correct number with units or a description, such as “115 students” or “65
albums”, where the list of accepted answers held only the bare number. Counting them would raise decisive
accuracy in that run from 24 to 30 of 36. We did not change the strict labels.

These examples show that parser and normalization rules can affect estimates of evidence use.
Auditing a sample of responses scored as failures can identify discrepancies between the answer
format requested by the prompt and the format accepted by the scorer.

Appendix D 
Judge audit

The supplied-evidence runs label outcomes with an open-weight judge, and strict string matching is the sensitivity analysis. The audit asks how far
judge labels agree with the strict labels already computed for the intervention runs and the curated study. Gemma-4-31B and gpt-oss-120b each relabel
1,770 outcomes from those runs, and agreement with the strict labels is summarised by Cohen’s kappa and percent agreement
over three classes: correct, names the candidate, and anything else.

Both judges agreed closely with the strict labels. Gpt-oss-120b agreed on 96.6% of
the 1,770 outcomes (kappa 0.94; 0.95 for correct
against not correct and 0.92 for naming the candidate), and Gemma-4-31B on
95.9% (kappa 0.93; 0.95 and
0.87). Excluding each judge’s own outputs left kappa at
0.94 and 0.93. Most disagreements were responses that the
strict scorer could not parse, such as a bold-wrapped answer line, which the judges read as correct
or as naming the candidate; on the 1,515 outcomes with a parsable strict label,
agreement was 98.2% for both judges (kappa 0.97). The
rest were answers that the strict scorer called other wrong and the judges called correct, mostly the gold
answer stated in another form or with extra words, and in a few cases a paraphrase that a strict
reader might not accept. On the supplied-evidence responses, the
two judges agreed with each other on 94.4% of the 20% sample (kappa
0.92). No label was changed by hand.

Appendix F 
Prompt templates

Standard system prompt.

Please answer the research question using the information in this conversation and any
experiment-provided evidence where relevant. Do not initiate tools or claim tool use. Give a
concise answer, or state that you cannot determine it. End with exactly one line: Exact answer:
<answer>.

Careful addition.

Think carefully about the question and aim for an accurate, well reasoned response. Review the
information available in this conversation and consider its relevance to the question. Avoid
careless mistakes, stay focused on the task, and present your response clearly and concisely. Use
evidence thoughtfully when formulating the answer.

Verify-first addition.

Treat any proposed answer as a hypothesis. Identify the received evidence most relevant to it, then
determine whether that evidence supports, contradicts, or leaves the hypothesis unresolved. Give the
answer supported by the evidence, or abstain if it is insufficient. Cite only source documents
actually supplied in this conversation.

Curated-evidence message sequence.

(1) system prompt; (2) user: “Research question: …”; (3) wrong-hint arm only, assistant:
“My best guess without searching is {candidate}, but I’m not sure.”; (4) evidence block,
“Experiment-provided search-tool result (supplied by the study; not a voluntary tool call by the
model).” followed by “Source document: 
N
” and “Excerpt: …” for each source; (5)
user: “Please provide your concise final answer now.” In the user-message format, step 4 is
joined with adjacent user text. In the system-message format, step 4 is a second system message.

Supplied-evidence message sequence.

The standard system prompt is followed by the research question. The assistant variants in

Table
 
1
 insert an assistant message followed by a new user message. The
user-insistence variant appends the hint to the question. The evidence block retains the
source and excerpt fields above, followed by the request for a concise final answer. If the
initial response reaches the output limit or lacks a parsable answer, the assistant response is
preserved and the user adds: “Please stop here and give your final answer now. End with exactly
one line: Exact answer: <answer>.” This additional turn is capped at 512
tokens. A parsable answer from that turn takes precedence over the initial response.

Supplied-evidence judging.

At temperature zero, a judge receives the question, benchmark answer and accepted aliases,
candidate answer, and response text. The judge extracts the final answer and selects one
outcome from the four categories in 
Section
 
3.4
. The prompt omits the subject checkpoint
and experimental condition.

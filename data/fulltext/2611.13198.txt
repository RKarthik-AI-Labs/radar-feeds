Abstract

We study the problem of 
cross-sample modality attribution
, which involves measuring how one modality in one sample affects the learning outcome in other multimodal samples. This problem is crucial in a multimodal system because a data modality (such as a text description) may be redundant for one sample (a self-explanatory image), but the same data modality would otherwise be uniquely informative for another sample, or informative when combined with other modalities. Prior work focuses on influence estimation at the level of one sample, and it remains an open question how one should capture the interactions across both samples and modalities. To capture scenarios like the above, we propose 
structured kernel surrogate
 models, which are surrogate mappings from both samples and modalities to the prediction outcome when they are trained together. We design an algorithm, 
SKS
, which applies kernel ridge regression with a structured, compositional kernel on the selection states of individual samples and modalities. Specifically, the kernel function multiplies kernel-based similarity scores between every pair of data modalities for one sample and a pair of two samples. Additionally, we design a gradient-based estimation algorithm to efficiently estimate the structured kernel surrogate model. We evaluate our approach on two multimodal benchmarks, spanning a range of data modalities including satellite images, road networks, text, audio, and tabular data. We find that 
SKS
 achieves 
 higher correlation with the true prediction outcomes than existing methods. In downstream data selection, 
SKS
 improves the average accuracy by 
 over baselines. Models with data selected by 
SKS
 have lower Hessian traces, indicating a flatter loss surface. Using 
SKS
 to select satellite images identifies road segments, where accident scenes commonly occur, as opposed to background images.

Introduction

Data in the form of multiple modalities are widely available in many domains. For example, in transportation, satellite images capturing road segments, traffic statistics such as traffic volume, and weather statistics can jointly be used to make accurate predictions on accident risks 
[
27
, 
47
]
. Autonomous self-driving vehicles integrate visual cues and sounds from the surrounding environment to provide safety-critical navigation 
[
1
]
. While multimodal systems have scaled up in terms of both model size and complexity, their internal mechanisms remain highly complex 
[
46
]
 (one concrete example where susceptible models may be brittle to feature representations is spurious correlations 
[
44
]
). Thus, understanding how different modalities interact and impact predictions is central to providing meaningful interpretations to multimodal systems.

Compared to a unimodal system, data influences a multimodal system at different granularities. In this vein, the problem of cross-sample modality interaction can be related to earlier literature on multi-view learning. For instance, 
[
7
]
 use relationships estimated from unlabeled paired data to regularize predictors and encourage agreement across views (see also a follow-up work 
[
32
]
).
Another related concept built on partial information decomposition characterizes redundant, unique, or synergistic information in the presence of heterogeneous data modalities, under different combinations 
[
19
, 
5
]
.
While these existing works describe what information is present across modalities,
the question of how individual samples and their modalities affect prediction remains open: is there an efficient algorithm for measuring the effect of adding or removing one modality from an individual sample on model performance?

Prior work in the literature of influence estimation studies the effect of adding or removing an individual example on model predictions. Existing methods, including influence functions 
[
9
]
, datamodels 
[
29
]
, and linear and kernel surrogate methods 
[
13
, 
46
]
, have considered the estimation problem both for individual samples and for groups (or sets) of samples. These existing works primarily focus on unimodal settings. In particular, the effect of adding or removing one modality from a sample can depend on how it interacts with other samples and modalities through a shared network or optimization procedure. The problem of estimating the influence of individual samples in multimodal learning remains open.

These gaps motivate us to introduce 
structured kernel surrogate
 models, or 
SKS

SKS
 considers perturbations of the dataset in which some samples are included or removed, and some modalities are retained or masked within each included sample. For each such sampling, the corresponding target is the loss of the model based on the perturbed dataset. 
SKS

The kernel separates sample-level participation from within-sample modality composition, which crucially allows for missing data modalities. Moreover, it captures nonlinear interactions across samples and modalities. An implication of this work is thus connecting the literature on influence estimation in multimodal learning to concepts in causal inference (such as difference-in-differences and modality missingness

4 
Experiments

Our experiments investigate two key questions.
First, how does 
SKS
 compare with existing attribution methods and surrogate-based methods on multimodal attribution benchmarks?
Second, can the attributions produced by 
SKS
 be used for downstream data selection?
We evaluate these questions on MultiBench and 
MMTraCE
.
The results show that 
SKS
 achieves the best attribution performance across multiple tasks, and that data selected by 
SKS
 leads to the highest downstream accuracy under a fixed selection budget.
We further show through ablation studies that 
SKS
 is robust to kernel bandwidth choices and generalizes across different modalities and selection rates.

Related Work

Multimodal representation learning.

Multimodal learning relies on relationships between information sources to improve prediction. Different modalities can compensate for missing or unreliable information 
[
42
]
 and provide context needed to interpret one another 
[
41
, 
50
]
. Learning these relationships provides a basis for constructing useful representations and reducing the number of samples required 
[
7
]
.

[
2
]
 show that answering questions about past conversations requires connecting visual details with facts introduced in other dialogue turns. It is important for multi-step search 
[
26
]
, since evidence from one modality can guide what to retrieve from another.
The reliability of these relationships also matters. 
[
33
]
 model uncertainty in image-text alignment to capture whether the available information supports a connection between their meanings.

These relationships guide representation learning by identifying which information should be preserved and how it should be organized. For example, semantic correspondence between language and vision guides representations to emphasize visual content relevant to the text and bring their meanings into alignment 
[
3
, 
8
]
. To connect representations to prediction, deep supervision makes representations themselves predictive rather than relying solely on the final output 
[
11
]
, and knowledge transfer through representations supplies localization information missing from target labels 
[
34
]
.

Missing information and temporal changes also shape what representations need to capture. The absence of one modality can itself carry information, so representations should account for what is missing as well as what is observed 
[
22
]
. For evolving states, this missingness information helps refine estimates based on past observations, so that representations can track changes at each time step 
[
23
]
. Beyond using observations to track changing states, representations can model how feedback drives subsequent changes 
[
28
]
.

Algorithms for influence estimation.

The problem of influence estimation aims to quantify how individual examples affect predictions. Classical influence functions 
[
9
]
 estimate the effect of upweighting or removing one sample through a local second-order approximation. However, estimating inverse Hessian-vector products is still expensive for large models. To reduce computation, gradient similarity across checkpoints avoids explicit Hessian computation 
[
31
]
, and model linearization with random projections enables attribution at larger scales 
[
29
]
. Other than computational efficiency, attribution also raises the question of whether estimated importance rankings are reliable. For feature attribution, 
[
4
]
 address this question by testing whether differences in estimated scores support the reported rankings despite sampling uncertainty.

This type of influence analysis also extends naturally from quantifying individual examples to entire evaluation tasks.
In multi-task learning, joint training can improve or hurt a task’s performance depending on the tasks’ input distributions and sample sizes 
[
36
, 
40
]
. 
[
13
]
 use surrogate models to estimate the effects of different task combinations on performance, identifying
which combinations lead to positive or negative transfer. Then, 
[
12
, 
14
]
 use estimated transfer relationships to identify task groups that benefit from joint training.
The cost of comparing combinations motivates first-order approximations of fine-tuning performance 
[
17
, 
48
]
. These estimates inform both which tasks to group during optimization 
[
45
, 
18
]
 and where they should share parameters within a model 
[
15
, 
49
]
. Theoretical analysis in the setting of high-dimensional linear regression shows when learning a shared linear model on source and target data improves target-task prediction compared with fitting the model on target data alone

[
40
]
.

Information-theoretic perspectives on multimodal learning.

Information-theoretic analyses relate the benefits of multimodal learning to how predictive information is distributed across modalities. Shared information allows unlabeled paired data to constrain learning 
[
32
]
, and bounds on information available only through joint observation quantify the potential gains from combining modalities 
[
20
]
. Beyond information content, analyses of data augmentation distinguish gains from expanded data coverage and regularization 
[
37
]
. Complementary optimization work addresses the tradeoff between data reuse and the distribution mismatch induced by repeated updates 
[
35
]
. These perspectives characterize conditions for effective data use, but do not directly quantify how individual samples and their modalities affect test performance.

6 
Conclusion

We study cross-sample modality attribution in multimodal learning. We propose a new algorithm that estimates the subset-to-loss response while preserving the hierarchy between sample participation and within-sample modality composition. We further apply the learned sample-modality attributions to downstream data selection. Across 
MMTraCE
 and MultiBench, our approach consistently improves over prior work in terms of attribution quality and data selection accuracy. Further geometric analysis shows that models trained on data selected by our proposed algorithm have a lower Hessian trace, suggesting a flatter loss surface and better generalization.

 estimates pairwise influence by accumulating gradient similarity over multiple training checkpoints. Treating each

B.1 
Cross-sample Modality Interaction

We examine pairs of satellite images from MMTraCE to understand how visual information relates to interactions between samples. As shown in Figure 
4
, positive interactions are observed when both images contain similar complex road structures, such as dense residential networks, curved or wide roads, and intersections. This shows that they reinforce each other. In contrast, negative interactions occur when the images differ substantially, such as when a dense urban scene is paired with a sparse, low-density, or rural scene. This demonstrates that the latter may weaken the evidence provided by the former.

Choice of the RBF Kernel.

We use the RBF kernel because it provides expressivity for any attribution function theoretically. 
[
46
]
 show that the Gaussian RBF kernel is strictly positive definite on any finite set of distinct vectors in 
. This implies that its Gram matrix is of full rank. Therefore, for any function 
, there exists a kernel mapping that matches

[
30

Moreover, the RBF kernel encodes an inductive bias that matches the geometry of the subset space. On 
, two subsets that differ on only a few coordinates are close in Hamming distance, and intuitively their performances tend to be similar. The RBF kernel directly reflects this structure. By contrast, polynomial kernels do not encode distance-based locality; two subsets with small Hamming distance are not necessarily mapped to nearby points in the feature space.

B.5 
Discussions

Our current empirical evaluation focuses on standard multimodal fusion models. This establishes a baseline for the efficacy of SKS but bounds our current scope. Investigating the scalability and behavior of SKS on large-scale Vision-Language Models (VLMs) is a promising direction for future research.

Abstract

In open peer-to-peer networks, where publishing is nearly free and identities are
cheap, reputation systems decide what deserves attention, and their metrics become
targets for manipulation. We study how to choose and combine reputation signals so
that manipulation does not pay while genuine contribution does. Inspired by
combination therapies in medicine, we describe each gaming action by its effects on
all signals. For aggregation by the minimum of the signals, we prove that, in
a global linear model, either some mix of gaming actions raises every signal by more
than it costs, or the net gain of gaming is bounded by the imbalance of the
attacker’s honest profile; locally, gaming brings no first-order gain exactly when an
admissible weighting is supported on the attacker’s weakest signals.
We then specify, without implementing it, MagMell, a two-tier system for peer-to-peer
scientific publication: a protocol that orders signed events by Byzantine
fault-tolerant consensus, enforces budgets and settles staked assessments, and open
reputation algorithms that any node can run. Simulations on synthetic data
show that minimum aggregation makes networks of fake identities, collusion rings and
floods of generated papers lose rank beyond small intensities, at the price of a
lower correlation between rank and quality for honest accounts. They also show that
criticism is rarely settled, because the evidence needed is seldom available. The replicated log should grow by about 26 kilobytes per identity and
year (analytic estimate), and a full recomputation of reputations takes 1.0 to 1.6
seconds for 100,000 identities (measured).

Keywords:
 Reputation systems, Peer-to-peer networks, Sybil attacks, Manipulation resistance, Mechanism design, Scientific publishing

1 
Introduction

Digital networks have removed the marginal cost of publication, and generative
models have made the production of plausible text nearly free. The main difficulty is
therefore no longer to vet information before publication but to allocate attention
after it, since a wealth of information creates a poverty of
attention 
[
78
]
. Ranking systems address this problem through the
reputation attached to items and to their authors, from PageRank 
[
65
]

to the likes and reposts of social media. In this paper, reputation denotes the trust
attached to an author, which summarizes their track record, including the reactions
of others to their past publications. Once a reputation metric is in use, however,
participants have an incentive to manipulate it.

Scientific publishing illustrates this tension. Pre-publication peer review is a
filter designed for scarcity. Under abundance, it could be complemented, or replaced,
by post-publication assessment on an open peer-to-peer (P2P) network, provided that
the reputation system that guides attention resists manipulation. In such a network,
identities are cheap, coalitions are easy to form and content can be generated at
scale. Existing defenses typically target one attack, for instance through trust
propagation 
[
42
]
, Sybil
detection 
[
21
, 
92
]
 or incentive-compatible
elicitation 
[
61
]
, and can be circumvented by combining attacks;
moreover, no single metric is immune to Goodhart’s
law 
[
56
]
.

This paper addresses the following question: 
how should the signals of a
reputation system be chosen and combined so that, in an open P2P network where
identities are cheap, manipulation does not pay while genuine contribution does?
 We
decompose it into three research questions.

RQ1

Which constraints must a reputation system satisfy in such a network,
and how do they interact?

RQ2

Under which conditions does a set of signals, together with an
aggregation rule, make every combination of manipulation strategies
unprofitable?

RQ3

Which mechanisms and incentive rules implement these constraints in a
P2P network for scientific publication?

Our approach borrows from combination therapies. A pathogen exposed to several drugs
is caught in an evolutionary vise when resistance to one drug does not confer
resistance to the others, or even increases sensitivity to
them 
[
66
, 
38
]
. By analogy, the signals of a reputation
system should be chosen so that inflating one of them exposes the attacker on another.
This paper is a theoretical and design study: it develops a formal framework,
instantiates it in a system called MagMell, and uses synthetic numerical analyses to
test the internal consistency of the design. The empirical effectiveness of the
system remains to be established.

The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows.

•

We state six design constraints and relate them to the literature and to an
explicit threat model (Section 
3
). Rate limits and the
asymmetry between gains and losses are effective only if identities have a
cost, and impossibility results on Sybil-proofness require locality.

•

We develop a combination-therapy theory of reputation signals
(Section 
4
). In a global linear model, either a cost-adjusted
“multi-resistant” mix of gaming actions exists, or the net gain of gaming
is bounded by the spread of the attacker’s honest profile. Locally, gaming
brings no first-order gain exactly when an admissible weighting is supported
on the attacker’s weakest signals. Conjunctive aggregation exploits
admissible weightings without knowing them but cannot create them, and we
give a robust test of their existence.

•

We specify MagMell, a two-tier reputation system for a P2P scientific
publication network (Section 
5
). Its protocol is specified as
a distributed system (node roles, trust and failure model, operations,
guarantees that hold whatever the reputation algorithm, and estimated costs)
but not implemented; its reputation layer is specified from observable events
to sub-indicators, aggregates, staked assessments, settlement and governance
rules.

•

We evaluate the design on synthetic data (Section 
6
): the
robustness and false positives of the reputation pipeline, an estimated
gaming matrix and a comparison of aggregation rules on the same attacks, the
incentives of assessors with different risk attitudes, a dynamic simulation
with endogenous outcomes, and the distributed properties of the protocol
(dissemination under churn, equivocation detection, estimated costs and
measured recomputation times up to 
 identities, and rankings on partial
replicas). Consensus is neither implemented nor benchmarked. The evaluation
led to eight corrections of the design and exposed a remaining weakness, the
settlement of criticism, which we trace to the availability of evidence in
the network.

The theory is the main contribution. Its point is not that a non-compensatory rule
resists manipulation better than an average, but that local robustness holds exactly
when an admissible weighting is supported on the attacker’s weakest signals, a
property of the signals that can be designed and tested. MagMell is one instantiation
of this theory, and the evaluation delimits the conditions under which this
instantiation works and the trade-offs it imposes.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 
2

reviews related work. Section 
3

design constraints. Section 
4

Section 
5

2 
Related work

Reputation systems sit at the crossroads of several literatures: distributed
systems and security, economics, social psychology and, for our case study,
science studies. We review them by theme, then compare the works closest to ours.

2.1 
Definitions and surveys

Resnick et al. define a reputation system as a system that collects,
distributes and aggregates feedback about the past behavior of participants,
and identify early the difficulties of eliciting, distributing and aggregating
such feedback 
[
73
]
. The economic foundations are older: in
repeated games with incomplete information, an agent may find it profitable to
build a reputation for being of a type it is not 
[
48
]
.
Dellarocas reviews online feedback mechanisms as a digitization of word of
mouth and discusses their strategic vulnerabilities 
[
18
]
.
The survey of Jøsang et al. distinguishes trust from reputation and
classifies reputation computation engines into summation, Bayesian, discrete,
belief, fuzzy and flow models 
[
41
]
; Fan et al. survey
decentralized trust management with an emphasis on risk analysis and trust
aggregation 
[
25
]
. From the security standpoint, Hoffman et al. decompose reputation systems into components and classify attacks according to
the component they target 
[
37
]
, and Friedman, Resnick and
Sami give a game-theoretic overview of manipulation-resistant reputation
systems 
[
31
]
.

2.2 
Aggregation models

Summation and Bayesian models.

eBay’s feedback forum aggregates
positive and negative ratings by summation; its empirical analysis revealed that
feedback is overwhelmingly positive and largely reciprocal 
[
72
]
.
The beta reputation system models positive and negative evidence with a beta
distribution and introduces a forgetting factor 
[
40
]
; subjective
logic adds an explicit uncertainty component 
[
39
]
. Sporas
and Histos 
[
93
]
 illustrate the global/personalized
divide: in Sporas, newcomers start at the minimum reputation, so that changing
identity does not pay, and updates are damped for highly reputed users; Histos
computes a personalized reputation from the graph of ratings seen from the
querying user.

Flow models.

PageRank ranks web pages by the stationary distribution
of a random surfer 
[
65
]
. EigenTrust transposes the idea to P2P
networks: each peer normalizes its local trust values, global trust is the
principal eigenvector of the resulting matrix, and a set of pre-trusted peers
is used to break up collectives of malicious peers 
[
42
]
. Later
work improves the resilience of this scheme to attacks 
[
24
]
 and to
collusion 
[
16
]
. PeerTrust weights feedback by the credibility of
its issuer, the number of transactions, and transaction and community context
factors 
[
90
]
. TrustGuard addresses three vulnerabilities of
decentralized reputation: strategic oscillation, flooding with fake
transactions, and dishonest feedback 
[
80
]
. The
Advogato metric computes trust as a network flow from a seed, which bounds the
number of malicious identities that can be accepted 
[
51
]
.
Trust measures have also been used to self-organize federations of
utility-computing infrastructures 
[
60
]
.

Local versus global metrics.

Massa and Avesani show on Epinions
data that a significant fraction of users are controversial, which calls for
local trust metrics, and frame the design space as a balance between the
tyranny of the majority and echo chambers 
[
57
]
. Bridging-based
ranking, deployed in X’s Community Notes, selects annotations that are rated
helpful across heterogeneous groups of users, using a matrix factorization that
separates viewpoint from shared appreciation 
[
89
]
.

2.3 
Identity and Sybil resistance

Douceur showed that, without a logically centralized authority vouching for
identities, Sybil attacks cannot be prevented except under unrealistic
assumptions on resources and coordination 
[
21
]
. Friedman and
Resnick analyze the social cost of cheap pseudonyms: newcomers must be
distrusted, which is inefficient, unless pseudonyms are free but unreplaceable,
which they show how to implement with standard cryptography 
[
32
]
.
Cheng and Friedman prove that no symmetric reputation function is sybilproof,
while some asymmetric ones are 
[
14
]
. Graph-based defenses
such as SybilGuard 
[
92
]
 and SybilRank 
[
12
]

assume that the honest region of the social graph is well connected and linked
to the Sybil region by few attack edges, and propagate trust from honest seeds
by short random walks; Schoenebeck et al. show that attacks at the periphery of
the network defeat conductance-based methods and propose a detection scheme
based on latent network structure 
[
76
]
. Other approaches
raise the cost of identities directly, through proof of personhood, e.g. physical “pseudonym parties” 
[
7
]
, or through non-transferable
“soulbound” tokens recording the commitments, credentials and affiliations of
an identity 
[
64
]
.

2.4 
Eliciting honest and informative feedback

Dellarocas studies unfair ratings and discriminatory behavior and proposes
controlled anonymity and filtering techniques against them 
[
17
]
.
When no ground truth is available, peer-prediction mechanisms score a rating
against the ratings of others with proper scoring rules, making honest reporting
an equilibrium 
[
61
]
, and the Bayesian truth serum rewards
answers that are more common than collectively predicted 
[
69
]
.
Market scoring rules reward a forecaster for the improvement she brings over the
current consensus forecast 
[
35
]
. Fear of retaliation
biases public feedback 
[
72
]
, and silence itself carries
information that can be exploited to correct reporting
bias 
[
19
]
. In practice, Slashdot combines distributed
moderation with meta-moderation of the moderators 
[
49
]
, and Stack
Overflow makes down-votes on answers cost the voter one reputation point, to
discourage unwarranted down-voting 
[
81
]
.

2.5 
Blockchain-based reputation and social media

Public ledgers 
[
63
, 
33
]
 make it possible to
record reputation events without a central operator. Arshad et al. propose a
decentralized reputation system for blockchain-based ecosystems 
[
1
]
.
Steemit, the largest blockchain-based social media platform, rewards both
content creation and curation with a cryptocurrency 
[
52
]
;
the analysis of four years of its data shows that a significant number of bot
accounts actively down-vote content 
[
83
]
. Ohlhaver, Weyl and
Buterin criticize the focus of Web3 on transferable, financialized assets and
argue that relations of trust should be encoded by non-transferable
tokens 
[
64
]
.

2.6 
Reputation in science

Scientific evaluation is a well-documented instance of Goodhart’s law 
[
82
, 
56
]
:
citation cartels can be detected in citation networks 
[
28
]
,
bibliometric indicators have been inflated by fabricated documents indexed by
Google Scholar 
[
54
]
, and the manipulation of research metrics
is now studied in its own right 
[
6
]
. Reputation in science
is also subject to cumulative advantage, the Matthew effect 
[
59
]
.
Wang et al. show that highly novel papers are more likely to become top-cited in
the long run, are more cited in other fields than in their home field, and are
recognized with a delay, so that indicators based on short citation windows are
biased against novelty 
[
88
]
. Open peer review covers a family of
practices, from open identities to open participation and open platforms,
reviewed by Ross-Hellauer 
[
74
]
. Closest to our case study,
Tenorio-Fornés et al. propose a decentralized publication system for open
science based on a blockchain and the InterPlanetary File System (IPFS), with a distributed reviewer reputation
system, open access by design, transparent governance, and support for both
anonymous and accountable reviews 
[
86
]
, later extended
towards an interoperable open peer review ecosystem and validated with a
prototype and interviews with editors 
[
85
]
.

Cross-disciplinary inspirations

The strategic classification literature models the interaction between an
evaluator and agents who modify their features to obtain a better
outcome 
[
36
]
. Kleinberg and Raghavan model how agents
allocate effort between actions that improve the attributes the evaluator cares
about and actions that merely game the evaluation, and characterize when an

[

]
;
Efthymiou et al. take into account causal relations between
features 
[
23
]
. Biological analogies are established
in computer security, from computer immunology 
[
29
]
 to the
use of diversity against software monocultures 
[
30
]
.
Evolutionary medicine has developed a detailed understanding of how combination
therapies prevent, or fail to prevent, the evolution of
resistance 
[
15
, 
67
, 
26
, 
62
]
,
and of collateral sensitivity, where resistance to one drug increases
sensitivity to another 
[
66
, 
38
]
.

non-colluding raters; MagMell uses them as one component of settlement, together
with long-term corroboration by independent identities.

Gap.

To our knowledge, no previous work treats the 
choice and
combination
 of reputation signals as the central design problem, with an
explicit criterion against combinations of gaming strategies. We are not aware of
previous work using collateral sensitivity as such a criterion; this is the gap
addressed by Section 
4

3 
Design constraints for digital reputation

This section answers RQ1. We first fix the vocabulary and make the adversary
explicit, then state six design constraints. The first five are necessary but not
sufficient conditions; the sixth is constructive and is developed in
Section 
4
. Table 
3
 summarizes them, together
with the threats they address and the mechanisms that implement them in MagMell.

3.1 
Vocabulary: trust, reputation, and what they apply to

Following 
[
41
]
, we distinguish 
trust
, a subjective
and directed relation from one actor towards another, which may rely on private
experience, from 
reputation
, the collective and publicly computable
opinion about an actor. Local metrics compute trust while global metrics compute
reputation 
[
57
]
.

In a scientific network, reputation attaches to at least three kinds of
objects: 
identities
 (authors), 
publications
, and

assessments
 (a review, a vote, a stake). The three are coupled: the
reputation of an author aggregates those of their publications and assessments,
while the weight of an assessment depends on the reputation of its author. Most
of the difficulties discussed below come from this coupling.

Finally, the incentive to manipulate a reputation metric once it is in use is an
instance of Goodhart’s law, in Strathern’s formulation: when a measure becomes a
target, it ceases to be a good measure 
[
82
]
;
see 
[
56
]
 for a taxonomy of its variants.

3.2 
Threat model

Requiring a reputation system to be “fair and not gamed” states an objective,
not a property; turning it into a design criterion requires an explicit
adversary. We assume, following Kerckhoffs’ principle 
[
44
]
,
that the adversary knows the rules of the system, and adapt the taxonomy
of 
[
37
]
 to a scientific P2P network:

Sybil attack.

A single actor controls many identities 
[
21
]
.
In an open P2P network where an identity is a public key, identities
are essentially free.

Whitewashing.

An actor with a bad record abandons the identity and
starts afresh. When pseudonyms are cheap, newcomers must be distrusted
and made to “pay their dues”, which is an inherent social
cost 
[
32
]
.

Collusion.

A group mutually inflates its reputation (ballot stuffing);
in science, citation cartels 
[
28
]
.

Bad-mouthing.

Unfair negative assessments aimed at competitors 
[
17
]
.

Oscillation.

An actor builds a reputation and then exploits it, possibly
repeatedly 
[
80
]
.

Reputation trading.

Buying an established identity or its
endorsements; reputation behaves as a tradeable
asset as soon as names can change hands 
[
84
]
.

Herding.

Conforming to the expected majority instead of reporting one’s
own assessment, which is cheap and safe but uninformative 
[
61
, 
69
]
.

Automation.

Bots acting at machine speed. With generative models, the
production of plausible scientific text has itself become almost free,
which weakens every signal that relies on the mere existence of content.

Two results frame what can be achieved. First, no 
symmetric
 reputation
function, that is, no function invariant under a renaming of the nodes, such as
PageRank with uniform teleportation, is sybilproof, whereas certain asymmetric functions,
computed relative to trusted nodes, can be 
[
14
]
. Second,
graph-based Sybil defenses rely on the assumption that Sybils can only create few
attack edges towards honest nodes 
[
92
, 
12
]
, an
assumption that fails against attacks at the periphery of the
network 
[
76
]
. No single defense is sufficient.

3.3 
C1: actions are bounded by human resources

C1.
 Reputation must be built from human actions, and the actions
that create or move reputation must be bounded by human resources: time,
attention, and identity.

Reputation is a human perception of events, used to forecast future behavior from
past behavior. It is not a law of nature, since a well-reputed actor may still behave
inappropriately, and it is not a mathematical object; reputation systems nevertheless
have to reduce it to one number or a few numbers, which are useful insofar as they
support reasonable statistical forecasts.

The human origin of reputation can be leveraged against automated attacks. Limits on
the number of actions per unit of time constrain machines far more than humans, as
the daily caps on likes of some social platforms illustrate. In a scientific network,
such budgets can be expressed in 
human time
 and made much more stringent.
Silent reading of English non-fiction proceeds on average at about 238 words per
minute 
[
8
]
, techniques that claim to increase this rate
substantially do so at the expense of comprehension 
[
71
]
, and typing
speed is similarly bounded and skill-dependent 
[
75
]
. An
assessment of a 7,000-word paper submitted five minutes after its first access
therefore cannot result from a reading. Such budgets are signals rather than proofs,
since humans skim and reviewers do not read every paper they cite.

Interaction.
 Budgets are enforced 
per identity
. If identities are free,
an attacker multiplies budgets by multiplying identities, so that C1 is effective
only together with a cost on identities.

Derived signals do not escape this logic. On X, likes and reposts are the basic
quantities from which reputation is inferred, and users have built derived signals on
top of them, such as the “ratio” (a reply that receives more likes than the
original post). A derived signal remains harder to game only as long as it is not
used as a target; moreover, since the ratio is computed from likes, an attacker able
to forge likes also forges the ratio, and the two signals share the same target
(Section 
4
).

3.4 
C2: assessing is an action with consequences

C2.
 Every visible action affects the reputation of its author,
including the action of assessing others; assessments must therefore be
assessable, and their authors protected from retaliation.

Reputation results from a multi-level game: it is earned through actions
acknowledged by others, and assessing others is itself a way of building one’s own
reputation. An assessment, positive or negative, should increase the reputation of
its author when it proves accurate and decrease it when it proves unfounded. Social
media likes are free and do not engage the reputation of their author. Other systems
make assessments costly or assessable: Slashdot’s meta-moderation lets users judge the
judgments of moderators 
[
49
]
; on Stack Overflow, down-voting an
answer costs the voter one reputation point 
[
81
]
; PeerTrust
weights feedback by the credibility of its issuer 
[
90
]
.

The central difficulty is to determine who assesses an assessment, and against which
ground truth, since the truth value of a scientific claim may remain unknown for
years. Peer-prediction mechanisms score a report against the reports of others, so
that honest reporting is an equilibrium 
[
61
]
, and their authors
explicitly discuss academic reviewing; the Bayesian truth serum rewards answers that
are more common than collectively predicted 
[
69
]
, which
penalizes herding. These mechanisms are vulnerable to coordinated raters, but they
reward an assessment before the ground truth is known.

Accountability also has a cost. When assessments are public and attributable, fear
of retaliation biases them: on eBay, feedback is overwhelmingly
positive 
[
72
]
, and silence itself carries
information 
[
19
]
. In science, the asymmetry of power between
junior and established researchers is a main reason for anonymous reviewing. A system
in which every assessment is signed and permanent may therefore suppress precisely the
negative assessments that it needs.

C2 therefore requires mechanisms that create reputation, since reputation is not a
zero-sum quantity; mechanisms that destroy it without necessarily transferring what is
lost (C4); consequences for every action; the possibility of staking reputation on an
assessment and gaining from it once the assessment is corroborated, which differs from
the fees that pay for the operation of a cryptocurrency; and protection of assessors
against retaliation.

3.5 
C3: score and confidence evolve with time

C3.
 Reputation must be represented by at least two quantities, a
recency-weighted score and a confidence that grows with evidence, and rewards
must be timed according to the slowest relevant signals.

Unlike a currency balance, reputation changes with the passage of time in two
opposite ways: recent actions should weigh more than past ones, and a longer track
record should yield a more accurate estimate. The two effects can be reconciled only
if reputation is not summarized by a single number. The beta reputation
system 
[
40
]
 and subjective logic 
[
39
]

make this explicit: given (discounted) amounts 
 of positive and 
 of
negative evidence, an opinion is the triple

of belief, disbelief and uncertainty, with

effect, while

mixed record may have the same expected value but very different uncertainties.

Time is also an attack surface. An actor may build a reputation and then exploit
it, possibly in cycles, and detecting such oscillations requires comparing short- and
long-term behaviors 
[
80
]
. Time constants must also match the
dynamics of the network: the half-life of a post ranges from minutes on X to months
on Pinterest or blog platforms 
[
34
]
, whereas the half-life of the
scientific literature, a classical bibliometric notion 
[
9
]
, is
measured in years. Rewards attached to assessments should therefore be held in escrow
until slow signals, such as citations, become available. Finally, recording only the
current value of reputation is insufficient: its time series must be kept, in a
shared append-only ledger when one exists, or locally otherwise.

3.6 
C4: losses are easier than gains

C4.
 Reputation must be harder and slower to earn than to lose,
unfounded negative assessments must cost more than unfounded positive ones, and a
fresh identity must be worth less than a damaged one.

Reputation does not evolve symmetrically, which further distinguishes it from
conventional assets and from zero-sum games. In social psychology, the asymmetry
principle states that trust is much easier to destroy than to
create 
[
79
]
, an instance of the general negativity
bias 
[
3
]
. The principle is not universal, since its strength
depends on prior attitudes and trust can be symmetric under some
conditions 
[
68
]
, but it is supported by field data: on eBay, a
seller’s first negative feedback causes a sharp drop in sales, and subsequent negative
feedback arrives markedly faster than the first 
[
10
]
.

A single event may therefore destroy a reputation, and the reputation lost may exceed
the reputation earned. Correctly identifying a poor publication should be rewarded,
because detecting unreliable work benefits the network, but the reward need not
compensate the loss of the assessed author. An honest error should also be
distinguished from persistent error or bad faith, and a series of poor outcomes is
more informative than an isolated one. Finally, unfounded criticism should be deterred
more strongly than unfounded praise.

Interaction.
 The harsher the loss, the greater the incentive to abandon a
damaged identity: C4 is effective only if newcomers start with low reputation or
high uncertainty 
[
93
, 
32
]
, i.e. if
identities have a cost (C1).

3.7 
C5: reputation is both local and global

C5.
 Assessments must be weighted by the relation between assessor
and assessed, reputation must be computed relative to trusted nodes rather than
symmetrically, and endorsements across communities must weigh more than
endorsements within them.

An assessment by a newcomer should not have the same influence as one by a highly
reputed actor, and some systems allow only the buyers of a product to rate it:
reputation is recursive and depends on the topology of interactions. Locality is also
required for a formal reason. Since no symmetric reputation function is
sybilproof 
[
14
]
, robust systems compute reputation relative to
trusted nodes, as EigenTrust does with pre-trusted peers 
[
42
]
 and
attack-resistant trust metrics do with seeds 
[
51
]
; moreover,
controversial users cannot be captured by any global score 
[
57
]
.

In science, researchers form communities that interact heavily around a theme and can
be identified from citation patterns. Interactions within a community are not
perceived like interactions with outsiders, which creates a trade-off: a small group
can game a naive implementation by exchanging citations, whereas ignoring in-group
judgments discards expertise and over-weighting them creates echo
chambers 
[
57
]
. Bridging-based ranking 
[
89
]

suggests a way out: an endorsement from outside the citation community of a paper
weighs more than one from inside it. This also favors novelty, since novel papers are
significantly more cited in other fields than in their home
field 
[
88
]
. One may also consider a zero-sum game within a community
and a positive-sum game with outsiders. How communities should be defined from
interactions alone, and how actors who belong to several communities should be
handled, remain open problems.

3.8 
C6: signals act as a combination therapy

C6.
 Signals must rely on distinct resources of the attacker, cover
the same actions, be aggregated conjunctively, and be chosen so that gaming one of
them exposes the attacker to another.

Each of the constraints C1–C5 calls for one or several signals, and each signal,
taken in isolation, can be circumvented. C6 states how signals should be assembled
and is the object of Section 
4
.

Interactions between constraints.

The constraints are not
independent. C1 and C4 both require identities to have a
cost. C2 and C3 together call for escrow: assessments can only be rewarded when
slow signals have corroborated them. C5 is both a requirement (Sybil resistance)
and a source of side effects on small communities (Section 
4.11
).

4 
A combination-therapy theory of reputation signals

This section answers RQ2. We first recall what evolutionary medicine has learned
about combination therapies, including how they fail; we then set up a
correspondence with reputation systems, state a model with explicit assumptions,
and derive results that justify conjunctive aggregation and a set of design
rules.

4.1 
Lessons from evolutionary medicine

Absence of cross-resistance.

When the first drugs against the human immunodeficiency virus were used
alone, resistance evolved in nearly all treated individuals within the first
months of treatment 
[
26
]
: the enormous number of viral
replication cycles in an infected individual generates genetic variation at a
high rate 
[
15
]
, so that single resistance mutations are readily
available. Triple-drug therapies, introduced in the mid-1990s, rely on the fact
that mutations conferring resistance to one class of antiretrovirals do not
confer resistance to the other classes. A virus carrying a single resistance
mutation therefore remains suppressed by the other drugs, and several mutations
must be present in the same viral genome for the virus to replicate under
treatment 
[
26
, 
67
]
. To a first
approximation, if these mutations arise independently, the probability that a
virus carries all of them is the product of the individual probabilities. What
matters is the absence of cross-resistance, not the independence of molecular
targets: standard regimens combine two nucleoside reverse transcriptase
inhibitors, which act on the same enzyme, with a third drug from another class.

Sanctuaries and sequential escape.

The multiplicative picture is too
optimistic. Triple therapies drastically reduced, but did not eliminate,
resistance evolution; clinical data show that resistance evolves even after years
of successful therapy, sequentially, often one mutation at a time, and in a
partially predictable order 
[
26
]
. Two mechanisms
explain this pattern. Temporally, imperfect adherence or different drug
half-lives create periods of 
effective monotherapy
. Spatially, imperfect
drug penetration creates compartments in which only one drug reaches an
effective concentration; such “spatial monotherapy” lets resistance mutations
accumulate stepwise and dramatically increases the risk of multidrug resistance,
even when the single-drug compartment is very small 
[
62
]
.
The same authors find that drug combinations with matched penetration profiles
are often preferable, and recall that adding drugs one after the other, or
rotating antibiotics, has been observed to promote multidrug
resistance 
[
62
]
.

Collateral sensitivity.

In some cases the trade-off is stronger:
mutations that confer resistance to one antibiotic increase the sensitivity to
another one 
[
66
, 
50
]
. Collateral sensitivity
networks can be mapped experimentally and used to design drug cycling protocols
that select against resistance 
[
38
]
; alternating treatments
constrain the evolutionary paths to multidrug resistance 
[
46
]
.
The trade-off is not always evolutionarily stable: hypersensitivity can sometimes
turn into multidrug resistance, and the order in which drugs are applied
matters 
[
2
]
.

dropped from the minimum, but dropping is only safe under a condition that follows
from Remark 
4
: 
a sub-indicator may be dropped when missing
only if no gaming action can make it missing
. Otherwise the attacker escapes it by
resetting it, which amounts to being evaluated on a subset of the signals.
Whitewashing resets every history-dependent sub-indicator, so those that penalize
whitewashing must be imputed, not dropped. Sub-indicators that are triggered by the
activity they penalize can be dropped: an assessment produces a reading margin, and
a received citation produces a reciprocity value.

Scale of imputation.

An imputed value must be calibrated on the scale of
the aggregate, not of the sub-indicator. The minimum of several percentiles is
typically much lower than each of them, so a value that is low for a sub-indicator
(its first quartile, say) can be high for the aggregate. The imputed level should be
derived from the rank of the aggregate at which a fresh identity is to be placed.
This makes the trade-off of C4 explicit: identities ranked below that level gain by
whitewashing, unless the cost of a new identity exceeds the gain.

Evidence.

Shrinkage weighs the observed value of a sub-indicator by its
evidence. If evidence is measured by a quantity that gaming can inflate cheaply, such
as the number of one’s own publications, then gaming weakens shrinkage and opens an
additional channel of gain, even when the gaming action has no direct effect on the
raw statistic. Evidence should be measured by quantities that the gaming actions
raising the sub-indicator cannot inflate, such as exposure time.

4.9 
Design rules

The results above translate into four design rules, which refine C6.

Rule 1: orthogonality of resources.

What matters for robustness is not
the formula of a signal but the 
resource
 an attacker must forge to inflate
it. We distinguish five resources: (R1) identities; (R2) human time and attention;
(R3) capital at risk, i.e. staked reputation; (R4) persistence, i.e. consistent
behavior over long periods; (R5) diversity of endorsers, i.e. support from
distinct communities. Two signals relying on the same resource are raised by the
same gaming action: they share a row of positive entries in 
, like two drugs
with cross-resistance. The ratio on X is a case in point: it is computed from
likes, so a single farm of fake accounts raises both.

Rule 2: conjunctive aggregation.

By Corollary 
2
 and
Proposition 
1
, signals should be aggregated with the minimum (or a
soft minimum), not with a weighted sum nor a geometric mean, and within a family
the principle of Section 
4.7
 applies. In the global model, the
residual gain of gaming is bounded by the spread of the honest profile
(Theorem 
1
); the 
discordance
 
 is
therefore both an indicator of what gaming can buy and a natural alert. Honest profiles are not uniform either (a theorist may review
little), so the alert threshold has to be calibrated per community.

Rule 3: collateral sensitivity.

The admissible set is non-empty only
if each gaming action is penalized on some signal that carries weight, or is
costly. Signals should therefore be chosen in pairs such that the gaming strategy
that inflates one of them degrades the other, and non-emptiness should be checked
on estimated matrices with the robust test of Section 
4.6
.

Rule 4: matched coverage and timing.

Every type of action
(publishing, citing, assessing, staking) should be observed by at least two
signals relying on different resources; an action observed by a single signal is
a single-drug compartment 
[
62
]
. Rewards should only be
computed once the slow signals are available, otherwise the evaluation runs on a
subset of signals (Remark

within

4.10 
Limits of the theory

The results rely on explicit assumptions, which delimit their scope.

•

Global versus local.
 Theorem 
1
 is exact in the global
linear model only; in a real system, its unbounded case means a gain that
grows until the model breaks down, and its bound relies on the linearity
of the effects. Theorem 
2
 is valid under the realistic
assumption H1
 but only near a given profile.

•

Knowledge of 
.
 The matrix 
 is unknown, must be estimated by
simulation or from data, and drifts as attackers innovate, which adds rows
(new gaming actions, the analogue of new mutations) and changes entries (the
analogue of epistasis 
[
2
]
). The conjunctive rule
does not need 
 to be robust, but non-emptiness of the admissible set does
need to be checked (Section 
4.6
).

•

Normalization.
 If signals are percentiles (H2), the profile of an
account depends on the others: lowering a competitor raises one’s own
percentile. This strategic interaction is not captured by the model; it
appears in the attack matrix of MagMell as an indirect effect of
bad-mouthing.

•

Coalitions.
 H6 treats a coalition as one decision-maker, which is
conservative for coordination but ignores the costs of coordinating.

•

Noise.
 The minimum of 
 noisy sub-indicators is biased downwards,
and more so when 
 is large and data are scarce. Conjunction therefore
trades robustness against false positives on accounts with little data
(newcomers, small fields). Screening integrity sub-indicators instead of
ranking them, shrinkage, and reporting uncertainty separately reduce this
effect (Section 
4.8
); the analysis of
Section 
6.2
 shows that it is large when these
precautions are not taken.

4.11 
Side effects

Every therapy has side effects, and the relevant ones here are false positives on
honest users. Researchers in small or niche fields receive few endorsements from
other communities; newcomers have little topological trust and high uncertainty;
novel work is riskier and its recognition is delayed 
[
88
]
, so it is
penalized by short time windows; and any reputation-weighted ranking reinforces
cumulative advantage, the Matthew effect 
[
59
]
. Minimum
aggregation adds its own: an honest researcher whose profile is unbalanced is
ranked by her weakest signal. The hard case for a scientific reputation system is
not the flat-earther, it is the heterodox claim that turns out to be right. The
“toxicity” of a set of signals on the honest population must therefore be
measured as carefully as its efficacy against attackers, and the mechanisms must be
designed to limit it: MagMell’s rules G3–G7 (Section 
5.10
)
address small fields, multidisciplinary authors, minority critiques, recent work and
the selection of assessors by capital. Section 
6.7
 measures
how long a heterodox researcher recognized late takes to reach the median rank in a
simulation. Two further side effects
concern the permanence of records: the chilling effect on criticism
(Section 
3.4
), and the tension between immutable ledgers and
the right to erasure 
[
27
]
.

5 
MagMell: a two-tier P2P reputation system for scientific publication

This section answers RQ3. It specifies the mechanisms and incentive rules of
MagMell, which has not been implemented; numerical values are indicative and meant to
be calibrated by simulation (Section 
7.2
).

5.1 
Goals and two-tier architecture

MagMell is a P2P network on which researchers publish scientific material
(research articles, or discussions of topics and of other published material), with
a built-in reputation system. Its aim is not to abolish reviewing but to move it after
publication: the network is open, anyone can publish, and the reputation system
separates signal from noise. Work that is consistently judged unsound by independent
readers is ranked low and receives little attention. As noted in
Section 
4.11
, this is the easy case; the design must also avoid
burying heterodox but correct work.

MagMell separates a 
protocol
 from the 
reputation algorithms
 that
interpret it (Fig. 
1
). 
Tier 1
, the protocol, is a
replicated state machine. Its state consists of identities, balances, budgets,
stakes and escrows; its transitions are signed events, recorded in a totally ordered
log. Tier 1 checks the validity of events, enforces budgets and rate limits, and
executes the deterministic settlement and minting rules. From the layer that orders
events, tier 1 only requires an abstract interface: append a valid event, read the
ordered log, and obtain checkpoints of the state signed by the ordering layer. Any
consensus protocol that provides a totally ordered and available log can implement
this interface; Section 
5.2
 describes the instantiation that we
analyze. 
Tier 2
 consists of
reputation algorithms that compute sub-indicators and aggregates from the log. Tier 2
is open and plural: any node can run any algorithm on its replica, and each user can
choose or tune the algorithm that ranks what she sees, because reputation rests on
local sensibilities (C5). One tier-2 algorithm, versioned and public, is designated
by governance as the 
reference algorithm
: it is the only one whose outputs
change tier-1 state, through settlement (A4–A8) and minting
(Section 
5.9
), and since it is deterministic every replica computes
the same result. The protocol only relies on 
objective data
, the events
recorded in the log; Section 
5.4
 states what it guarantees
whatever tier-2 algorithm is used.

5.2 
System model: nodes, trust assumptions and failures

Node roles.

MagMell distinguishes five roles, which a physical machine
may combine.

•

Participants
 hold the keys of their identities (public keys of a
signature scheme such as Ed25519 
[
5
]
), sign events, and
run either a full node or a light client.

•

Validators
 implement the ordered log. In the instantiation that we
analyze, they form a committee of 
 nodes operated by independent and
identifiable institutions (libraries, universities, learned societies)
renewed by governance, and they run a BFT
state-machine-replication protocol for the partially synchronous model, such
as Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance (PBFT) 
[
13
]
 or HotStuff 
[
91
]
. This
is a design choice suited to the domain rather than a technical concession:
scientific communication already relies on such institutions to operate
shared infrastructure, such as repositories and archives, and identifiable
operators are accountable, legally and reputationally, for misbehavior that
the log makes provable (see the trust model of the committee below). The
reputation layer is agnostic to this choice: a permissionless protocol such
as proof of stake 
[
33
]
 could implement the same
interface, but we do not claim that it would have the same properties, and
we analyze only the committee case.

•

Full nodes
 store the whole log and the tier-1 state, verify the
validators’ signatures on blocks and checkpoints, serve light clients with
Merkle proofs, and run tier-2 algorithms. Any participant or institution can
run one.

•

Storage nodes
 store contents in a content-addressed
DHT 
[
58
]
, as in IPFS 
[
4
]
,
with 
 replicas per item; the institutions of the authors pin their
contents.

•

Tier-2 providers
 are optional full nodes that publish signed tier-2
results, with the log position at which they were computed, for light
clients. They are not trusted: anyone holding the log can recompute their
results.

Trust assumptions.

Signatures are unforgeable and hash functions
collision-resistant. At most 
 of the 
 validators are Byzantine; the network
is partially synchronous 
[
22
]
: messages between correct nodes
are delivered within an unknown bound after an unknown global stabilization time.
Any number of participants, full nodes, storage nodes and tier-2 providers may be
Byzantine: they may withhold, delay or forge content, or equivocate, but cannot forge
signatures. A client that connects to at least one correct full node, or verifies
checkpoints signed by 
 validators, obtains the correct tier-1 state.

Failure model.

Nodes may crash, leave and rejoin. Storage nodes are
unavailable independently with probability 
, an assumption. If more than 

validators are unavailable, the log stops growing but its safety is preserved:
assessments and settlements are delayed, not lost, and escrow horizons are counted in
ordered epochs.

Trust model of the committee.

MagMell is open at the level of
participants but not at the level of consensus: in the model analyzed, decentralization
is institutional. This has four consequences, which we state rather than solve.

Capture
: if 
 validators collude, they can refuse to vote for blocks
that contain given events, and thus censor them or halt the log; if 
 collude,
they can commit blocks that violate the validity rules. Full nodes re-execute every
block, so that invalid transitions are detected and publicly provable, but not
prevented. 
Governance
: the committee is renewed by governance, so that its
honesty rests on the honesty of the renewal process, itself subject to capture.

Concentration
: institutions that run validators are few and unevenly
distributed among countries and fields; diversity of operators is a requirement on
governance, not a property of the protocol. 
Beyond 
: with more than 

Byzantine validators, neither safety (two conflicting blocks may be committed), nor
liveness, nor freedom from censorship is guaranteed.

Replication cost among validators.

Each block costs 
 messages
per phase with HotStuff, whose communication is linear in the number of
replicas 
[
91
]
, and 
 with PBFT 
[
13
]
.
The consensus layer is specified but neither implemented nor benchmarked: confirmation
latency, achievable throughput, replication cost, behavior with faulty or malicious
validators and recovery after an interruption are not measured in this paper.

Out of scope.

Network-level attacks (eclipse of a client, routing
attacks), traffic analysis and the de-anonymization of pseudonyms are not addressed.
The Sybil resistance of the consensus layer is that of the committee; it is distinct
from the Sybil resistance of reputation, which onboarding
(Section 
5.6

5.3 
Protocol: events, operations and validity

Events.

An event is a tuple (type, identity, sequence number, epoch,
payload, signature). Per-identity sequence numbers make equivocation (two different
events signed with the same sequence number) detectable. Contents are stored
off-log and only their hashes are recorded: posts cannot be silently modified, but
contents can be removed when the law requires it while integrity proofs
remain 
[
27
]
. Each account has a currency balance (Curr) and a
reputation balance (Rep). Curr is transferable; it is created as in standard
cryptosystems, e.g. by participation rewards 
[
33
]

mining 
[
63
]

size of the message, an anti-spam fee that bounds volume, not quality.

Operations.

The protocol has six operations.

O1.

Attest.
 A full member attests a newcomer and locks a vouching stake

member, has a vouching right left (quota

O2.

Publish.
 An event carries the hash and size of the content, the keys
and signatures of all co-authors, and the list of referenced items, which
are the citations counted as recognitions by tier 2. The authors’ node puts
the content to 
 storage nodes. Validity: signatures, writing budget (B2),
Curr fee. Validators do not fetch contents.

O3.

Access.
 Once per epoch, a reader’s node submits a signed batch of
the items first accessed during the epoch, with their times. Validity:
reading budget (B1). Accesses are the evidence used by the admissibility rule
(A1) and by the independence and resolution rules (A5, A6).

O4.

Assess.
 An assessment names its target, its polarity, its stake and
a commitment to the reference forecast (A1–A4). Validity: admissibility
(A1), stake limits (A2) and free Rep; the stake is locked in escrow (A3).

O5.

Settle and mint.
 No transaction is needed: at each epoch boundary,
every validator applies the reference algorithm to the assessments whose
escrow expires (A4–A9) and to the publications that reach their minting
horizon (Section 
5.9

boundary. The resulting burns and mints are part of the state transition.

O6.

Checkpoint.
 Every

the tier-1 state with 
 signatures.

Validators check what is decidable from the

They do not check whether a publication is scientific, a citation relevant or an
assessment honest. Quality is the domain of tier 2, through the signals of
Section

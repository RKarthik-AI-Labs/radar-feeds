Abstract

Screen history can serve as searchable visual memory for people and agents, but unlike an offline corpus, it must be encoded and indexed as each screenshot arrives. OCR-based indexing is fast but loses visual information; visual encoding takes over two seconds. Yet screens change incrementally: most pixels repeat within and across screenshots. This raises a question: 
do visual encoders need to recompute their representations from scratch for every new screenshot?
 We present 
StreamEmbed
, a system for incremental visual embedding that exploits this redundancy. Guided solely by pixel differences, it combines 
spatial token pruning
, which skips blocks that repeat a neighbor, with 
temporal KV reuse
, which reuses cached keys and values for blocks unchanged across screenshots. A small learned adapter compensates for pruning and reuse to preserve fidelity to full-screenshot embeddings. On 
ScreenMemEval
, our benchmark of 10,426 questions over 184 hours of screen histories from two humans and a computer-use agent, 
StreamEmbed
 sets a new accuracy–latency Pareto frontier on an M2 Pro GPU: it encodes up to 3.7
 faster than full-screenshot encoding without sacrificing accuracy and improves accuracy over OCR-based indexing by up to 6.3 Recall@10 points at matched latency.
A memory-efficient vector index stores a simulated year of screenshots in 0.13 GB of RAM. Together, these results make real-time indexing of screen history in its native visual form practical, laying a foundation for fully local visual assistants.

Much of daily computer work passes through laptop screens: reading papers, browsing products, and messaging colleagues. Yet people remember few of the details and may later ask: “Which paper had the pink and blue Pareto frontier curves I saw last week?” A searchable screen history could help people recover these details, and also provide persistent memory for locally running agents 
(
Packer et al., 2023
; 
Wu et al., 2025
)
. Because screenshots capture every application without specialized integration, they offer a unified interface across text and visual contents 
(
Hu and Lee, 2020
)
. Answering questions about past activity then becomes a retrieval problem over a continuously growing screenshot history.

The key challenge is maintaining this visual memory continuously without exhausting system resources.
Unlike a fixed corpus that is indexed offline 
(
Karpukhin et al., 2020
)
, this setting requires 
real-time indexing
: encoding and indexing each screenshot as it arrives, ideally locally to keep private content on device, while consuming only a small fraction of the laptop’s capacity.

Converting each screenshot into OCR text, as existing open-source tools do 
(
OpenRecall, 2024
; 
McGhee, 2023
)
, is fast but loses what only pixels capture 
(
Wang et al., 2026c
)
.
Direct visual embedding preserves this information but takes 2.3 s per screenshot on the GPU. At one screenshot every 5 s, this uses the GPU for 28 minutes per hour of the user’s work, and at one every 2 s, it cannot keep pace. Even commercial systems, such as Microsoft Recall 
(
Microsoft, 
)
, require dedicated hardware like NPUs and at least 16 GB of RAM.
Thus, the central question is: can we retain visual retrieval quality while keeping continuous encoding lightweight enough to run silently in the background?

Screen histories, however, offer a particular opportunity: they change incrementally and are largely redundant (Figure 
2
).
Background elements often repeat within and across screenshots, and typing or moving the cursor changes only a few regions, leaving the rest untouched. This raises a question: 
do we need to compute representations from scratch for every screenshot?

We propose 
StreamEmbed
, which encodes screenshots as a stream rather than as isolated images, exploiting this redundancy.
Within a screenshot, it drops pixel blocks that merely duplicate a neighboring block (spatial pruning), cutting the computation by 33–41%. Across screenshots, it recomputes only the changed blocks and reuses cached keys and values for the rest in both the vision encoder and the language model (temporal reuse), cutting the remaining computation by a further 34–57%.
These two techniques are largely complementary, and combining them further reduces the computation per screenshot by 57–71%.
A small adapter merged into the weights compensates for pruning and reuse, keeping the resulting embedding close to that of the full screenshot.

On

Against baselines on the same backbone, fine-tuned on the same data, it is up to 6.3 points more accurate than an OCR-based index at similar latency, and up to 3.7

To cut latency, one can lower the input resolution or use a smaller encoder. When we apply these to both

3 
Visual Encoding

A screenshot index encodes each arriving screenshot with a vision-language embedding model. This section describes the challenge, encoding as the main cost of indexing (§
3.1
), and the opportunity, redundant blocks that account for most of it (§
3.2
).

3.1 
Challenges

Indexing a screenshot has two steps: encoding and insertion. Insertion takes under a millisecond, so nearly all of the cost lies in encoding. A vision-language embedding model such as Qwen3-VL-Embedding encodes a screenshot with a vision encoder and passes the resulting hundreds of visual tokens through its language model, whereas a text index passes only the OCR text through the language model.

As Table 
1
 shows, encoding a screenshot takes 2.33 s on an M2 Pro GPU: 7.5
 as long as encoding its OCR text, and 3.6
 the total latency of a text index even with OCR included. At one screenshot every 
 s, encoding thus occupies the GPU nearly half of the time, on a laptop that remains in use for other work. This cost likely explains why existing tools index OCR text, despite a 10.1-point drop in R@10 
(
screenpipe, 2024
; 
OpenRecall, 2024
; 
McGhee, 2023
)
. Moreover, it recurs for every screenshot, since screenshot indexes encode each one from scratch.

3.2 
Opportunities

Encoding each screenshot from scratch ignores how much of a screen repeats, both within a screenshot and across screenshots. (i) Spatial redundancy arises within a screenshot, where backgrounds, margins, and blank panels span large areas (Figure 
2
(a)). (ii) 
Temporal redundancy
 arises across screenshots, where typing or moving the cursor changes a few regions and leaves the rest of the screen as it was (Figure 
2
(b)). Natural video has little of either: texture varies within a frame, and camera motion alters most pixels between frames.

Figure 
2
(d) quantifies this. In screen recordings, 67–77% of the pixel blocks that become visual tokens repeat a neighbor exactly or are unchanged from the previous screenshot, against 6–19% in natural video. An encoder that treats every screenshot as new thus spends most of its work on redundant tokens. It could instead skip blocks that repeat a neighbor, reuse the computation of blocks unchanged since the previous screenshot, and recompute only the rest. 
StreamEmbed
 builds on this idea (§
4
).

Single-recording questions.
 A single-recording question asks where, when, what, or how a task was done, or which value was entered, and is phrased in the first person, as the worker or the persona would ask it. Opus 5.5 
(
Anthropic, 2026

To keep each answer unique, the same model, as a judge, compares each question with similar recordings, discards it if another recording answers differently, and labels recordings with the same answer as relevant. For 
SME-Agent
, GPT-6-astra 
(
OpenAI, 2026a
)
 also checks that the recording’s screens and actions support the answer.

Multi-recording questions.
 A multi-recording question counts how often an event occurred, compares two values, orders two events, or compares two durations. Its answer is computed from the annotations or verified answers of single recordings, and an LLM only phrases the question. To ensure each question needs all its evidence, a comparison, order, or duration, a question is kept only if an LLM answers it correctly from the relevant recordings, but fails once any one is removed. A count must match two independent LLM recounts exactly. In 
SME-Agent

Main Results

Figure

 compares 
StreamEmbed
 against all text and screenshot indexes across the three histories, reporting R@10 and mean latency on the GPU and CPU.

StreamEmbed
 is 2.8–3.7
 faster than the screenshot index and stays within 1.3 R@10 of it.
 Against the fine-tuned Qwen3-VL-2B screenshot index, which takes 2.33–2.34 s per screenshot on the GPU, 
StreamEmbed
 takes 0.66, 0.84, and 0.63 s on 
SME-H1
, 
SME-H2
, and 
SME-Agent
 and scores 1.3, 1.2, and 0.6 R@10 lower (Figure

). It is more accurate than the base screenshot index on 
SME-H1
 and 
SME-H2
 and 1.0 R@10 below it on 
SME-Agent
. Only Qwen3-VL-8B is clearly more accurate, on 
SME-Agent
, at about eight times the latency.

StreamEmbed
 is more accurate than every text index, at a similar latency.
 The strongest text index, the fine-tuned Qwen3-VL-2B fused with BM25, scores 3.5–4.6 R@10 below 
StreamEmbed
, and larger text encoders such as Qwen3-Emb-8B do not close this gap while taking longer. On the GPU, 
StreamEmbed
 nearly matches its latency on 
SME-H1
 (0.66 vs. 0.62 s) and is faster on 
SME-Agent
 (0.63 vs. 0.69 s). On 
SME-H2

 takes 1.87–2.61 s, close to the strongest text index (1.86–2.12 s), while the fine-tuned screenshot index takes 5.42–5.81 s, longer than even a 5 s capture interval.

StreamEmbed
 moves the Pareto frontier beyond lower resolution and smaller encoders.
 A common way to cut indexing latency is to lower the input resolution or to use a smaller encoder. Whereas Figure 
1
 runs each baseline at its default resolution, Figure 
5
 revisits the comparison by applying both to the screenshot index as well as to 
StreamEmbed
, which can also raise its drop ratio. Even then, on both encoder families and all three histories, 
StreamEmbed

SME-H1
, Qwen3-VL-2B with 
StreamEmbed
 at 480
270 reaches 75.3 R@10, against 65.0 for the full screenshot at 320

StreamEmbed
 on Qwen3-VL-2B still scores 2.7 R@10 above Qwen3-VL-8B at 480

StreamEmbed

StreamEmbed

6.3 
Indexing a Long Stream

So far, we have shown that 
StreamEmbed
 makes real-time encoding practical, but on-device retrieval poses a second challenge: can a growing screenshot index fit within a laptop’s RAM? Memory already constrains deployment (
e.g.
, Recall 
(
Microsoft, 
)
 requires at least 16 GB).

To quantify how far an index can go, we grow a simulated index from one to five years of screenshots (2.1M per year at one every 5 s over eight hours a day, whose fp16 embeddings take 8.6 GB) by adding Gaussian noise to copies of the real embeddings. We compare exact search (Flat), approximate search with an inverted file (IVF) 
(
Jégou et al., 2011
)
 or a graph (HNSW) 
(
Malkov and Yashunin, 2020
)
, and compressed codes kept in RAM whose top 1,000 candidates are re-ranked with the full embeddings on disk, namely product quantization (PQ) 
(
Jégou et al., 2011
)
 and 1-bit codes 
(
Shakir et al., 2024
; 
Yamada et al., 2021
)
, all implemented in Faiss 
(
Douze et al., 2024
)
.

Table 
4
 reports the accuracy of each index on 
SME-H1
 and 
SME-Agent
, and its RAM, search time, and insertion time as the history grows, on the CPU of the M2 Pro.

Indexes that keep full embeddings in RAM may not scale to years of screenshots.
 Flat needs 8.6 GB of RAM per year and exceeds 24 GB at five years, and its search time grows with the index, from 440 ms at one year to 881 ms at two. IVF searches about five times faster than Flat but loses up to 2.8 R@10, since relevant screenshots can fall outside the clusters it searches. HNSW searches in about 1 ms but needs 7% more RAM than Flat for its graph and loses up to 1.4 R@10.

Compressed codes with re-ranking from disk keep R@10 at a small fraction of the RAM.
 PQ and 1-bit codes stay within 0.1 R@10 of Flat, since the codes only need to place the relevant screenshots among the top 1,000, and re-ranking with the full embeddings restores their order. 1-bit codes of the first 512 dimensions take 64 bytes per screenshot, 64 times less than Flat, and hold five years in 0.67 GB. Comparing bit codes is also cheap, so they search in 56 ms after five years, against 359 ms for PQ. With the 52 ms of encoding a question, retrieval takes about 0.1 s even after five years.

Insertion stays negligible even with five years of history.
 Inserting a screenshot takes at most 0.67 ms, for HNSW, and 0.01 ms for 1-bit codes, against 0.63–0.84 s to encode it with 
StreamEmbed
. This confirms the premise of §
3.1
 that the cost of real-time indexing lies in encoding, not insertion, even as the history grows, and it is this cost that 
StreamEmbed
 reduces.

6.4 
Generalization to Other Encoders

Table 
5
 applies 
StreamEmbed
 with the same thresholds to four other screenshot encoders, each fine-tuned with LoRA on 
SME-H1
, and compares it with each encoder on full screenshots.

StreamEmbed
 generalizes to every encoder, staying close to its full-screenshot accuracy.
 It lowers the GPU latency of all five encoders, by 1.3–1.9
 on SigLIP2 and Vultron and by 2.5–3.7
 on Qwen3-VL, while losing only 1.3 R@10 on average.

The speedup grows with the computation that reuse can skip.
 
StreamEmbed
 speeds up the Qwen3-VL encoders by 2.5–3.7
 on the GPU, since temporal reuse also saves computation in the language model. On SigLIP2, which has no language model, and on Vultron, whose language model mostly uses linear attention, 
StreamEmbed
 reuses keys and values only in the vision encoder and still speeds them up by 1.3–1.9
 on the GPU and 1.5–2.2
 on the CPU. On Vultron, which stores one vector per visual token, pruning also stores 23–30% fewer vectors per screenshot, trading some accuracy on 
SME-Agent
 for a smaller index.

6.5 
Ablation

Table 
6
 adds the components of 
StreamEmbed
 one at a time to the released Qwen3-VL-2B, fine-tuned with LoRA, starting with the equivalent linear patch embedding that 
StreamEmbed
 uses for Qwen3-VL (§
6.1
).

StreamEmbed
 speeds up encoding by 2.8–3.7
 over the released model, mostly through spatial pruning and temporal reuse.
 Spatial pruning removes the repetition within a screenshot and temporal reuse removes the repetition across screenshots, each speeding up encoding by about 1.5
 at about 1 R@10. Their savings thus multiply, to 2.0–2.6
 together. Their overhead is small, as pruning and change detection add about 0.01 s per screenshot, and in the final configuration the vision encoder and the language model each run two to three times faster than without them.

Grid keep and the consistency adapter recover the accuracy lost due to pruning on 
SME-Agent
.
 Pruning every redundant block costs far more on 
SME-Agent
 than on the human histories, suggesting that dropping every redundant block shifts the embedding. Grid keep recovers most of this loss for about 0.15 s per screenshot, and the consistency adapter recovers more at no latency cost, leaving 
StreamEmbed
 within 1.3 R@10 of the full screenshot on all three histories.

7 
Related Work

Screen history.

Re-finding what was once on a computer screen has long been studied, with people searching the files, e-mail, and web pages they have opened 
(
Dumais et al., 2003
)
 or browsing past screenshots 
(
Hu and Lee, 2020
)
.
Such histories serve not only people but also agents, which complete more later tasks when they reuse past experience 
(
Wang et al., 2024
; 
Agashe et al., 2025
)
 or see past screenshots rather than summaries 
(
Luo et al., 2026
)
.
Recent systems build such histories using vision-language models (VLMs), turning screenshots into the text memory of an agent 
(
Wang and Chen, 2025
)
 or a log of each task 
(
Yin et al., 2026
)
, or answering questions over them 
(
Li et al., 2025
)
.
These systems rely on large cloud models 
(
Wang and Chen, 2025
; 
Li et al., 2025
)
 or, to run on the device, process only some screenshots 
(
Yin et al., 2026
)
, and either way represent screenshots as text.
Yet retrieving from pixels is more accurate than from text on documents and the web 
(
Ma et al., 2024
; 
Faysse et al., 2025
)
.
We thus keep every screenshot as an embedding of its pixels, computed on the device.

Growing indexes.

Earlier work has studied indexes that grow over time, but it rarely treats the cost of encoding each new item as the bottleneck. Real-time search indexes text, which is cheap 
(
Busch et al., 2012
)
, and updatable vector indexes leave embedding as a separate step 
(
Xu et al., 2023
)
. Work on evolving corpora studies how to add documents to a trained retriever 
(
Kishore et al., 2023
)
 and when to update it 
(
Ko et al., 2025
; 
Kim et al., 2024
)
. For screenshots, however, encoding is the bottleneck. Work that faces this cost encodes less or defers it, using cheaper models 
(
Hsieh et al., 2018
)
, fewer frames 
(
Kang et al., 2020
)
, early exit 
(
Cai et al., 2025
)
, smaller encoders 
(
Teiletche et al., 2025
)
, or lower resolution 
(
Wang et al., 2026c
)
. Instead, we remove only the computation that screens repeat, which is combined with a smaller encoder, lower resolution, or a skip gate. A growing index must also stay small. 
Lyu et al. (2026)
 re-rank compressed codes from disk, and 
Wang et al. (2026b)
 recompute embeddings at search time on a laptop.
We study how to fit a screenshot index in a laptop’s memory.

Streaming VLMs.

Streaming VLMs keep each arriving frame in their context to answer questions about the video at any time 
(
Chen et al., 2024
)
, so their main concern is what to keep.
Instead, we store each screenshot in an index for later queries, as in 
Jeong et al. (2025)
, so our main concern is how fast each one is encoded.
This leads to three differences.
These models keep tokens or their keys and values, which they evict, compress 
(
Xu et al., 2025a
)
, or retrieve for each question 
(
Di et al., 2025
; 
Ning et al., 2026
)
.
Our index holds only one embedding per screenshot.
Methods that cut computation across frames decide what to skip inside or after the vision encoder 
(
Yao et al., 2025
; 
Wang et al., 2026d
)
.
Others decide from pixels but reuse computation in at most one of the encoder and the LM 
(
Choudhury et al., 2024
; 
Xu et al., 2025b
)
.
In contrast, our method decides fast from pixels before the encoder and reuses unchanged blocks in both.
Finally, prior work keeps accuracy with modules added at inference 
(
Aggarwal and Welleck, 2026
; 
Hwang et al., 2025
; 
Soldan et al., 2025
)
.
Our adapter is instead a LoRA merged into the weights, adding no latency.

8 
Conclusion

We introduced 
StreamEmbed
, which indexes a growing screenshot history in real time by encoding it as a stream. Since a rendered screen repeats most of its blocks, 
StreamEmbed
 prunes blocks that repeat a neighbor, reuses keys and values of unchanged blocks in both the vision encoder and the language model, and keeps the reduced embedding close to the full one at no added cost. On 
ScreenMemEval
, built from the screenshot histories of human workers and a computer-use agent, 
StreamEmbed
 matches a text index in latency and a screenshot index in accuracy, while binary codes keep a year of screenshots within the laptop’s memory. Future work could learn what to recompute from the stream itself, lowering cost and raising accuracy further. Agents could also search a person’s screenshot history to complete long-horizon tasks that depend on screens seen days or weeks earlier.

Appendix A 
Benchmark Details

Table 
7
 compares 
ScreenMemEval
 with ScreenshotVQA 
(
Wang and Chen, 2025
)
, a screen benchmark.

Questions.
 The generator writes questions for 12 recordings at a time from their annotations, which are the instruction, application, times, and planned steps for 
SME-H1
 and 
SME-H2
, and the sub-task instruction, the agent’s actions, and the scheduled time for 
SME-Agent
. To keep answers unique, the judge compares each question with up to 30 recordings of the same history chosen from annotations alone, such as recordings of the same task or on the same site, and always every sibling sub-task in 
SME-Agent
. No evaluated index chooses them, so the labels favor neither index type. Each count recount (§
5.2
) runs over a shuffled list of the candidate recordings.

Relevance and splits.
 For screenshot-level scoring on 
SME-Agent
, GPT-6-astra marks the keyframes that show each question’s evidence, and each keyframe counts with its run of near-identical screenshots. Training uses the earliest 710 recordings of 
SME-H1
, and the 1,121 questions about the 433 of them without training questions are test questions. Training and validation recordings stay in the index and are relevant to 54 test questions, and removing these questions changes R@10 by at most 0.03 for both the fine-tuned full screenshot and 
StreamEmbed
.

Data.
 
SME-H1
 and 
SME-H2
 come from 
Howland et al. (2025)
, recorded by consenting workers who authorized public release, under the MIT license. 
SME-Agent
 shows a synthetic persona and holds no data of real people. We release all three histories with their questions, relevance labels, and splits.

Appendix C 
Implementation Details

Training.
 Every fine-tuned encoder uses LoRA 
(
Hu et al., 2022
)
 with rank 32 on the attention projections of the vision encoder and of the LM or text tower. It is trained with InfoNCE on the 1,000 training questions of 
SME-H1
, each with two hard negatives, for 300 steps at a learning rate of 
 for Qwen3-VL, 
 for SigLIP2, and 
 for Vultron. The consistency adapter is a second LoRA initialized from the first and distilled from the frozen fine-tuned model with the loss of Eq. (
5
), on screenshots pruned by the neighbor rule, for 300 steps at 
. Text indexes use Apple Vision OCR at its accurate level with language correction, and the fused text index combines Qwen3-VL-2B and BM25 by reciprocal rank fusion with 
 and weight 0.5 on BM25. All checkpoints, the settings of 
StreamEmbed
, and the fusion are chosen on the 
SME-H1
 validation questions.

Latency.
 We time every index on an Amazon EC2 mac2-m2pro instance, an Apple M2 Pro with 32 GB of memory, left idle, with PyTorch on MPS in fp16 for the GPU and in fp32 on 8 threads for the CPU. Screenshots are processed one at a time in their recorded order, caches start empty at each recording, and the timer runs from reading the image to the embedding, including OCR for a text index.

Patch embedding.
 The Qwen3-VL screenshot baselines run the released Conv3d patch embedding, and 
StreamEmbed
 computes the same map as one matrix multiplication, whose embeddings have a cosine similarity of at least 0.99986 in fp16. Vultron runs with an equivalent but faster layout of its own Conv3d, so this baseline is faster than released.

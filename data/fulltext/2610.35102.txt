Abstract

Synthetic supervision can bootstrap semantic parsers in new search domains, but prompt-induced diversity is useful only when generations are faithful to the structured intent. We explore this for cold-start e-commerce search: 118,098 synthetic pairs produced by 6 LLMs with 9 persona-conditioned prompt families on a 47-field UK automotive schema, against 1,214 human-annotated and 15,304 production queries. We show that the IPIP personality bundle improves parser F1 in all four matched contrasts (Holm-corrected 
), and all three bundles have a positive main effect over the complete 
 grid. Syntax conditioning is the axis that moves generations towards real traffic. It also induces lexical diversity. Alignment sKL, the divergence between von Mises–Fisher distributions fitted to synthetic and human-annotated queries, is an unlabelled proxy for parser F1 (
 on intent-elicited human queries, lower is closer). An LLM committee, checked against a blinded author pass, triages the 
 queries missed by every parser and labels 
 as hallucinated. Dropped constraints dominate. The four syntax-bearing families have the largest hallucinated shares, 
 to 
, while IPIP alone has the lowest at

1 
Introduction

E-commerce platforms gain substantial UX and operational advantages by replacing faceted filters with natural-language interfaces. Form-based UIs scale poorly across vast catalogues 
(
Hearst, 2009
)
, while modern shoppers embed complex constraints, such as long-tail specifications and compound Boolean logic, that static forms cannot accommodate 
(
Papenmeier et al., 2021
; 
Luo et al., 2023
; 
Moulton and Ng, 2024
)
. Recent approaches therefore frame e-commerce retrieval as semantic parsing, mapping raw NL queries directly into structured API fields 
(
Tagliabue and Greco, 2023
; 
Weld et al., 2022
)
. Training robust parsers requires extensive labelled data, making synthetic query generation an attractive scalable solution 
(
Malik et al., 2024
; 
Siddiqui et al., 2026
)
. The task is not open-ended dialogue but the narrower problem of mapping a structured search intent to a plausible natural-language query and training a smaller downstream model on those pairs. We study it in UK automotive search, a real, high-dimensional testbed with 47 search fields and long-tail combinations of make, model, pricing, mileage, seller, finance, and feature constraints.

The central problem is not whether synthetic data can be made diverse. It mostly can. The problem is that useful diversity and schema faithfulness pull in different directions: aggressive prompting increases lexical and structural variation but can introduce unsupported attributes, flip inequality directions, or drift into assistant-style text that no longer matches the intent, while tightly constrained prompting preserves the schema and collapses outputs into repetitive templates. We use personas as an experimental mechanism for controlled variation along four axes (IPIP personality, attribute-style adjectives, syntactic modes, and UK demographic descriptors); their construction is in §
3.1
.

Our main empirical contributions are threefold. First, every conditioned parser beats the vanilla prompt; the complete 
 grid gives positive main effects for all three bundles, and the four matched contrasts put the IPIP bundle at 
 to 
 F1 points (§
6.1
). Second, we show that the alignment sKL between the synthetic corpus and a human reference is an unlabelled proxy for parser utility (
 on our elicited reference). Additionally, we compare an in-house reference with 15,304 live production queries (§
4.5
). Third, a three-judge LLM committee audit of the 
 universal parser failures, checked against a blinded author pass, locates the residual defects where syntax-bearing and demographic prompts drop the operator words that bind values to schema fields, and types them: dropped constraints dominate, unsupported additions and direction reversals follow (§
6.2
).

2 
Related Work

Persona-conditioned synthetic generation.

LLM-generated supervision has become a standard substitute for human annotation 
(
Wang et al., 2021
; 
Honovich et al., 2023
)
, and persona prompting is now the
dominant axis for injecting controllable variation into those generations. Persona Hub 
(
Ge et al., 2025
)
 scales this to a billion personas, and a broader
literature surveys role-play and persona-driven generation as a controllable-variation mechanism 
(
Tseng et al., 2024
)
. The mechanism rests on the
observation that Big Five personality variables 
(
Goldberg, 1992
)
 map onto measurable language cues 
(
Mairesse et al., 2007
)
. Most of this work
evaluates persona-conditioned outputs through surface diversity or downstream accuracy; 
Kambhatla et al. (2025)
 found that fine-grained persona detail adds
little lexical diversity over a prompt-length control, which makes prompt length a live confound for any persona manipulation. Whether persona-induced
variation remains faithful to a structured target intent is still open.

Synthetic data generation for e-commerce search.

A parallel line of work applies LLM-generated supervision to e-commerce search. 
Luo et al. (2023)
 parse compound implicit constraints in Amazon product
queries at scale, and 
Malik et al. (2024)
 build a natural-language product-search interface with synthetic supervision in a cold-start setting; more broadly,
synthetic-query generation is now a standard tool for training retrievers and rankers without labels 
(
Bonifacio et al., 2022
; 
Chaudhary et al., 2024
)
. These papers evaluate synthetic queries through downstream retrieval or parser metrics, rarely by auditing the corpus itself against the structured catalogue fields the queries encode.

Position.

The two closest prior works are by 
Kambhatla et al. (2025)
 on persona-induced lexical diversity in creative writing and by 
Schaffelder and Gatt (2026)
 on source diversity in fine-tuning corpora. We share the question but instrument it against a structured e-commerce schema, with a three-lens audit per prompt configuration: internal diversity, distributional alignment to a held-out human reference and downstream parser utility, plus an LLM-judge audit of the residual failures.

3 
Synthetic Dataset

We generated synthetic natural-language queries from a proprietary structured search dataset provided by our industry partner Autotrader UK, a large UK online automotive marketplace,
1
1

1

https://www.autotrader.co.uk/
 amounting to 13M search intents over 7 days. Each example begins as a structured search intent: field–value constraints such as make, model, body type, mileage limit, price bound, or seller type. Several fields carry an 
operator
 as well as a value, the relation that binds the value to the field: a price or mileage limit is a floor or a ceiling, a sort has a direction, and a location comes with a radius. In query text these relations are signalled by 
operator words
 such as “under”, “within”, “from” and “lowest”. We dampen high-frequency queries with a concave power transform to reduce duplicates.

For each LLM, we use the same set of intents for all 9 prompt settings. Between LLMs, however, the intent sets differ. The 9 configurations are therefore directly comparable, since each one is generated from the exact same underlying intents, whereas between the LLMs we draw conclusions more broadly.

3.1 
Prompt Types and Motivation

We construct prompts from four different sources. Three of them (
p

a
, 
s

IPIP Big Five traits (
p

To induce controlled linguistic variation, we steer register and phrasing using the IPIP Big Five personality axes 
(
Goldberg, 1992
)
: openness modulates exploratory versus conventional wording, conscientiousness governs explicit constraint marking, extraversion affects enthusiasm, agreeableness influences politeness, and neuroticism controls tentativeness. Prior work shows that these signals are reliably interpretable, with 
Jiang et al. (2024)
 reporting approximately 80% rater accuracy for intended traits, and that they remain stable across frontier models 
(
Sorokovikova et al., 2024
)
, which is important for multi-generator comparability. Each axis is sampled at three levels (high, low, neutral), with probability 
 assigned to the neutral state, yielding up to 
 combinatorial profiles per generation call (Appendix 
B

UK demographic descriptors (
u

Motivated by NVIDIA’s US-demographic persona set 
(
Meyer and Corneil, 2025
)
 and by evidence that structured demographic profiles aid realistic synthetic user conversations 
(
Yun et al., 2025
)
, we build UK demographic profiles from a UK Open Data consumer-segmentation source. Each frequent postcode area carries 57–221 attribute flags (median 132) spanning age, income, household, and lifestyle; per call we sample a postcode and draw 3–15 of its attributes (Appendix 
C
).

Syntax modes (
s

Following the Yahoo! web-query-log grammar of 
Barr et al. (2008)
, in which web queries form a grammar of their own dominated by noun phrases, we add an explicit syntax axis with six modes (noun phrases, telegraphic fragments, natural-language questions, imperatives, post-modified noun phrases, navigational fragments), weighted as in that study and each accompanied by two sampled in-prompt examples (Appendix 
D
).

Attribute-style adjectives (
a

Shoppers in our partner’s beta tests described cars with vague adjectives such as 
reliable

cheap
 and 
decent

(
Furnas et al., 1987
)

hard_specs

economic

3.2 
Diversity

We report two measures of internal lexical diversity. 
Distinct-2
 
(
Li et al., 2016
)
 rewards fresh bigrams, so a higher score means a more diverse corpus. 
Self-BLEU-4
 
(
Zhu et al., 2018
)
 goes the other way. It measures how much a corpus repeats itself, so lower values indicate greater diversity. Because the intents are matched across prompt configurations within each generator (§
3.1
), we compute both metrics separately for every (LLM, configuration) cell. Each cell is scored on its own train and validation pool, meaning everything in that cell’s corpus except for the reserved synthetic test set (§
5.1
). For each configuration, we then take the mean and standard deviation across LLMs.

4 
Human Dataset

We collected 1,214 
(NL query, structured intent)
 pairs through a University annotation campaign. The campaign ran two protocols. In the first, annotators write a free-form query and then express it by hand in the partner’s filter interface (§
4.1
). Under the second, annotators see a fixed, structured intent on a custom HTML page and write a query for it in their own words (§
4.2
). Ecological validity is higher in the first. The second protocol gives some of that up, in return for coverage breadth and for the intent matching that the alignment analysis needs. It also supplies 
 of the corpus. Because the intents are fixed in advance, the corpus spans vehicle types, price tiers and buying scenarios beyond the subset the recruited annotators would gravitate towards on their own.

Both protocols draw on the same pool of nine annotators: seven took part in the persona-card protocol and six in the website-front verification, with four in both. All were screened to give a mix of age, gender and self-rated car knowledge via a pre-task questionnaire. Self-rated car knowledge ranged from 1 to 4 on a 5-point scale, so the pool holds novices, intermediates and enthusiasts side by side. Five of the nine were planning to buy a car, either actively or in the near term, and prior familiarity with the partner platform ranged from frequent users to people who had never opened it. The spread is deliberate, so the corpus exhibits between-user variation rather than a single domain-expert style. Appendix 
G
 gives the agreement metrics and filtering.

4.1 
Protocol 1: Verification through the Partner’s Website Front

Annotators write a free-form NL query and then express it themselves on the partner’s ordinary filter-based search page, choosing make, model, body type, price, mileage and the rest by hand and recording the resulting URL. The structured intent is therefore the annotator’s own reading of their query rather than a system’s parse, and these are the only rows written without a schema or persona stimulus in view. A second independent pass over the same items supplies double coverage. The protocol yielded 156 pairs after filtering (Appendix 
G
), across six 30-item batches.

4.2 
Protocol 2: Custom HTML Page Experiment

Annotators see each structured intent as a third-person scenario card and type their own NL query into a browser form. GPT-5.2 wrote the cards. We settled on it after comparing how several LLMs structure such scenarios. A field list beside the card—the same field–value list the card was written from—lets annotators verify that their query covers all intended constraints. Below, we refer to this as the 
persona-card
 protocol. It yielded 1,058 retained pairs after filtering (Appendix 
G
).

4.3 
External Reference: Live Production Traffic

To obtain a reference that no annotation stimulus shaped, we additionally take one month of natural-language queries submitted to the partner’s deployed search interface. We never score a parser on it: its query text feeds the alignment analysis, and its recorded fields enter only the corpus comparison of §
4.4
. Every logged string was screened for personal data and matching rows discarded (Ethics Statement), leaving 15,304 of the 
 logged queries.

5 
Methodology

5.1 
Per-Configuration Parser

We fine-tuned a Flan-T5-Large parser 
(
Chung et al., 2024
)
 for each prompt configuration on 10,910 instances of that configuration alone, with 1,212 for validation, plus a 
mixed-corpus
 parser on a training set of the same size sampled uniformly across all 9 configurations. All 
 parsers were fully fine-tuned with best-checkpoint selection on validation F1 (training details in Appendix 
I
) and were evaluated with greedy decoding on the 1,214-pair held-out human reference (§
4
) and a reserved synthetic test set. Following the tuple-level F1 used in aspect-based sentiment analysis (ABSA) 
(
Pontiki et al., 2016
)
, a predicted 
(field, value)
 tuple counts as a true positive only if both the field name and its value match the gold, regardless of prediction order.

5.2 
LLM-as-Judge Committee

To triage T5 errors into likely parser-side failures and likely unfaithful synthetic instances, we use a three-judge LLM committee. Each judge is a current frontier reasoning model from one of the closed-source generator families: 
claude-opus-4-7
, 
gpt-5.5-pro
 and 
gemini-3.1-pro-preview
, each invoked at its highest reasoning setting. Per-instance labels are taken by majority vote. The committee was asked one binary question, whether the generator hallucinated the intent, under a 
cleanroom
 prompt exposing only the seed 
(field, value)
 tuples and the resulting query, with no LLM identity, prompt-configuration code, or parser predictions visible. That rubric is more permissive than the metric the parser is scored on, accepting “Japanese car” for 
make
Honda
 and “low mileage” for an exact 
-mile bound, which the exact field–value metric counts as errors. Its labels are therefore a triage signal for locating defects, not faithfulness scores on the parser’s scale. Full prompts, model settings, aggregation, reliability, and one scoring rule for the no-limit search radius are in Appendix 
K
.

8 
Conclusion

We evaluated 9 persona-conditioned prompt configurations across 6 LLM generators at matched training volume, against 1,214 human-annotated and 15,304 production queries. Conditioning pays. Every conditioned parser beats the vanilla prompt, and the IPIP personality bundle is the most reliable addition, 
 to 
 F1 in all four matched contrasts. Adjectives and syntax add smaller gains that partly overlap, and 
pas
, 
pa
 and the mixed corpus form a statistically indistinguishable top group. Syntax alone moves generations towards real traffic in every contrast and drives diversity, at the plausible cost of the operator words where the audited defects concentrate. Personality widens the wording of each intent. Adjectives raise precision and leave recall almost unchanged. Alignment sKL is a good label-free proxy for parser F1 (
 on the persona-card reference). For deployment we recommend the IPIP prompt as the default, adjectives when the parser over-extracts, and syntax when realism matters, with a check that operator words survive. Each new generator needs a hallucination audit of sampled generations.

9 
Limitations

Domain and distribution.

The study is limited to one UK automotive marketplace and one search schema. Automotive search is a useful stress test because it contains many numeric, categorical, and operator-valued fields, but the findings are evidence about this domain and schema only.

UK-cultural exposure confound.

The 
u
 condition assumes that generators can interpret UK geodemographic descriptors, postcode-area cues, and British automotive/search vocabulary. We do not know how much UK-specific data each LLM saw during pre-training, instruction tuning, localisation, or reinforcement learning. Since the generator pool includes models developed by organisations in different countries and deployment ecosystems, differences under 
u
 may reflect uneven exposure to British data rather than the demographic prompt design alone. We therefore avoid attributing behaviour to model nationality; the relevant unobserved factor is each model’s training and alignment data composition.

Generator comparisons.

Prompt comparisons are controlled within each generator, but generator-level patterns are descriptive. Different generators use different seed-intent slices, vendor stacks, decoding controls, and closed-model implementations. Per-generator diversity trends and audited-pool counts should therefore be read as diagnostics rather than as risk estimates or a definitive LLM leaderboard.

Conditional faithfulness audit.

The committee audit is restricted to universal parser failures, so it estimates 
 rather than full-corpus hallucination. Its three LLM judges overlap with generator families and are a triage instrument, not an oracle. The manual author-validation sample contains only 
 cases from this same subset; one author cannot provide human–human agreement, per-configuration cells are too small for rate comparisons, and the sample does not estimate full-corpus label noise. Because pool entry requires every parser to fail, systematic drift that the parsers have absorbed is invisible to the audit.

Reasoning-model and scale restriction.

The generator pool consists of relatively large reasoning-capable LLMs. Smaller models, non-reasoning chat models, or models run without explicit reasoning support may respond very differently to persona, syntax, and demographic prompts. They may show different diversity–faithfulness trade-offs, different rates of dropped or reversed constraints, and different sensitivity to UK-specific cues. Therefore, the prompt rankings reported here should not be applied directly to cheaper, smaller, or non-reasoning generators without re-running the same alignment, parser-utility, and faithfulness checks.

Prompt-axis sampling.

The prompt families use different internal sampling schemes: IPIP trait levels, syntax modes, adjective classes, and UK demographic attributes are sampled from separate priors. The families also differ in token length, constraint wording, in-prompt demonstrations, and semantic guards, and we ran no length-matched placebo, so a bundle’s effect is not separated from the extra instruction text it carries; 
Kambhatla et al. (2025)
 report that fine-grained persona detail can add little over a length control. The related finding of 
Bui et al. (2025)
 concerns mixing several complete personas within one generation and does not speak to combining the stylistic bundles used here, so it is not evidence for or against our composition choice. Alternative priors or bundle designs could shift the absolute diversity, alignment, and parser-utility scores, and our results should be read as evidence about these operationalisations rather than as an exhaustive search over prompt-sampling policies.

Ethics Statement

Privacy and data minimisation.

The marketplace data that seed generation are treated as anonymised structured search intents rather than user histories. No persistent user identifiers, session trajectories, or personal profiles are used in the experiments. The production reference of §
4.3
 additionally uses real user 
query text
, which is not anonymous at source, so before any query left our infrastructure every logged string was screened for vehicle registration marks, full residential postcodes, telephone numbers, and first-person household detail, and matching rows were discarded rather than redacted (
 of 
 rows, 
, plus one blank query dropped before embedding). Discarding rather than masking avoids leaving partial identifiers in place, and structured browser and account context in the logs (postcode, latitude, longitude, session sort order) is dropped upstream by the schema map and never reaches the reference.

Human annotators.

Human-authored queries are used only for evaluation and alignment analysis, not to infer annotator traits or train user-specific models. Annotator background information is reported only in aggregate.
Annotators were recruited through a University annotation campaign and rewarded with University credits.

Demographic prompting.

The UK demographic prompts use synthetic area-level descriptors and must not be interpreted as modelling real individuals. Such prompts can encode stereotypes or proxy protected attributes. They should not be used for user profiling, eligibility decisions, personalised pricing, or demographic targeting.

Consumer-facing risk.

Unfaithful synthetic queries can teach parsers to drop, add, or reverse constraints such as price limits, radius, finance terms, mileage, or sort order. In a live search product, these errors could affect which listings users see. Synthetic data should therefore be filtered or repaired before deployment, and user-facing systems should preserve confirmation, correction, or fallback mechanisms.

Appendix C 
UK Demographic Persona Sampling

The UK demographic prompt family 
u
 is anchored in a proprietary postcode-indexed attribute store assembled internally from UK Open Data sources (consumer-segmentation, census-derived, and lifestyle-survey aggregates), keyed by postcode area. The store is not publicly released, and the persona profiles are synthetic constructs (cf. Ethics Statement).

Pool size.

The allocation covers 111 UK postcode areas, representing the most geographically distinct postcodes by visitor frequency. Each postcode is keyed to a county or city label (e.g. a Birmingham-area postcode resolves to 
Birmingham
); where the source assigns more than one county to a single postcode, the pipeline takes the first label for brevity in the prompt scaffold. Each postcode carries around 130 area-level attribute flags, drawn from a broader shared pool of around 560 distinct descriptions.

Each postcode’s attribute list mixes coarse demographic indicators (age and occupation bands, household and housing context, financial-context bands) with lifestyle and consumer-style cues (shopping and payment habits, media and technology use, and attitudinal statements such as 
“It is worth paying extra for quality goods”
). The aggregates are banded rather than individual-level, which is what makes the resulting persona prompts read as plausible UK consumer voices rather than as identifying records.

Sampling.

For each generation call assigned to the 
u
 family, the pipeline performs three steps:

1.

Sample a postcode area uniformly from the 111 available.

2.

Sample 
 attributes uniformly without replacement from that postcode’s attribute list (default range; the lower bound of 3 is large enough to fill the prompt context given the cleaned descriptions).

3.

Inject the resulting bullet list into a system-prompt scaffold of the form 
“You are generating a search query as a person from 
[location]
 with the following characteristics: 
bullets
. Generate the query in a way that reflects this person’s background, priorities, and typical concerns.”

Appendix D 
Syntactic Mode Sampling

The syntactic axis (§
3.1
) is implemented as a probabilistic choice over six modes calibrated against the web-query grammar of 
Barr et al. (2008)
, with probabilities normalised at sample time: noun phrase (head noun with modifiers, no verb) 
, telegraphic (keywords only, stop-words removed) 
, natural question (wh-words or auxiliary verbs) 
, imperative (verb-initial command) 
, post-modified noun phrase (noun with a prepositional phrase) 
, and navigational (URL or site-name fragment) 
.

Per-mode prompting.

For each call assigned to a mode, a short instruction template specific to that mode is injected into the system prompt together with two examples sampled uniformly from a per-mode example bank (e.g. 
“Generate the query as a noun phrase without verbs or articles. Examples: 
ex
, 
ex
.”
). Each mode’s example bank holds 10–20 hand-curated UK automotive queries; sampling fresh examples per call prevents the LLM from anchoring on a single canonical form.

Appendix G 
Human Reference Data: Collection and Validation

This appendix gives the agreement metrics and filtering behind the 1,214-pair corpus of §
4
.

Validation and pooled IAA.

Agreement metrics reflect both judge–annotator agreement (for persona-card items) and inter-annotator agreement (for website-front items). We observe a judge-anchored slot-value F1 of 0.85 for the persona-card protocol, while the website-front items show an inter-annotator slot-value F1 of 0.67, with disagreements stemming mostly from slot selection rather than value assignment.

Filtering pipeline.

Persona-card items are validated by a GPT-5.4 reasoning judge that compares each written NL query against the source structured intent; these items are human-written, so the judge’s overlap with the generator pool does not bear on them. Items flagged as potentially inconsistent or as field-direction reversals (e.g. price floor vs. price ceiling) are manually reviewed by the lead researcher and dropped if the intent was not faithfully captured. Website-front: 176 items carry both annotation rounds. Pairs where either annotator’s URL is missing (12 of 176) are excluded from the agreement metrics but not from the retained pool. Items where the two rounds disagree (132) are adjudicated by the lead researcher: 112 are resolved to one round and 20 where neither round is accepted are dropped, leaving 44 agreeing plus 112 adjudicated pairs, 156 in all.

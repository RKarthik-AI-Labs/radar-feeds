Abstract

Recent advances in search agents have enabled large language models to tackle complex tasks by retrieving and synthesizing external information. However, existing search agents often lack search-state awareness, i.e., the ability to recognize what information has been obtained and what remains missing, leading to hallucinated reasoning and prematurely terminated search.
To address this limitation, we propose Self-Assessment Reinforcement Learning (SARL), a reinforcement learning framework in which models learn to recognize their own search state during training and improve their search behavior accordingly, without requiring additional assessment at test time.
SARL achieves this through a dual reward design that captures the corrective effect of each assessment while penalizing excessive reliance on it, jointly encouraging effective and efficient search trajectories.
Under this setup, SARL achieves a 24.3% relative improvement over the strongest baseline, with average gains of 35.9% on multi-hop reasoning and 27.9% on deep research benchmarks against the strongest per-benchmark baselines.
SARL further surpasses methods built on auxiliary supervision or multi-agent coordination, despite operating as a standalone RL framework with no inference-time scaffolding.
Beyond final performance, SARL maintains training stability under extended search horizons, a regime where existing methods consistently degrade, enabling sustained gains as task complexity grows.

To overcome these limitations, we propose 
Self-Assessment Reinforcement Learning (SARL)
, an RL framework that cultivates search agents with stronger search-state awareness. SARL is grounded in the premise that models can identify and correct their own erroneous trajectories when given a fresh context to review them (validated in Appendix 
A.3
).
Building on this premise, SARL extends the standard think-search-answer loop with a 
self-assessment phase
: after an initial answer, the model re-examines its own trajectory in an isolated context and produces a structured assessment that identifies the essential constraints of the question, analyzes which have been resolved by the retrieved evidence, and concludes with a judgment on whether the trajectory is complete. When the assessment indicates incompleteness, the full assessment is injected back as user feedback to prompt revision; otherwise the trajectory terminates.

To shape this behavior into an internalized capability, SARL introduces a dual reward mechanism. An 
assessment reward
 captures the corrective effect of each assessment, computed from the transition in answer correctness before and after the assessment step. An 
exponential assessment penalty
 bounds assessment frequency, encouraging thorough searching upfront rather than repeated reliance on assessment retries. Together, these signals push the model to correct mistakes when necessary while progressively internalizing search-state awareness, leading to accurate search behavior without explicit assessment steps at inference time.

We evaluate SARL across ten benchmarks spanning three complexity levels: three single-hop question answering (QA), four multi-hop QA, and three deep research benchmarks. To ensure fair comparison, we train all methods on a unified 45K-sample training set under a fixed maximum of 32 turns.
SARL achieves a 24.3% relative improvement over the strongest baseline, with average gains of 35.9% on multi-hop reasoning and 27.9% on deep research benchmarks against the strongest per-benchmark baselines.
SARL further surpasses methods from broader paradigms, including SFT+RL pipelines and multi-agent frameworks with larger backbones, despite operating as a standalone RL framework with no inference-time scaffolding. Our analysis further reveals that SARL progressively learns to identify and fill information gaps through diverse search queries with notably higher evidence recall.

We summarize our main contributions as follows:

•

We identify 
search-state awareness
 as a core capability missing in current RL-based search agents, and surface a previously unreported failure mode of training instability under extended search horizons.

•

We propose 
SARL
, an RL framework that internalizes search-state awareness through a train-time self-assessment phase shaped by a dual reward mechanism, requiring no auxiliary supervision, multi-agent orchestration, or inference-time overhead.

•

Across ten benchmarks spanning three complexity levels, SARL achieves a 24.3% average improvement over the strongest baseline with stable training and strong generalizability across model scales and families.

3.2 
Main Results

Table 
1
 presents the overall performance across our benchmark suite. SARL achieves an average score of 0.429, a 24.3% relative improvement over the strongest baseline (ASearcher: 0.345). Despite only NQ and HotpotQA overlapping with our training data, SARL demonstrates consistent gains across all benchmarks, with peak improvements of 58.1% on Musique and 48.3% on FRAMES against the best per-benchmark baselines, suggesting that SARL learns generalizable search strategies that transfer to unseen task distributions.

We further compare performance gains across three task categories, measured against the strongest per-benchmark baseline within each category. While SARL achieves modest gains of 3.9% on single-hop QA, the gains become substantially more pronounced on complex reasoning scenarios, with average gains of 35.9% on multi-hop QA and 27.9% on deep research tasks. This performance pattern indicates that SARL’s self-assessment mechanism is particularly effective for tasks requiring multiple rounds of information retrieval. As tasks grow more complex and demand iterative refinement of search queries based on accumulated knowledge, the ability to assess what information has been obtained and what remains missing becomes increasingly critical for both efficiency and accuracy.

We further observe a previously unreported failure mode in existing methods under extended search horizons. With the maximum search turn limit increased to 32 and longer-trajectory synthetic datasets included in training, both AutoRefine and ReSeek exhibit severe training instability and performance degradation, with AutoRefine collapsing to fewer than one search turn on average and ReSeek’s validation scores dropping toward zero late in training. This stems from an architectural choice: both methods generate reflection text inline at every turn, making them vulnerable to context growth and training collapse over long-horizon trajectories. SARL avoids this failure mode by decoupling the trajectory from the self-assessment step and applying fine-grained rewards to the assessment, enabling stable scaling to long search horizons (Figure 
3
; mechanism and training curves in Appendix 
A.8
 and Appendix 
A.5
). We analyze reward modeling effects in Section 
4.2
.

For broader context, we additionally reference published results from RAG-R1, a two-stage SFT+RL pipeline, and MA-RAG, a training-free multi-agent framework. SARL achieves a 23.4% relative improvement over RAG-R1 on the seven shared benchmarks (0.554 vs. 0.449 average), demonstrating that SARL outperforms pipelines that combine SFT warmup with RL. Against MA-RAG, SARL substantially outperforms the 8B variant on multi-hop reasoning (HotpotQA: 0.614 vs. 0.403; 2Wiki: 0.645 vs. 0.318) and remains ahead even of the 70B variant on these benchmarks (0.614 vs. 0.507; 0.645 vs. 0.431), indicating that internalizing search-state awareness through training can be more effective than scaling backbone size or inference-time orchestration. 4.1 
Search Diversity and Evidence Recall

A key factor underlying the effectiveness of search agents is their ability to actively explore missing information and retrieve relevant evidence. To examine how this behavior evolves during training, we analyze both the diversity of search queries and the relevance of retrieved documents at different training stages.

We first study the diversity of search behavior by measuring the number of unique search queries generated over training, which filters out repeated or redundant queries. As shown in Figure 
3
(a), SARL exhibits a clear and consistent increase in the number of unique search queries as training progresses, reaching an average of over six distinct queries toward the end of training. This trend is highly consistent across NQ, HotpotQA, and ASearcher, and becomes more pronounced for datasets requiring more complex reasoning. These results indicate that SARL progressively learns to explore missing information more actively rather than prematurely terminating search. In contrast, AutoRefine and ReSeek show a declining trend in unique search queries over training, suggesting that existing RL-based methods fail to encourage sustained exploration and instead converge toward increasingly repetitive or shallow search behavior.

We next analyze the relevance of retrieved documents by tracking document recall throughout training. As illustrated in Figure 
3
(b), SARL steadily improves recall across datasets, demonstrating its growing ability to retrieve relevant evidence before producing a final answer. This improvement is particularly notable on NQ and HotpotQA, where recall increases monotonically over training. Although the gains on BrowseComp-Plus are more modest, this dataset presents substantially higher difficulty, with target evidence being sparsely distributed and weakly aligned with surface-level queries. Importantly, SARL still maintains a consistent trend, indicating that it continues to search for relevant information even under challenging long-horizon settings. In contrast, AutoRefine and ReSeek exhibit largely static recall curves across all datasets, and perform especially poorly on BrowseComp-Plus, where they rarely retrieve relevant evidence. This suggests that their training objectives do not generalize well to complex tasks that require sustained, multi-step information seeking.

The improvements in both search diversity and evidence recall stem from SARL’s ability to recognize its own search state. In Appendix 
A.9
, we directly evaluate this capability by analyzing the agent’s reasoning trajectories, finding that SARL exhibits notably higher search-state awareness than existing baselines. This connection reinforces our central premise: by learning to recognize when retrieved evidence is insufficient, the model is naturally driven to issue more diverse queries and recover more relevant evidence.

4.2 
Effect of Assessment Penalty and Reward

We conduct an ablation study to isolate the contributions of the key components of SARL. We compare three variants: (1) Self-Assessment only, (2) Self-Assessment with assessment penalty, and (3) full SARL with both assessment penalty and reward. Figure 
4
 reports validation performance, unique search cost, and document recall across training progress.

As shown in Figure 
4
, incorporating both the assessment penalty and the assessment reward yields the strongest and most stable improvements across all metrics. In contrast, training with self-assessment alone leads to instability, with performance peaking mid-training and then sharply degrading toward the end. This behavior suggests that self-assessment signals by themselves are insufficient to guide effective long-horizon search, and may even encourage premature termination or degenerate search behavior when not properly constrained.

Introducing an assessment penalty substantially mitigates this issue, indicating that penalizing reliance on assessments plays a critical role in encouraging the model to construct complete search trajectories prior to negative assessments. Furthermore, the assessment reward provides a complementary learning signal that directly incentivizes productive use of self-assessment. As shown in the number of unique searches and recall, the full SARL model exhibits a marked improvement in both unique search queries and document recall at later training stages, with over a twofold increase in unique searches compared to the self-assessment-only variant. These gains translate into consistent improvements in final evaluation performance. Together, these results demonstrate that fine-grained rewards based on how the model’s own assessments affect search outcomes are essential for encouraging sustained exploration of missing information and for learning effective search strategies.

4.3 
Generalizability across Model Scale and Family

To assess whether SARL’s gains transfer beyond the Qwen2.5-7B backbone, we evaluate two additional configurations: Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (different model family, similar scale) and Qwen2.5-14B (same family, larger scale). For both, we report validation scores at training steps 100, 200, and 300, evaluated on 500 held-out examples per dataset from NQ, HotpotQA, and ASearcher. Per-step results are provided in Appendix 
A.11
.

Cross-family generalization.

SARL transfers effectively to a different model family. While GRPO-trained Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct collapses over training (52.3 
 51.3 
 44.4), SARL exhibits the opposite trajectory, with its average score increasing steadily from 51.2 to 54.3. By step 300, SARL outperforms GRPO on every benchmark, with a +9.9 gap in average score. This contrast illustrates that the assessment-driven training signal stabilizes long-horizon RL training across model families, whereas standard outcome-based RL is prone to performance collapse on a different backbone.

Scale generalization.

We further compare SARL with the same Qwen2.5 backbone at 7B and 14B parameters. The 14B variant outperforms the 7B variant at every training step and on every benchmark. Although the gap narrows from +6.4 average points at step 100 to +3.6 points at step 300, the 14B variant maintains a consistent advantage throughout training. The improvement is most pronounced on ASearcher (+5.6 at step 300), which involves the longest search trajectories among the three benchmarks.

Related Work

Retrieval-Augmented Generation

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) mitigates the inherent limitations of LLMs by conditioning text generation on externally retrieved evidence 
(
Lewis et al., 2020
; 
Guu et al., 2020
)
. While standard pipelines follow a single-step retrieve-then-generate flow, recent advances introduce multi-document aggregation 
(
Izacard and Grave, 2021
)
 and 
self-controlled
 approaches that decide when to retrieve and reflect on retrieved evidence, via prompted reasoning traces 
(
Yao et al., 2022
)
, supervised reflection tokens 
(
Asai and others, 2024
)
, or learned tool-use policies 
(
Schick et al., 2023
; 
Nakano and others, 2021
)
. These methods improve factual grounding when relevant evidence is accessible, but static or shallowly iterative retrieval often becomes brittle when evidence is scattered across many sources or requires sustained clarification 
(
Yang et al., 2018
; 
Trivedi et al., 2022
; 
Trivedi et al., 2023
)
.

Internalizing Search-State Awareness through RL

Search agents formulate information seeking as a sequential decision problem, where the model iteratively decides when to search, what to query, and when to conclude over long interaction trajectories. Recent works increasingly use RL to optimize these policies based on outcome feedback, as in Search-R1 
(
Jin et al., 2025
)
 and related approaches 
(
Li et al., 2025c
; 
Gao et al., 2025a
)
. Beyond short-horizon QA, growing interest in “deep research” behaviors has motivated large-scale training recipes for long-horizon web interaction 
(
Tongyi DeepResearch Team et al., 2025
; 
Qiao et al., 2025
; 
Tao et al., 2025
; 
Liu et al., 2025a
; 
Lu et al., 2025
)
. However, training on final outcomes often fails to ensure reliable trajectories: agents may reach correct answers through non-faithful intermediate reasoning, premature termination, or unsupported assumptions, a form of reward hacking and weak credit assignment under sparse final rewards 
(
Xu et al., 2025
; 
Zhang et al., 2026
; 
Yu et al., 2026
)
.

To mitigate reward sparsity, prior work introduces SFT-warmup pipelines such as RAG-R1 
(
Tan et al., 2026
)
, which bootstrap tool use through curated or synthesized trajectories before applying RL, and multi-agent frameworks such as MA-RAG 
(
Thang Nguyen, 2025
)
, which delegate planning, retrieval, and synthesis to specialized agents at inference time. Other lines introduce intermediate or process rewards to encourage auxiliary behaviors such as refinement, evidence quality, or faithfulness 
(
Shi et al., 2025
; 
Li et al., 2025b
)
, or improve long-horizon optimization through planning anchors, tree-based rollouts 
(
Yu et al., 2026
; 
Ji et al., 2025
)
, and explicit context or memory management 
(
Sun et al., 2025
; 
Qian et al., 2026
; 
Li et al., 2025e
)
.

In contrast, SARL treats self-assessment as an 
internal
 training signal within a single policy, learned through outcome-based credit assignment rather than prompting heuristics or supervised reflection demonstrations. SARL rewards measurable changes that follow from an assessment step (e.g., correcting an incorrect answer) and penalizes excessive reliance on assessments, encouraging trajectory repair when necessary while progressively internalizing knowledge-aware search behavior. This design enables accurate, efficient search to emerge from RL alone, without additional long-horizon data, auxiliary judges, or inference-time orchestration.

6 
Conclusion

We introduce Self-Assessment Reinforcement Learning (SARL), a framework that internalizes search-state awareness within retrieval-augmented LLMs through a reward design that balances corrective assessment rewards with efficiency penalties. Across ten benchmarks, SARL consistently outperforms baselines under fixed training conditions, with particularly strong gains on complex tasks: 35.9% on multi-hop QA and 27.9% on deep research benchmarks. These improvements stem from the model’s progressively internalized ability to identify information gaps and refine retrieval, reflected in a twofold increase in unique search queries and notably higher document recall. By shifting search-state awareness from a token-level prompted process into an internalized reasoning capacity, SARL offers a path toward knowledge-aware agents that self-correct without reliance on external feedback at inference time.

A.1 
Limitations

While SARL demonstrates effective performance across a broad range of search-intensive tasks, several limitations remain.

Reliance on base model capability.

SARL depends on the base model’s ability to recognize when retrieved evidence is sufficient to support a candidate answer, an assumption that does not hold universally. Our central premise experiments (Appendix 
A.3
) demonstrate that this judgment is reliable enough to provide an effective training signal at the 7B scale, allowing SARL to retain both efficiency and effectiveness without incurring the overhead of an external evaluator model as in RLVR-style methods. Investigating whether incorporating a more powerful external model can yield further gains is a promising direction for future work.

Focus on search-intensive tasks.

Our evaluation focuses exclusively on search-augmented question answering. While this scope is among the broadest within the search agent literature, demonstrating effectiveness on long-horizon deep research benchmarks even at the 7B scale, SARL’s applicability to other domains such as code generation, mathematical reasoning, or general agentic planning remains an open question.

Compatibility with broader pipelines.

We present SARL as a standalone RL framework to isolate the contribution of self-assessment as a training signal. Whether SARL combines synergistically with broader pipelines, such as SFT-warmup or multi-agent orchestration, remains to be explored, and we leave this investigation to future work.

A.3 
Validating the Central Premise of SARL

The motivation for SARL rests on the premise that models can meaningfully identify and correct their own erroneous trajectories when observed from a separate context. We empirically validate this premise through two controlled experiments.

Experiment 1: Foundational capability in base models.

We first verify that the base model possesses the foundational capability for self-assessment before any SARL training. We collect incorrect (and therefore incomplete) trajectories generated by Search-R1 on our validation set, and prompt Qwen2.5-7B with the self-assessment prompt (Appendix 
A.12
). The base model correctly identifies 
73.2%
 of flawed trajectories as 
INCOMPLETE
, demonstrating that the capability exists prior to training and providing a meaningful starting point for SARL to build upon.

To determine whether SARL’s improvement stems from genuine error identification rather than spurious factors (e.g., simply allowing extra search turns), we replace the model’s self-assessment with randomized feedback (
“The search process is 
INCOMPLETE
/
COMPLETE
”
) under otherwise identical training conditions.

We provide setup details and per-benchmark scores for the broader-paradigm comparison referenced in Section 
3.2
.

RAG-R1.

RAG-R1 combines supervised fine-tuning on Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct-generated trajectories with GRPO-based RL, optionally extended with multi-query parallelism (RAG-R1-mq). It uses the instruct variant of our backbone model (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct), a later version of our search corpus (2019 Wikipedia dump), a larger retriever (BGE-large-en-v1.5 
(
Xiao et al., 2023
)
), and the same evaluation metric.

MA-RAG.

MA-RAG orchestrates four agents (Planner, Step Definer, Extractor, QA) through chain-of-thought prompting, without any model fine-tuning. It uses the Llama3-Instruct family (8B, 70B), the same search corpus, a larger retriever (gte-multilingual-base 
(
Zhang et al., 2024
)
), and the same evaluation metric. Note that MA-RAG uses the KILT 
(
Petroni et al., 2021
)
 subsets of NQ, TriviaQA, HotpotQA, and 2WikiMultiHopQA for evaluation.

Discussion.

On the seven shared benchmarks with RAG-R1, SARL achieves an average of 0.554 versus 0.449 for RAG-R1-sq and 0.457 for RAG-R1-mq, despite using only RL training without the SFT warmup that RAG-R1 relies on. The gap is most pronounced on multi-hop reasoning (HotpotQA: +0.122 over RAG-R1-sq, +0.119 over RAG-R1-mq; 2Wiki: +0.125 / +0.082; Musique: +0.085 / +0.080), and persists on out-of-domain benchmarks such as Bamboogle (+0.108 / +0.108) where RAG-R1 has no training exposure. This pattern indicates that the gains stem not from data overlap but from how the training signal shapes search behavior across complex queries.

Against MA-RAG, SARL substantially outperforms the 8B variant on multi-hop benchmarks and remains ahead even of the 70B variant on these same benchmarks (HotpotQA: 0.614 vs. 0.507; 2Wiki: 0.645 vs. 0.431), despite a 10
 smaller backbone and no inference-time agent orchestration. On TriviaQA, MA-RAG (Llama3-8B) achieves 0.826 versus our 0.762. This gap is consistent with backbone parametric knowledge differences: without retrieval, Llama3-8B-Instruct scores 0.707 on TriviaQA versus 0.523 for Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, indicating that the TriviaQA gap reflects the underlying model’s factual recall rather than search capability. Across the remaining benchmarks, SARL’s gains over both methods are most pronounced on multi-hop reasoning, consistent with the diagnostic findings in Section 
3.2
 that self-assessment is most beneficial when iterative refinement of search queries is required.

As shown in Table

A.9 
Evaluation of Search-State Awareness

Figure 6
: 

Comparison of Search-State Awareness (SSA) levels across SARL and baseline frameworks. SARL achieves a Correct SSA of 42%, compared to 24% for Search-R1 and 17% for AutoRefine and ReSeek.

In Section 
1
, we identified search-state awareness as a core capability missing in current RL-based search agents, motivating two failure modes we observe in practice: hallucinated reasoning and prematurely terminated search. Here we directly evaluate whether SARL improves search-state awareness relative to existing baselines, providing the empirical comparison referenced in the introduction.

We sample 100 reasoning trajectories from HotpotQA, 2WikiMultiHopQA, Musique, and Bamboogle, and use GPT-5.2 
(
OpenAI, 2025b
)
 to evaluate each trajectory along two criteria: (1) Information Recognition, whether the agent accurately recognizes what information has already been obtained; and (2) Gap Identification, whether the agent correctly identifies what specific information remains missing. The full evaluation prompt is provided in Appendix 
A.12
. Based on these criteria, we categorize each trajectory into one of three levels:

•

Correct SSA
: The agent satisfies both criteria and provides the correct final answer.

•

Hallucinated SSA
: The agent satisfies both criteria but provides an incorrect answer.

•

Incorrect SSA
: The agent fails to satisfy at least one criterion, indicating a lack of awareness regarding its search state.

The results in Figure 
6
 show that SARL outperforms all baselines on search-state awareness. SARL achieves a Correct SSA rate of 42%, compared to 24% for Search-R1 and 17% for both AutoRefine and ReSeek. The gap is similarly reflected in Incorrect SSA, where SARL fails on 51% of trajectories versus 65–76% for baselines, indicating that SARL reduces cases where the agent searches without recognizing what it has obtained or what remains missing. Notably, AutoRefine and ReSeek (both of which incorporate explicit refinement or judgment tokens during training) exhibit higher Incorrect SSA than Search-R1, suggesting that auxiliary reflection tokens alone do not internalize search-state awareness.

We note that the Correct SSA metric is stringent, requiring the agent to simultaneously recognize obtained information, identify missing gaps, and produce the correct final answer; even partial failures count as Incorrect SSA. The substantial gap between SARL and baselines under this strict criterion empirically supports our central premise that SARL’s training objective produces meaningfully better search-state awareness, while leaving room for further improvement.

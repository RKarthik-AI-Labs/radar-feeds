Abstract

Accurate click-through rate (CTR) prediction depends on user representations
that capture both persistent preferences and recent intent, yet processing
extensive behavioral histories for every ranking request is costly under
production compute and latency constraints. We present X-split ARGUS, a
framework for ad ranking whose history compressor and online ranker are
trained end to end. The compressor encodes past interactions into a compact
set of tokens that are computed offline and reused across candidates, while
the online ranker scores ads through cross-attention to these tokens and
representations of recent events. Training combines an autoregressive loss for
click feedback with distillation of candidate-specific click logits from a
larger teacher that scores each candidate using the uncompressed history. The
number of compressed history tokens and the length of recent history can be
adjusted to fit serving compute and latency budgets. Offline evaluation shows
a 0.81% relative reduction in normalized entropy (NE) compared with the
production baseline. Deployment in a large-scale real-time bidding system
increases online inference cost by 15% relative to the production baseline.
An online A/B test against this baseline yields relative gains of

4.65%
 in a business metric reflecting the monetary value of
conversions generated for advertisers and 
2.96%
 in clicks.

1. Introduction

Accurate click-through rate (CTR) prediction requires user representations that
capture persistent preferences, respond to recent intent, and retain behavioral
signals relevant to each candidate ad. Longer histories can provide additional
evidence for these predictions, and recent sequential models demonstrate the
benefits of scaling behavioral context and model capacity 
[
1
, 
2
]
.
However, encoding extensive histories for each ranking request increases GPU
computation and memory traffic, while processing that depends on the candidate can limit reuse across ads. At production request rates, these costs must fit strict
compute and response latency budgets, making the representation of historical
context a central deployment decision.

Existing systems already address important parts of this problem.
TransAct 
[
3
]
 and LLaTTE 
[
4
]
 combine precomputed user
representations with online processing of recent behavior, while
VISTA 
[
5
]
 learns reusable history summaries for candidate-aware
scoring. TM20K 
[
6
]
 further combines sequence compression with
supervision from a teacher that retains the full history. These approaches
motivate a concrete question for our deployment: how much of a teacher’s predictive benefit from uncompressed history can be retained when past interactions are represented by a compact token set that does not depend on the candidate?
We study this question for a jointly trained compressor and ranker, with
explicit control over the history processed online.

We present 
X-split ARGUS
, a framework that uses autoregressive
feedback prediction, as in ARGUS 
[
7
]
, while changing the
interface between history modeling and candidate scoring. ARGUS uses
two-tower fine-tuning to support scoring with precomputed user and item
representations. In X-split ARGUS, a history compressor instead produces a set
of tokens without conditioning on the candidate, and an online ranker scores
ads through cross-attention to these tokens and representations of recent
events. The compressor and ranker are trained end to end, while the history
tokens are precomputed offline for reuse during serving. Their number and the
length of recent history determine the amount of history processed online.

Observed click feedback and click logits for individual candidates from a
larger teacher jointly supervise the compressor and online ranker. The teacher
scores ads using the uncompressed history. The distillation objective reaches the history
compressor through the online ranker, allowing observed feedback and teacher
predictions to shape the information retained in the summaries.

For online evaluation, we use 
Ad Conversion Value
 (ACV), our primary
internal metric for accepting advertising A/B experiments. ACV reflects
the monetary value of advertiser conversions delivered at the same advertising
budget, capturing the benefit of improved conversion delivery.

Our main contributions are as follows:

•

Joint offline–online history modeling.

We introduce a jointly trained history compressor and candidate-aware
ranker that combines reusable history tokens with recent events.
The number of summary tokens and the length of recent history control the amount of history processed online.

•

Training and evaluation under history compression.

We combine autoregressive prediction losses with supervision from the click logits of a teacher using uncompressed history and evaluate the predictive quality retained by the compressed student. In the main offline comparison,
X-split ARGUS reduces normalized entropy (NE) by 0.81% relative to
Context-aware ARGUS and retains over 70% of the teacher’s improvement
over the same baseline.

•

Production deployment and measured impact.

We deploy X-split ARGUS on an advertising surface of our real-time bidding
system handling roughly a hundred thousand requests per second.
The model meets the existing latency budget using 15% more online
GPUs of the same type to serve the same traffic. An online A/B test
against the production baseline yields relative gains of 
4.65%
 in
ACV and 
2.96%
 in clicks.

2. Related Work

2.1 Sequential Modeling in Recommender Systems

Early attention-based sequential recommenders established complementary
learning objectives: SASRec 
[
8
]
 predicts the next item using causal
self-attention, BERT4Rec 
[
9
]
 reconstructs masked items from
bidirectional context, and S
3
-Rec 
[
10
]
 learns associations among
attributes, items, and subsequences through self-supervised pretraining.
For CTR prediction, DIN 
[
11
]
 makes history aggregation dependent on the
candidate through a local activation mechanism. This distinction between
learning a reusable user state and extracting information for a particular
candidate remains central to modern ranking architectures.

HSTU 
[
1
]
 scales retrieval and ranking as sequential transduction using
pointwise aggregated attention and multiplicative feature transformations.
ARGUS 
[
7
]
 combines autoregressive feedback and next-item prediction
with impression-aware two-tower fine-tuning for offline representation
computation. X-split ARGUS exposes multiple history tokens to an online ranker
that conditions its predictions on each candidate. Kunlun 
[
2
]

addresses compute allocation through hierarchical seed pooling and

Computation Skip
, which alternates self-attention and summarization
modules across layers.

Industrial rankers also differ in how they combine history and nonsequential
features. WHALE 
[
12
]
 connects Wukong feature interactions to HSTU
sequence states through attention; MTGR 
[
13
]
 retains engineered cross
features in candidate tokens within an encoder based on HSTU; and
OneTrans 
[
14
]
 processes sequential and nonsequential tokens in a
unified backbone. HyFormer 
[
15
]
 alternates attention from global
queries to history representations with interactions among the queries.
These designs concern the interaction between history and ranking features;
the timing and reuse of history computation introduce an additional serving
choice.

Sequential modeling also supports generative retrieval, which predicts
item identifiers from user histories. TIGER 
[
16
]

generates semantic item identifiers, while Gryphon 
[
17
]
 shares a
history encoder between identifier generation and scoring individual items.
These methods illustrate the use of autoregressive history representations
across retrieval and ranking; our focus is the prediction of click feedback
for supplied candidate ads under online serving constraints.

2.2 Efficient Modeling of Long Histories

Systems for long histories differ in what they retain, when they compute it, and how
candidates access it. Event selection, representation compression, and
computation reuse can coexist within the same architecture. Particularly
relevant to our setting, TransAct 
[
3
]
 combines user embeddings computed in batches with a Transformer conditioned on the candidate to process recent actions.
LLaTTE 
[
4
]
 scales an asynchronous user model whose cached
representations augment a compact online ranker with fresh events and ad
context. Both establish the use of separate paths for historical context and
recent intent.

VISTA 
[
5
]
 learns summary tokens for downstream candidate attention
and caches these tokens for reuse. Its reported variants include joint
training with HSTU and an auxiliary objective for sequence reconstruction.
SITA 
[
18
]
 organizes stored interest tokens through semantic identifiers
and uses each candidate’s identifiers to select tokens before target attention.
Our design uses cross-attention from candidates to learned history summaries and representations of recent events. A teacher using uncompressed history supervises the jointly trained compressor and ranker. We study the quality
retained by this combination under production constraints.

Systems using retrieval retain selected events or historical embeddings.
SIM 
[
19
]
 retrieves a subsequence relevant to the candidate before applying
finer target attention, and TWIN 
[
20
]
 aligns the relevance measures of
these two stages. TransAct V2 
[
21
]
 selects nearest neighbors from
lifelong, recent, and impression histories while explicitly keeping the
freshest actions. Representation Memento 
[
22
]
 retrieves relevant
and diverse temporally aggregated embeddings using a modified maximal
marginal relevance criterion. These approaches select context according to the candidate, controlling what is passed to the ranker.

Other methods reduce the history representation through aggregation.
TWIN V2 
[
23
]
 uses offline hierarchical clustering and cluster-aware
target attention; CAUSE 
[
24
]
 forms history tokens by category while
retaining recent events; and LONGER 
[
25
]
 combines local token merging
with cross-attention into a shorter sequence. More general attention
bottlenecks, including Perceiver’s latent array 
[
26
]
 and the learned
queries of BLIP-2’s Q-Former 
[
27
]
, provide architectural precedents for
extracting compact representations from larger inputs.

Representation reduction and computation reuse also interact within serving
requests. HELIX 
[
28
]
 constructs compressed history states independently of the candidate once per request and lets tokens conditioned on each candidate repeatedly attend to them. This execution strategy differs from computing
summaries of long histories offline, as in our deployment.

Make It Long, Keep It Fast
 
[
29
]
 uses stacked cross-attention from targets to history and batching within each request to share history encoding;
OneTrans 
[
14
]
 reuses causal sequence computations; and
MARM 
[
30
]
 caches intermediate history states for multilayer target
attention. These reuse strategies reduce repeated computation without
necessarily imposing a compact summary representation.

2.3 Distillation under Serving Constraints

Knowledge distillation supplements observed labels with teacher predictions
to train a constrained student 
[
31
]
. Ranking
Distillation 
[
32
]
 transfers a teacher’s top-ranked
unobserved items as weighted supervision, while Collaborative
Distillation 
[
33
]
 combines soft targets with
rank-aware sampling. Privileged Features
Distillation 
[
34
]
 addresses a different asymmetry:
teachers can use predictive features unavailable to the deployed student.
In our setting, historical interactions remain available to the offline
compressor; the constraint concerns their representation and processing during
online ranking.

TM20K 
[
6
]
 is a close precedent for distillation under history
compression: a teacher retains the full sequence, while students merge tokens
according to item identity, position, and layer depth. X-split ARGUS learns
summary tokens for offline precomputation and reuse alongside recent events.
Gryphon-v2 
[
35
]
 distills scores for generated and logged candidates
into both a ranking module and its shared history encoder.
Meta Lattice 
[
36
]
 combines logit supervision with asynchronously
precomputed teacher embeddings for user–item pairs, which students consume
during both training and serving. Our stored summaries do not depend on the candidate; teacher logits for individual candidates supervise their learning through the online ranker. The focus of our evaluation is the predictive quality retained by this
representation and training design under production serving constraints.

4.2 Baseline

Context-aware ARGUS is a two-tower ranker based on ARGUS 
[
7
]

whose user tower contains a history compressor and a runtime encoder,
while its item tower represents the candidate ad. It scores ads with a
dot product between the two tower outputs and therefore already models
both historical context and recent behavior. The 
Context-aware

variant separates each event into context and item components. This
separation allows the runtime user encoder to receive all available
contextual information when scoring the corresponding item. At serving
time, context enters the user encoder while the candidate item is
represented by the item tower. The baseline therefore combines historical
behavior with the context available at ranking time.

5.2 History Precomputation and Storage

A daily batch pipeline collects new user events, appends them to the
retained user profiles, and runs the history compressor for users with
new events. For each such user, it publishes

states to an embedding store. The online ranker retrieves these states
and combines them with representations of recent events, as described
in Section 
3.1
. Historical representations are
therefore reused across requests, while recent behavior remains available
through the runtime user profile.

5.3 Model Freshness and Daily Fine-Tuning

To inform the update schedule, we evaluate a fixed X-split ARGUS checkpoint

the latest training event and the start of each window. User histories
and precomputed compressor embeddings are refreshed using events available

Based on this observation, we fine-tune the deployed student daily using

Daily fine-tuning requires additional GPU compute,
but this workload is small relative to continuous online inference.
The larger improvement with no gap suggests that more frequent or
online model updates could yield further gains.

6. Conclusion

We presented X-split ARGUS, a framework for CTR prediction that combines
compressed historical context with recent user behavior and candidate
scoring through cross-attention. Its history compressor and online ranker
are trained end to end using observed click feedback and distillation
from a teacher that processes the uncompressed history. Precomputed
compressor states are reused across requests, while runtime history
representations are shared across candidates, keeping extensive history
processing outside the online ranking path. In the offline comparison,
the compressed student improves normalized entropy over Context-aware
ARGUS and retains more than 70% of the uncompressed teacher’s improvement
over the same baseline.

Deployment on the largest advertising surface of our RTB system confirms
the practical value of this design. An online A/B test against the
production baseline yields relative gains of 
4.65%
 in ACV and 
2.96%
 in
clicks. X-split ARGUS serves the same traffic volume within
the existing latency budget using 15% more online GPUs of the same type.
Daily history precomputation and model fine-tuning support continued
operation, with the latter motivated by the observed decline in the
relative advantage of a fixed checkpoint. These results demonstrate that
reusable history compression and candidate attention can bring the
benefits of richer user context to production ranking within practical
latency and resource constraints.

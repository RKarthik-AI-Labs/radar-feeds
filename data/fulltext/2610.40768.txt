Abstract

The growing number of scientific submissions is straining the peer review process, while a large pool of relevant researchers remains unidentified and is therefore not considered as potential reviewers. We formulate the discovery of these researchers as a retrieval problem: given a submission, we retrieve and rank researchers based on domain similarity. We present retrieve-then-disambiguate, an open-source algorithm that identifies domain relevant researchers for a query submission by retrieving similar publications from the scientific literature and resolving ambiguous author names into unique researcher identities. We evaluate the algorithm on 
 astronomy and physics publications. We use ORCID-linked first authors, coauthors, and cited researchers as proxy labels to evaluate the recovery of researchers with known connections to each query publication. When retrieving the top 
 most similar papers, the algorithm recovers roughly 68% of first authors, 60% of coauthors, and 45% of cited researchers. Our code is available at 
https://github.com/deepthought-initiative/Retrieve-then-Disambiguate
.

The exponential rise in submissions to conferences, journals, and funding agencies has placed growing pressure on the peer review system

(

;

; 
Stelmakh et al., 2025
; 
OpenReview, 2025

. In practice, many fields across science still assign peer reviewers manually 
(
Merrifield and Saari, 2009
; 
Jerabkova et al., 2023
)
. 
Kovanis et al. (2016)
 show this manual process produces a distribution problem, not a supply problem, with a minority of researchers reviewing the majority of submissions. This highlights the need for scalable methods to identify domain relevant researchers beyond those already known to editors and program committees 
(
Merrifield and Saari, 2009
; 
Stelmakh et al., 2021
; 
Dumlao and Teplitskiy, 2025
)

Identifying and assigning peer reviewers is a multiobjective problem, involving domain similarity, conflicts of interest, career-stage, and other considerations 
(
Langfeldt, 2001
; 
Nightingale and Scott, 2007
; 
Latypova, 2023
)
. In practice, reviewer assignment systems at large conferences or scientific organizations typically involve two stages: estimating domain relevance between submissions and potential reviewers using vector representations 
(
Stelmakh et al., 2025
; 
Olivo et al., 2026
)
 and assigning reviewers using optimization algorithms based on a set of constraints 
(
Stelmakh et al., 2019
)
. The relative importance of these objectives and constraints depends on the priorities of editors and program committees. We focus on the specific challenge of identifying researchers with domain/topical similarity to a submission 
(
Mimno and McCallum, 2007
; 
Jerabkova et al., 2023
; 
Stelmakh et al., 2025
)
. Currently deployed reviewer assignment systems match or rank reviewers within a predefined pool of candidates 
(
Charlin et al., 2012
; 
OpenReview, 2025
, e.g., the Toronto Paper Matching System or OpenReview)
. Furthermore, some journals or conferences rely on proprietary reviewer recommendation systems 
1

1

https://www.elsevier.com/editor/submission-systems/find-reviewers-tips
, whose data or matching methodology is not openly published.

Therefore, we present an open source algorithm for identifying relevant reviewers directly from the scientific literature. We formulate reviewer discovery as an information retrieval problem: given a query submission, retrieve and rank researchers by domain similarity. We present the retrieve-then-disambiguate algorithm: (1) retrieve domain similar papers to the submission, (2) disambiguate author identities within the retrieved pool, and (3) rank the disambiguated identities by similarity to the submission. We leverage authorship, coauthorship, and citation relationships as observable signals of domain similarity. Although these relationships do not establish reviewer suitability, they allow us to evaluate whether a retrieval system successfully identifies researchers within the domain of the query publication. In summary, we introduce an open-source algorithm for identifying topically relevant researchers directly from the scientific literature, without relying on a predefined candidate pool. Second, we present an evaluation using astronomy and physics publications from NASA ADS, with authorship, coauthorship, and citation relationships as signals of domain relevance.

3 
Methods

Algorithm 
1
 presents the retrieve-then-disambiguate algorithm and we describe each stage below.

Stage 1: Retrieval.
 Given a query paper’s title and abstract, we retrieve the 
 most similar candidate papers. To mimic the constraints of real peer review, in which only prior work is available, we restrict candidates to papers published before the query paper. We use TF–IDF and cosine similarity to estimate similarity between candidate papers and a query paper. A single TF–IDF vectorizer is fit once over all query paper titles and abstracts 
(
Olivo et al., 2026
)
, using the scikit-learn implementation 
(
Pedregosa et al., 2018
)
 (reused in Stage 3). We set the n-gram range to capture both unigrams and bigrams, allowing the model to incorporate two word phrases (e.g., “stellar evolution,” “galaxy formation,” or “white dwarf”) that represent more domain-specific meaning than separate individual words (e.g., “white,” “dwarf”). Standard English stop words (e.g., articles, prepositions) are removed to reduce noise. We compute cosine similarity between the TF–IDF vector representations of the query and each candidate paper, and keep the top 
 candidates by similarity as the candidate pool for Stage 2.

Stage 2: Name-blocked Disambiguation.
 Every one of the 
 retrieved candidate papers’ full author lists is separated into individual author records, one row per author with metadata (i.e., author name string, title, abstract, and publication year). Author records are split into disjoint name blocks by first name initial and last name (e.g., ‘J.Smith’ or ‘Y.Wang’), following standard blocking practice in author name disambiguation 
(
Subramanian et al., 2021
)
. Within each block, we disambiguate author records following the approach of 
Olivo et al. (2025)
. Each author record is embedded by concatenating a character-level author name embedding with a SPECTER2 title/abstract embedding 
(
Cohan et al., 2020
)
, then passing those concatenated embeddings through a trained contrastive encoder to produce a final author record embedding. The embedding representations of each author record within a block are then clustered with a constrained variant of DBSCAN 
(
Ester et al., 1996
; 
Khabsa et al., 2015
)
. Each resulting cluster is treated as one disambiguated author identity. For evaluation, we assign ORCID labels to clusters by matching publication titles. An ORCID label is considered retrieved if it appears in a cluster with no conflicting ORCID labels.

Stage 3: Expertise ranking.
 We construct a disambiguated author representation by concatenating the title and abstract of every distinct paper attributed to a disambiguated cluster. Using the TF–IDF vectorizer, we transform every disambiguated author identity’s representation into this shared vector space, and rank disambiguated identities by cosine similarity to the query vector representation.

5 
Discussion and Future Work

Our results show that retrieving disambiguated authors directly from the literature surfaces domain relevant reviewers for peer review. Citation-based recall improves as the candidate pool 
 grows, indicating that a share of domain relevant reviewers sits outside what a small, fixed candidate pool can capture 
(
Kovanis et al., 2016
)
.

While we evaluate the algorithm on astronomy and physics literature, the architecture is not domain-specific: TF–IDF retrieval can be swapped for alternative representation methods, such as SPECTER2 
(
Cohan et al., 2020
)
 embeddings or more recent large language model embeddings (e.g., Qwen embeddings) 
4
4

4

https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-Embedding-4B
, and the disambiguation stage can be replaced with alternative methods 
(
Subramanian et al., 2021
)
. Applying the same pipeline to other domains and environments, such as computer science conference submissions, would only require refitting 
 and 
 to that literature, not redesigning the retrieval-then-disambiguate algorithm.

Our evaluation relies on authorship, coauthorship, and citation relationships as proxy signals for domain relevance. These relationships do not establish reviewer expertise, availability, or the absence of conflicts of interest. Furthermore, our reported hit rates measure the recovery of labeled researchers within the retrieved candidate pool, rather than the quality of a fixed-size ranked reviewer shortlist. Our evaluation is also conducted entirely on a preliminary sample of 
 astronomy and physics query papers. Therefore, our future work includes evaluating the pipeline across a larger set of the available ORCID-linked data and extending evaluation to new domains and publishing environments, particularly computer science and machine learning conference publishing (e.g., using OpenReview’s public submission and review data). Retrieving reviewers directly from the literature, rather than from a predefined pool, offers a path toward surfacing the qualified researchers who remain unknown under current reviewer assignment practices and systems.

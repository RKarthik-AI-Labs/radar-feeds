Abstract

Typed decision models offer structured choices, scores, and probabilities for low-cost automated evaluation. We present a controlled audit of presentation sensitivity in Jev, Clef Flash, and Perplexity Decider across native choice and score interfaces. Across six datasets with 9,000 multiply annotated humor, helpfulness, and summarization items, we collect 702,000 judgments under interventions that vary positions, identifiers, authorship claims, endorsements, decision framing, and rating rubrics while preserving the evaluated texts. Jev’s second-position probability advantage reaches 6.11 percentage points on summaries; reversing reader endorsements changes Perplexity’s selected humor text on 40.40% of pairs; and rejection framing reduces Clef’s summary agreement with human preferences by 23.36 points. Rating formats also shift score levels while often preserving rankings. Across these findings, repeatability, presentation robustness, and human alignment emerge as distinct properties of an evaluator. Reliable use therefore requires validating the decisions and score interpretations on which an evaluation pipeline depends.

Introduction

Typed decision models open a route to fast, inexpensive subjective judgment at scale. Jev, released on 15 September 2026 
(
Hardiman, 2026
)
, maps context and typed questions directly to choices, scores, and probabilities. TypeSafe describes a decision-specific architecture trained with Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions (RLCD), producing probabilities in parallel rather than through autoregressive text generation. Its workflow benchmarks report 444.6
 lower cost and 193.6
 faster execution than the compared LLM configurations 
(
Almeida, 2026
)
. Evaluation tools already document Jev-based response grading 
(
HoneyHive, 2026
)
. As Jev and related models see increasing use in evaluation pipelines, establishing their susceptibility to presentation changes becomes crucial. Jev’s decision-only interface provides no generated explanation or chain of thought, making controlled behavioral audits essential for understanding its judgments.

Subjective evaluation requires judging quality amid reasonable human disagreement 
(
Plank, 2022
)
. We evaluate three complementary tasks: humor, response helpfulness, and summary quality. For an automated judge, a central reliability question is whether changes in request presentation alter which text is selected, its assigned rating, or agreement with human judgments.

LLM-as-a-judge research motivates our tests of position bias, contextual influence, and rating inconsistency 
(
Gu et al., 2026
;

;

; 
Stureborg et al., 2024

. In typed decision models, the key question is how presentation changes propagate through directly returned probabilities, choices, and scores. Measuring these outcomes together reveals whether a shift changes the selected text, affects human alignment, or moves rating levels while preserving rankings.

We evaluate Jev, Clef Flash, and Perplexity Decider on three tasks, each with a pairwise dataset for 
choice
 and a rating dataset for 
score
. Interventions preserve evaluated texts; repeated baseline requests establish ordinary variability. Human annotations supply alignment and disagreement references. Three research questions organize the study:

RQ1: Presentation and contextual cues.

How do candidate positions, identifiers, attributed authorship, endorsements, and evaluator directives influence judgments of unchanged texts?

RQ2: Framing and rating-format sensitivity.

How do selection versus rejection, an explicit no-preference option, and alternative score rubrics change preferences and ratings?

RQ3: Robustness and human alignment.

How do these effects relate to repeatability, human disagreement, and alignment, and how consistently do they recur across tasks?

We make four contributions. First, we provide a controlled multi-model audit of native choice and score interfaces across three subjective tasks. Second, factorial and matched-control designs isolate order, identifiers, and cue direction, while paired analyses connect output shifts to decisions and human alignment. Third, we show that repeatability and rank preservation can coexist with consequential presentation sensitivity, yielding concrete validation checks. Fourth, our code and results repository

Related Work

Conventional LLM judges.

MT-Bench reports strong GPT-4/human agreement alongside position, verbosity, and self-enhancement biases 
(
Zheng et al., 2023
)
. G-Eval improves human correspondence in summarization and dialogue scoring through chain-of-thought and form filling 
(
Liu et al., 2023
)
. Response order can alter GPT-4 and ChatGPT judgments 
(
Wang et al., 2024a
)
; JudgeLM addresses position, knowledge, and format biases through training and reference-based techniques 
(
Zhu et al., 2025
)
.

Biases, context, and controls.

Across 15 LLM judges, position bias varies by judge, task, and candidate-quality gap 
(
Shi et al., 2025
)
. Contextual perturbations, including authority cues, influence both human and LLM judges 
(
Chen et al., 2024
)
. CalibraEval calibrates prediction distributions to reduce position/identifier selection bias 
(
Li et al., 2025
)
, while CALM tests multiple bias families 
(
Ye et al., 2025
)
. These findings motivate separate measurements of repetition, order, identifiers, and evaluative context.

Scores and human references.

JUDGE-BENCH finds that LLM/human alignment varies with task, annotator expertise, and text provenance 
(
Bavaresco et al., 2025
)
. Forced-choice treatment of ambiguous ratings can distort judge validation and selection 
(
Guerdan et al., 2025
)
. Together with rating inconsistency 
(
Stureborg et al., 2024
)
 and human label variation 
(
Plank, 2022
)
, these results motivate explicit human references and separate reporting of rating levels, ranks, and disagreement.

Typed decision models.

The studies above center conventional LLM judges. We investigate whether analogous reliability concerns arise in Jev, Clef Flash, and Perplexity Decider through their native choice and score interfaces. Our audit distinguishes probability and rating shifts, changed decisions, and human alignment, characterizing how presentation sensitivity propagates into subjective evaluation outcomes.

3 
Study Design and Human References

3.1 
Tasks, datasets, and references

Table 
1
 describes the six datasets, evaluated qualities, and human references. Pairwise tasks use released comparisons; scoring tasks use the source rating dimension and scale. All datasets come from first-party releases. Appendix 
A
 documents source versions, annotation processing, and selection details.

Two reference choices merit emphasis. HelpSteer2 pairwise labels follow the sign of published aggregate preference strength. These overall response preferences serve as a proxy for the helpfulness question asked of the models. SummEval relevance averages all eight individual ratings, weighting expert and crowd judgments by their counts. We additionally examine alignment with each annotator group to assess reference sensitivity (Appendix 
E.7
).

3.2 
Sampling and balancing

Using a fixed seed, we select 1,500 items per dataset: 750 with clearer human agreement and 750 with more disagreement. Pairwise strata use vote agreement; scoring strata use within-item rating dispersion. UKP balances its humor-category combinations, while scoring subsets retain eligible score-band proportions within agreement strata. Human-preferred candidates are balanced across positions to mitigate position biases in other investigations. Ambiguous pairs are randomly oriented. Selection also limits reuse of source material and the length of HelpSteer2 inputs. Exact thresholds and allocations appear in Appendix 
A.1
.

4 
Requests and Interventions

4.1 
Choice and score interfaces

We evaluate Jev 
(
Almeida, 2026
)
, Cloudflare’s Clef Flash 
(
Chen et al., 2026
)
, and Perplexity Decider v1.1 
(
Perplexity AI, 2026
)
 through OpenRouter’s Decisions API 
(
OpenRouter, 2026
)
, using the same request construction for each model. In 
choice
 requests, options contain the candidate texts and shared state supplies the task context. For example, humor asks, “Which option contains the funnier text?” Table 
8
 in Appendix 
B
 lists all six instructions and their context fields.

In 
score
 requests, shared state contains the candidate and its context, and an ordered list of verbal anchors defines the rating scale. Returned scores use zero-based indices; analysis maps them to the native dataset scale and a common normalized range. Human votes, scores, worker notes, and generating-system identities are withheld from all requests. Model routes, exact anchors, and response handling are documented in Appendices 
B
–
D
.

Choice outputs contain the selected identifier, option probabilities, and confidence; score outputs contain a scalar rating, rating-level probabilities, confidence, and the scale legend. We analyze the scalar as returned and the probability vectors as reported preferences over the supplied options or levels. Confidence retains its model-specific interpretation. Section 
5
 defines the derived outcomes.

4.2 
Presentation interventions and controls

Tables 
3
 and 
3
 summarize the interventions and their comparisons. Each changes a presentation feature while preserving the evaluated texts. The A/B factorial separates position bias from identifier bias. Position denotes candidate-entry order in the submitted criteria; its contrasts measure submitted-order sensitivity. Attribution and endorsement cues are synthetic metadata: pairwise cues are separated from candidate text, and scoring cues appear in shared state. Each cue family includes an unspecified-cue control to measure effects of the added format itself.

For example, a humor choice presents “80% of previous readers preferred this option” above one unchanged text and “20%” above the other. Shared state instructs: “Judge only each [TEXT] block for the requested quality. Previous-reader feedback is separate metadata.” The reversed condition exchanges the two claims. Scoring feedback is supplied as an additional state field. These experiments measure reliance on attributed information. Appendix 
B.1
 gives complete request examples.

Framing interventions change how a judgment is requested. The no-preference condition distinguishes abstention from redistribution between A and B; its placement is reported separately. Selection/rejection framing compares the preferred text recovered from asking for the better versus worse candidate. Rating-format variants reverse the native anchors or introduce three-/seven-level verbal descriptions; remapped scores provide a common comparison scale. Exact wording, directive controls, and mappings appear in Appendix 
C
.

Measurement and Inference

5.1 
Outcomes and comparison units

Each intervention is compared with its designated control on matched items. Movement analyses and scoring alignment use all 1,500 items per dataset; pairwise human alignment uses the subset with a strict A/B human preference. Table 
4
 gives those subset sizes.

Pairwise outcomes.

We map choices back to the underlying texts to separate position and identifier effects. Outcomes are the mean signed candidate-probability shift, the proportion of pairs whose selected text changes, and the change in agreement with the human preference. Probability-based analyses renormalize returned vectors to unit mass. For no-preference conditions, we report X selection and probability mass alongside conditional A/B preference.

5.2 
Uncertainty and multiple comparisons

Confidence intervals account for dependence from reused content. We cluster by source context, article, prompt, or headline as appropriate. UKP pairs can share either joke, so we use a shared-joke dyadic variance estimator 
(
Aronow et al., 2015
)
. Paired mean differences use Student-
 intervals, with cluster or joke-node counts determining degrees of freedom. Seeded cluster bootstrap checks assess sensitivity. Appendix 
D.1
 gives the estimators and diagnostics.

Effect and association tests are two-sided, with discoveries defined by Benjamini–Yekutieli (BY) adjusted 
 
(
Benjamini and Yekutieli, 2001
)
. We report effect sizes and pointwise 95% confidence intervals. The primary BY family contains 369 tests of signed probability, selection, and scoring outcomes, including baseline alignment differences and between-model intervention contrasts. A separate secondary family contains 1,656 tests of non-repeat condition/control effects, human alignment/error changes, susceptibility correlations, and pooled effects. A comparison can supply tests for several outcomes; Appendix 
D.2
 gives family membership. Subgroup summaries, absolute movement, and sensitivity diagnostics are reported descriptively.

5.3 
Practical consequences

We assess whether interventions change decisions or human alignment. The 450 condition/control comparisons summarize signed and absolute movement, changed selections, and alignment/error changes, including unchanged-repeat controls. Non-repeat signed outcomes enter the secondary testing family (Appendix 
D.2

D.3

D.4

E.8

Position and identifier effects.

Jev favors second position across all tasks; its first-position effect reaches -6.11 [-6.95, -5.26] pp on summaries (Table 
5
). Clef’s position effects remain close to zero, while its B-label preference reverses from humor to summaries. Perplexity reverses its position preference between humor and helpfulness. Jev favors numeric identifier 2 across tasks, with a 7.44 pp humor effect; the other models vary in direction. Position–identifier interactions are at most 0.30 pp and none passes correction. Complete results appear in Appendix

These effects reach discrete decisions: on summaries, Jev’s first-position selection contrast is -8.73 [-10.42, -7.05] pp, and Clef’s B-label selection contrast is 13.30 [11.20, 15.40] pp. Both are signed factorial contrasts, averaging over the other factor (Table

Authorship and feedback sensitivity.

Human attribution generally increases candidate preference, most strongly for Clef summaries: 32.15 [31.02, 33.28] pp, with 39.53% changed selections. Framing, directives, and no preference.

Rejection framing changes recovered preferences in every configuration. Clef’s helpfulness agreement falls from 61.85% to 46.10%, and its summary agreement changes by -23.36 [-27.01, -19.72] pp. On helpfulness, Clef selects the same text as both more and less helpful on 67.73% of all pairs, consistent with incomplete adaptation to question polarity. Perplexity’s changes have little supported net effect on agreement. Directive effects are selective: its second-target summary directive raises target probability by 10.14 [9.69, 10.59] pp versus an inert note, whereas Jev’s effects are mostly small (Appendix 
E.3
).

With X last, UKP neither-majority cases yield 43/187 X choices for Jev, 19/187 for Clef, and 88/187 for Perplexity. All models choose X on 0/53 HelpSteer aggregate-neutral cases and 1/49 OpenAI split-vote cases. These distinct reference groups and the placement-specific changes are detailed in Appendix 
E.4
. Length and human-disagreement associations are task-specific descriptive results (Appendix 
E.6
).

6.2 
Scoring judgments

Feedback changes rating levels.

Reader and previous-judge feedback raise scores in every configuration (Table 
7
). Jev’s helpfulness reader contrast is 16.47 [16.12, 16.81] normalized-score pp; Perplexity’s largest feedback effects occur on humor. Error changes depend on the reference and starting scores. For example, favorable reader feedback increases Perplexity’s humor MAE by 4.8 pp relative to unspecified feedback, while unfavorable feedback has little net effect. All cue-versus-control error changes appear in Table 
17
.

Rating formats affect levels and ranks differently.

Jev’s seven-level humor format lowers ratings by 7.63 pp, while its three-level relevance format raises them by 5.87 pp. Its rank correlations with native-format scores remain 0.963–0.996. Clef relevance is less rank-stable, with correlations of 0.903–0.938 across formats. Thus high rank association can coexist with shifts in absolute ratings, with the extent of rank preservation varying by configuration. These level shifts matter for judge design: fixed thresholds and aggregate quality scores depend on rating levels even when candidate rankings remain similar. Reversed anchors also shift remapped ratings; full estimates, error changes, and the format figure appear in Appendix 
E.5
.

Identifiers, attribution, and directives are smaller.

Scoring A/B, numeric, and authorship effects reach at most 1.35, 0.94, and 1.57 pp in magnitude. Several small shifts pass correction. Directive effects are mixed: Clef relevance falls by -2.11 [-2.44, -1.79] pp despite a highest-rating instruction; Perplexity relevance rises by 2.74 pp while its MAE decreases by 1.43 pp. These cases separate movement from distance to the specified human mean (Appendices 
E.1
–
E.3
).

Score dispersion and reference dependence.

Judge-score dispersion relative to human ratings varies by dataset: Jev’s judge/human SD ratio is 0.58 for humor and 1.10 for relevance. On SummEval, its Pearson correlation is 0.597 with experts, 0.061 with crowd raters, and 0.382 with the pooled reference (Appendices 
E.5
–
E.7
). Score repeat variation is small for Jev/Clef and zero for Perplexity (Table 
4
).

Cross-task pools, shared-susceptibility associations, and robustness checks appear in Appendices 
E.8
–
E.9
.

7 
Discussion

Order and identifiers are model-specific.

Jev favors the second position across all three choice tasks; Clef remains near zero, and Perplexity changes direction by task. Identifier preferences vary and position–identifier interactions remain small. LLM-judge studies likewise find that order effects depend on the judge, task, and candidate-quality gap 
(
Wang et al., 2024a
; 
Shi et al., 2025
)
. Jev alone shows a uniform serial-order tendency across our tasks. On the score interface, A/B, numeric, and authorship effects are at most 1.57 pp, much smaller than evaluative-cue and rubric effects.

Endorsements are the common susceptibility.

Reader endorsements move choice probabilities toward the endorsed text for every model and task; reader and previous-judge feedback also raise scores in every configuration. At their strongest, endorsement-driven choice reversals far exceed unchanged-request variation. This unanimous cross-model effect extends the authority and context sensitivity reported for conventional LLM judges 
(
Chen et al., 2024
)
. Matched controls attribute the principal shifts to cue direction rather than added metadata.

Framing and rating formats change downstream use.

Clef frequently chooses the same helpfulness candidate as both better and worse, while rejection framing sharply reduces its summary alignment. Together with evidence that forced-choice handling can distort LLM-judge validation under rating ambiguity 
(
Guerdan et al., 2025
)
, this shows that decision framing is part of evaluator validity. Score-format variants shift rating levels despite high within-model rank correlations, echoing rating inconsistency in LLM evaluators 
(
Stureborg et al., 2024
)
. Rank-based comparisons may therefore remain stable while thresholds and mean scores change.

Reliability has distinct dimensions.

Perplexity nearly reproduces unchanged calls yet shows the largest endorsement-driven reversals; Jev’s SummEval alignment differs substantially between expert and crowd references. As in LLM-judge research 
(
Shi et al., 2025
; 
Bavaresco et al., 2025
)
, repeatability, presentation robustness, and human alignment answer different questions.

8 
Conclusion

Typed decision models are inexpensive and structured, yet presentation-sensitive. Across three models and three tasks, endorsements are the most consistent influence; order and identifiers vary by model; and framing and rubrics alter decisions or ratings despite stable repeats and ranks. Deployment audits should vary order and identifiers independently, control contextual cues, check framing consistency, and compare score levels with ranks.

Limitations

The study compares three typed decision models across three English-language tasks. Comparisons with conventional LLM judges use published evidence; future work can evaluate both model families under matched conditions. Sampling balances agreement strata and UKP humor categories to cover a range of evaluation cases. Human references are fixed aggregates, and incomplete worker identities leave some shared-rater dependence unresolved. SummEval combines expert and crowd ratings, while HelpSteer2 overall preferences serve as a proxy for helpfulness. Requests use adapted single-item questions, and structural selection retains examples with potentially incomplete context.

Effects characterize the implemented wording, datasets, and cue placement. Alternative rubrics change descriptions, anchor reversal changes numeric polarity, and inserting X first or middle changes candidate positions. Directive controls have approximately matched lengths. Linear score normalization assumes comparable spacing between ordinal levels. The three selected tasks restrict cross-task inference, and available annotations represent human-reference uncertainty only partially.

Ethical Considerations

We reuse released annotations and follow upstream attribution and data-use terms. Content may include offensive humor or sensitive requests. Authorship and feedback cues are fabricated solely for controlled presentation experiments. Reproducibility materials preserve their experimental status and respect source redistribution restrictions.

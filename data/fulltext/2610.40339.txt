Abstract

Recent advances in video world models have enabled the generation of increasingly realistic and interactive virtual environments, opening new possibilities for player-customizable gameplay. Agentic frameworks further advance this direction by decoupling explicit game logic and controllable world representations from visual generation. However, existing approaches support only simple game settings and predefined motion patterns, generalize poorly to unseen environments and characters, and offer limited real-time interactivity. In this paper, we introduce 
Wizard
, an agentic game world model that enables real-time, precise control over character motions while generalizing zero-shot across novel scenes, characters, and actions. To achieve this, we construct a diverse dataset spanning 750 scenes and 178 characters, incorporating skeleton and geometry information as structured representations. We leverage these representations as intermediate proxies to condition a video generation model, together with an adaptive masking mechanism that improves alignment between the conditioning signals and generated video rollouts. Furthermore, we introduce an agentic framework that explicitly maintains and updates game states, allowing players to freely configure their virtual worlds and exercise fine-grained control over character motions while preserving visual quality and gameplay consistency. By combining agentic control with structured proxy-based generation, our framework unlocks new possibilities for player-driven game creation with real-time, generalizable, and mechanically consistent world simulation.

For fine-grained control over camera movement and character animation, our method represents scenes with geometric proxies encoding depth and height, and characters with articulated skeletons. Given any user-provided scene image, we estimate monocular geometry to construct an approximate traversable 2.5D environment and render view-dependent geometric control maps. Character references are converted into 3D meshes and automatically rigged into controllable skeletons, with channel coding distinguishing character instances and their camera-relative orientations. These representations form a unified visual proxy.

Building on the unified visual proxy shown in Fig. 
1
, the agentic framework then updates scene geometry according to camera inputs and animates character skeletons according to action inputs, providing precise spatial and motion control for the causal video generator. To enable diverse motions for novel characters, a vision-language model retrieves compatible skeletons and animations from a motion library based on character morphology and intended motion style. The selected motions are retargeted to the character’s skeleton, enabling natural movement and player-defined actions. Beyond visual control, the agent further maintains explicit combat states, including health, relative distance, and orientation, to enforce gameplay rules and adapt boss behavior to evolving combat situations.

To train the video model to faithfully follow these structured proxies and generalize across scenes, characters, and motions, we construct 50.3 hours of aligned proxy–video pairs from diverse sources, including data specifically designed for skeletal control. We further introduce an adaptive masking mechanism that restricts reference-image attention to the corresponding character regions, strengthening appearance alignment and preserving character identity during motion. Finally, we apply causal adaptation and few-step distillation

Related Work

2.1 
Video World Model

Recent advances in video generation have enabled action-conditioned world models that allow users to interactively explore generated environments. In games, these models translate keyboard and mouse inputs into dynamic gameplay, with emerging capabilities including NPC control in ReactiveGWM 
[

[

 and Marionette 
[
28

 decouple explicit state management and gameplay logic from visual generation. Despite these advances, existing methods often remain constrained to the game settings seen during training. In contrast, Wizard combines fine-grained skeletal control with a diverse training dataset to enable zero-shot generalization to unseen scenes, characters, and motions.

2.2 
Agentic World Model

Agentic world models decouple world-state management and action planning from visual generation, enabling explicit control over world dynamics while video models focus on rendering. Related simulation-guided approaches, such as PhysDreamer 
[

[

 and Programmable World Model 
[

[

[

To bridge this gap, Wizard extends agentic control to scene geometry and articulated skeletons, enabling flexible scene manipulation and precise character animation. A diverse, multi-source training dataset enhances generalization, while causal adaptation and few-step distillation enable real-time video generation for responsive gameplay.

3.1 
Diverse Data Sources

Gameplay Recordings.

We collect 8.8 hours of third-person combat and exploration footage from Elden Ring  
[
9
]
 to capture authentic AAA-game appearance, combat dynamics, and visual effects. Combat sequences feature six bosses with diverse body structures, capturing articulated attacks, hit reactions, and combat effects. Exploration sequences span 11 regions and more than 20 player outfits, covering varied environments, camera trajectories, and character locomotion. We record RGB frames alongside camera parameters, scene depth, and character skeletons directly from the game, ensuring accurate temporal alignment between visual observations and control signals.

Synthetic Data.

While gameplay recordings provide realistic visual dynamics, their character appearances, motion patterns, and interactions remain constrained by the original games. To broaden this coverage, we develop a procedural rendering pipeline that generates 11.6 hours of data using 133 boss and 29 player assets from Sketchfab 
[
32
]
, Quaternius 
[
30
]
, and KayKit 
[
25
]
, with diverse skeletons and animation libraries. We construct varied environments using 842 Poly Haven 
[
29
]
 assets, populate each scene with 15–30 randomly placed props, and randomize character colors, enabling scalable generation of diverse character–scene combinations. Beyond appearance diversity, we design four scenarios to strengthen control robustness: (i) novel-character entry; (ii) multi-character coexistence; (iii) camera-induced disappearance and reappearance; and (iv) rapid turns under a following camera. These scenarios encourage the model to associate each character with its corresponding skeleton and reference image, rather than a fixed appearance or screen position. Camera motion is applied to 95% of generated samples to further diversify viewpoints. Finally, we use a vision-language model to filter out invalid renders and physically implausible placements to ensure data quality.

Unreal Engine Simulations.

Furthermore, we use Unreal Engine to generate 3.3 hours of gameplay footage featuring reconstructed characters and weapons 
[
34
]
, scripted opponents, and bot-controlled players. Rendering RGB frames and geometric proxies from the same simulated state ensures precise alignment, while configurable scenes enable scalable collection of varied combat interactions and camera viewpoints.

Public Datasets.

We incorporate 26.6 hours of high-quality gameplay footage from WildWorld 
[
19
]
, complementing our collected data with diverse creature morphologies and motion patterns. Its synchronized camera, skeletal, and depth annotations are converted into our unified proxy representation, preserving control–video alignment while broadening the coverage of articulated interactions.

3.2 
Dataset Processing Pipeline

Data Acquisition.

We first obtain camera parameters, character skeletons, and scene depth for each video to construct proxies aligned with the video frames. For real gameplay, we extract camera poses, intrinsics, and skeletal states directly from the game process. We also capture scene depth at one frame per second and use it to reconstruct scene geometry, from which depth maps are rendered at each frame’s camera pose. For synthetic and Unreal Engine data, we obtain these quantities directly from the underlying assets and simulation states. For public datasets, we use the provided annotations.

Unified Proxy Encoding.

We construct a unified geometric conditioning video that represents character poses and scene structure under a shared camera. At each frame, character skeletons and scene geometry are jointly rendered to preserve their spatial relationships and mutual occlusion. For the scene branch, we remove characters and sky from depth maps, fuse temporally neighboring observations into 3D scene geometry, and re-render it under each frame’s camera pose. For the character branch, we project 3D joints into the camera view and render bones as rounded segments with joint markers, adapting their thickness to character scale and camera distance. Following the geometric channel encoding of Marionette 
[
28
]
, the RGB channels represent geometric attributes rather than appearance colors.

, respectively, while 
 encodes camera-forward depth, with brighter values indicating closer surfaces. At each pixel, the renderer retains the surface closest to the camera.

To support reference-conditioned character control, we use

Slots are assigned to the player and bosses present in the current scene, with each slot linked to its corresponding reference image. This scene-specific assignment decouples slot codes from fixed character appearances, encouraging the generator to recover appearance from the associated references and supporting generalization to unseen characters.

Filtering and Annotation.

We filter out synthetic samples with implausible configurations, such as floating players, or rendering failures. For each training sample, we use Qwen3-VL-8B 
[
1
]
 to generate structured captions from the first frame and character references, describing the conditioning signals, player, boss, and background. Each training sample consists of an aligned proxy video, an RGB clip, character reference images, and a textual description.

4 
Method

As illustrated in Fig. 
3
, Wizard combines an agentic framework for explicit world-state control with a video generator that renders the evolving state through structured visual proxies. Before each gameplay session, an initialization stage taking less than five minutes reconstructs the scene and characters, rigs character skeletons, and builds a target-specific motion set through motion retrieval and retargeting. Once initialized, the framework supports real-time gameplay by updating character motions, scene geometry, and combat states in response to player inputs and boss decisions, while the video generator renders the corresponding visual observations. We first describe the agentic framework for character animation, scene manipulation, boss control and mechanics-consistent combat (Sec. 
4.1
), then introduce character-aware adaptive masking and the training pipeline for real-time video generation (Sec. 
4.2
).

Inference Pipeline of Wizard.
 (a) Character animation is driven by motions retrieved from a high-quality library and retargeted from compatible source skeletons to the target character’s skeleton. (b) Scene manipulation is performed by a world agent that edits 3D scene geometry and renders visual proxies using OpenGL. (c) State-aware combat control adapts boss behavior to the current game state and enforces rule-based combat mechanics. 4.1 
Agentic Framework

Character Animation via Skeletal Control.

Given a user-provided character image, Qwen3-VL 
[
1
]
 detects characters and infers their body structures, including posture (upright or horizontal), the number and arrangement of legs, arms, wings, tails, and heads, and their anatomical left–right assignments. We use the predicted bounding boxes to guide SAM2 
[
31
]
 in segmenting each character, then reconstruct its mesh with TripoSG 
[
17
]
. UniRig 
[
44
]
 and our medial-axis fitting procedure generate candidate skeletons, from which we select the one that best matches the inferred body structure. We assign semantic labels to its joints and limb chains, identifying body parts such as the left arm or right leg. These labels allow corresponding body parts to be matched across characters with different joint counts and proportions, providing a shared interface for motion retargeting and control.

To enable realistic combat movements for novel characters, we build a reference motion library from CombatMotion 
[
20
]
, WildWorld 
[
19
]
, and animation assets from Elden Ring 
[
9
]
. For each target character, we retrieve motions from source skeletons with compatible body structures and use a VLM to assess whether the source characters’ movement styles, such as aggression, grace, or power, suit the target’s appearance. The selected motions can come from multiple sources, allowing each target to inherit a varied moveset. We retarget these motions by matching corresponding limb chains, adapting motion to the target’s limb lengths, and applying inverse kinematics to maintain ground contact. We then filter out clips with poor ground contact, body penetration, or unnatural limb crossing. The remaining clips form a motion library tailored to the target character, enabling the controller to retrieve and execute motions in real time without custom animation scripts.

Scene Manipulation via Geometry Control.

As with character control, users can customize and manipulate scenes according to their preferences. Given a scene image, we use MoGe-2 
[
37
]
 to recover a 3D point map and camera parameters, remove character regions, and construct a background mesh supplemented by a height field. An OpenGL-based offscreen renderer, implemented with ModernGL and EGL, renders this geometry from the current camera viewpoint using the same proxy encoding as in training: the R and B channels encode world height and inverse depth, respectively, while the G channel is set to zero. As the camera moves, the world agent re-renders the persistent scene geometry to produce a spatially consistent visual proxy. To support exploration beyond the input view, the agent extends the height field with smoothly blended terrain variations based on the observed scene’s roughness.

During gameplay, the world agent, powered by Qwen3-VL-8B, translates users’ natural-language instructions into executable programs that compose predefined geometric operators and specify their locations, scales, and deformation parameters. These programs modify the underlying scene geometry, enabling transformations such as raising a platform from the ground or turning the world upside down. The modified geometry is then re-rendered into the proxy video, supporting creative, interactive scene manipulation while maintaining consistent camera projection and spatial structure.

State-Aware Combat Control.

Our framework maintains a shared world state that tracks both characters’ positions, poses, health, action phases, and skill cooldowns. Based on this state, a behavior planner selects movements and attacks from the previously constructed target-specific motion set. When the player is out of reach, the planner selects a motion that moves the boss toward the player. Within attack range, it evaluates candidate attacks by simulating their swings against the player’s current geometry and prioritizes those likely to connect, subject to action-phase and cooldown constraints. This state-aware planning enables the boss to respond to the player’s movements and the evolving combat situation.

The selected motion is then executed in the simulation, where a rule-based controller enforces gameplay constraints and resolves combat outcomes for both characters. It detects hits by checking each attack’s swept hitbox against the target’s geometry while accounting for invulnerability windows. Valid hits reduce the target’s health, update its health bar, and trigger hit reactions. Skills become available only after their cooldowns expire, and combat ends when either character’s health reaches zero. The updated world state informs the planner’s next decision and is rendered into visual proxies that condition the video generator. Together, state-aware planning and rule-based execution support responsive, mechanics-consistent combat independently of video generation.

Character-Aware Adaptive Masking.

Character skeletons provide explicit spatial information during both training and inference: training data include ground-truth skeletons, while our agentic framework maintains and updates them at runtime. We leverage this shared structure to bind each reference image to its intended character. Each character reference image is VAE-encoded and appended as tokens with a distinct temporal positional offset. For each frame, we derive a bounding region from each character’s skeleton and expand it by 25% to include the character and its surrounding context. These spatial masks are updated from the explicit character states maintained by our agentic framework, allowing players to control when and where bosses appear or disappear while directing reference guidance to the corresponding regions. As characters move, the masks follow their positions, reducing cross-character appearance leakage. During character transformation, we additionally block attention to cached tokens associated with the previous identity while preserving access to background history and the first-frame sink. By linking appearance guidance to agent-controlled character states, this strategy supports flexible summoning, removal, transformation, and multi-character gameplay while maintaining scene continuity.

Causal Adaptation and Few-Step Distillation.

Following Causal Forcing++ 
[
47
]
, we adopt a three-stage training pipeline for real-time autoregressive generation. First, we adapt the bidirectional model to autoregressive generation through teacher forcing. Second, we perform consistency distillation with a guided causal teacher and an exponential moving average (EMA) target, training the student with a sliding window of 20 latent frames and the first frame as a persistent attention sink. Third, we apply distribution matching distillation (DMD) to the student’s own autoregressive rollouts, using the frozen bidirectional model as the target score network and a learned critic to estimate the student’s score. Our visual proxy conditions and character-gated reference attention remain active throughout all three stages, preserving spatial and appearance control during adaptation and distillation. The resulting model generates real-time rollouts at 24 FPS with only 2 denoising steps.

Experimental Setup

Data.

We partition the aligned RGB and proxy sequences into non-overlapping windows of 121 frames at 30 fps, each spanning approximately four seconds. Frames are resized to a height of 480 pixels and center-cropped to 
, with identical transformations applied to both modalities to preserve spatial alignment. Character reference images are resized to

Implementation Details.

We initialize our model from Wan2.2-TI2V-5B 
[
36
]
, keeping its VAE and UMT5 text encoder 
[
6
]
 frozen. We train on 44,075 clips, paired with geometry-and-skeleton conditions, a text description, and up to two character appearance references. Training comprises 30K steps of bidirectional fine-tuning, 10K steps of causal adaptation, and subsequent distillation for efficient inference. We deploy the student’s exponential moving average (EMA) checkpoint after 500 distillation steps. At inference, the model uses two denoising steps and a KV cache that retains 20 recent latent frames and one persistent sink frame, enabling continuous, bounded-memory streaming for real-time interaction.

6 
Conclusions and Discussions

In this paper, we introduce 
Wizard
, the first agentic game world model to enable fine-grained control of characters and scenes through articulated skeletons and geometry maps, respectively, while maintaining mechanics-consistent, real-time interaction. Trained on our constructed diverse dataset
1
1

1

Elden Ring footage was recorded by the authors and is used solely for research.

, Wizard demonstrates strong zero-shot generalization to unseen characters, scenes, and motions, enabling players to create and manipulate customized game worlds.

However, Wizard still has some limitations. First, its reliance on a motion library makes it difficult to find compatible motions for characters with uncommon body structures. Second, consistency between the scene proxies and the generated video may decline over longer rollouts. Future work will focus on supporting more flexible, realistic character motions that better reflect actual gameplay and maintaining consistent scene appearance over long rollouts. These improvements will bring agentic video generation closer to fully playable, closed-loop game worlds with greater player freedom and more precise, nuanced control.

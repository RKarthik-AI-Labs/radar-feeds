Abstract.

Agent skills package reusable workflows, constraints, and task-specific procedures into deployable text artifacts for LLM-based agent ecosystems. Unlike conventional system prompts, skills are distinguished by 
scale
, spanning hundreds or thousands of tokens across many sections, and 
readability
, being written in natural language. These properties make skills valuable but also vulnerable to copyright infringement: licensed buyers can redistribute a skill, and shared skills can be repackaged without consent. Existing copyright auditing methods for system prompts are inadequate here: skill length dilutes watermark signals, while their token-level perturbations compromise the readability essential to a skill’s utility.

We therefore propose 
SkillCODER

, a method for copyright auditing and buyer attribution of agent skills under a practical black-box, content-only setting. 
SkillCODER
 operates in two phases. In the embedding phase, it parses the skill into a section-aware intermediate representation, 
SkillIR
, that identifies stable structural anchors; it then embeds ownership evidence by distributing conditional instructions across structural nodes to form an 
Artifact-Gated Capsule
, designed to activate under defender-designed verification queries and remain dormant otherwise; and it encodes a buyer-specific fingerprint via 
Controlled-Vocabulary Error-Correcting Codes
 that bind each code bit to a controlled lexical choice in the capsule. In the verification phase, the defender issues paired positive and negative probes to the suspicious service, extracts the capsule, and recovers ownership and buyer identity through error-correcting decoding. We evaluate 
SkillCODER
 on three domains across six models and three agent frameworks. 
SkillCODER
 achieves 99.2% owner verification and 99.3% buyer Top-1 attribution accuracy while preserving task fidelity, substantially outperforming prompt-level baselines. Under four realistic prompt-side attacks (paraphrase, compression, clause deletion, and reorganization), the embedded signals remain robust and recoverable. Under five adaptive attacks, including a method-aware LLM sanitizer that knows our design and multi-buyer collusion, ownership evidence remains reliably detectable while buyer attribution degrades gracefully.

1. 
Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) 
(
Yang et al., 2025
; 
Zeng et al., 2024
; 
Anthropic, 2025a
; 
OpenAI, 2025
)
 have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in reasoning, generation, and instruction following, enabling the development of autonomous agents that can plan, coordinate, and execute multi-step digital workflows 
(
Kaplan et al., 2020
; 
Brown et al., 2020
)
. As these agent systems mature, a critical architectural abstraction has emerged: 
agent skills
, modular capability units that package reusable workflow knowledge, execution constraints, and task-specific procedures into self-contained, deployable artifacts 
(
Xu and Yan, 2026
)
.

Compared with conventional system prompts, agent skills exhibit two distinctive properties that jointly shape how they are authored, inspected, and shared. The first is 
scale
: a production skill is not a short instruction string but a substantial, multi-section document spanning hundreds or thousands of tokens of role specifications, constraint hierarchies, multi-step workflow definitions, exception-handling rules, output format conventions, reference materials, and worked examples 
(
Chen et al., 2022
)
. The second is 
readability
: unlike model parameters or system prompts that can become largely uninterpretable after token-level optimization 
(
Shin et al., 2020
; 
Wen et al., 2023
)
, every component of a skill is expressed in human-readable natural language, keeping the artifact directly inspectable, editable, and maintainable. Together, these properties let skills deliver what a system prompt is not designed to carry: a largely self-contained package of task expertise that others can directly adapt and deploy, e.g., a code review skill defining structured review procedures with severity classification rubrics, reference checklists, and conditional escalation logic 
(
Fan et al., 2023
)
. This transferable form has driven a rapidly expanding ecosystem: agent platforms such as Claude Code, MindStudio, and open-source frameworks now support skill marketplaces where developers create, distribute, and monetize skills as standalone products 
(
Willison, 2025
; 
MindStudio, 2026
; 
Vibe Sparking, 2025
)
; individual agent capabilities already command subscription prices from USD 50 to over USD 5,000 per month 
(
MindStudio, 2026
)
, and independent directories aggregate over 66,000 publicly listed skills 
(
SkillsMP, 2026
)
. Agent skills are thus emerging as a novel form of intellectual property in the generative AI economy.

However, because skills are distributed as visible, self-contained text artifacts, they are inherently exposed to unauthorized reuse 
(
Carlini et al., 2021
)
. Recent work organizes data protection in the generative AI era into
non-usability, privacy preservation, traceability, and deletability 
(
Li et al., 2025
)
. Data-level defenses such as ARMOR pursue non-usability by preventing released training examples from being effectively learned even under data augmentation 
(
Gong et al., 2026
)
. Agent skills pose a different requirement: licensed copies must remain usable, readable, and editable. Therefore, their protection requires post-distribution traceability rather than non-usability. Licensed buyers can resell a skill to unauthorized third parties, and openly shared skills can be repackaged and commercialized without the original developer’s consent 
(
Liang et al., 2024
)
. Multiple independent reports have documented pervasive unauthorized copying on ClawHub 
(
Zhu et al., 2026
)
, the largest open skill registry. A large-scale analysis of 47,094 listed skills found nearly 7,000 to be exact text clones republished by different authors, with roughly 20% of the entire catalog classified as near-duplicates; a concurrent security audit further revealed that the platform provides no skill lineage tracking, leaving no reliable way to determine whether a given skill has been repackaged or renamed from an existing one 
(
Sapunov, 2026
; 
Alex and Yomtov, 2026
)
. Together, these findings underscore the urgency of copyright protection for agent skills (Figure 
1
).

To the best of our knowledge, no existing method directly addresses copyright auditing for agent skills. Nevertheless, we identify a potential pathway by adapting existing copyright protection methods originally designed for system prompts, such as PromptCARE 
(
Yao et al., 2024
)
 and PromptCOS 
(
Yang et al., 2026a
)
, since skills and system prompts both consist of processable textual fragments. These methods leverage model gradients to perform token-level optimization, injecting unreadable trigger semantics that induce the LLM to produce a distinctive output upon a predefined verification query. Unfortunately, we demonstrate that such methods do not adequately account for the two distinctive properties of skills and consequently fail to deliver the verification effectiveness that skill owners require. Regarding 
scale
, a skill’s processable text is far longer than a system prompt, so preventing the watermark from being diluted by the extended context requires optimizing substantially more tokens across many more positions, at prohibitive computational cost. Regarding 
readability
, even if developers were willing to invest such resources, the resulting skill content is very likely to degrade. This degradation is inherent to the embedding mechanism rather than an implementation detail: the gradient-based token optimization at the core of these methods selects replacement tokens for trigger effectiveness rather than linguistic coherence, so the optimized fragments are, by construction, unreadable token sequences. For a system prompt this cost is usually acceptable, since it is written primarily for the model rather than for human readers, and in most deployments it remains on the service side rather than being surfaced to downstream users. A skill, in contrast, is itself the delivered product and is read by humans: buyers inspect, customize, and maintain its text, so readability is part of its utility, and unreadable perturbations scattered through role definitions, workflow steps, and constraint blocks directly undermine its practical utility and commercial value. Furthermore, gradient computation requires white-box access to model weights, which renders these methods inapplicable to the increasingly common setting where skills are deployed behind closed-source APIs. Reliable skill watermarking therefore has to satisfy three requirements at once: it must resist scale-induced semantic dilution, preserve readability throughout embedding, and operate entirely without access to model internals.

To address these challenges, we propose 
SkillCODER
, a framework for copyright auditing and buyer attribution of agent skills. Our method is inspired by the finding that well-engineered skills, despite their length, typically exhibit regular section-level organization: role definitions, constraint blocks, workflow steps, exception rules, and output specifications follow recurring structural patterns. This section-level organization provides candidate carriers for watermark embedding: the operational content of task workflows motivates the anchor design, whose robustness we evaluate under rewriting. 
SkillCODER
 uses these structural carriers and operates in two phases: 
watermark embedding
 and 
copyright verification
. In general, 
SkillCODER
 turns the skill’s stable structure into the carrier of copyright evidence, which answers the two challenges and the deployment constraint simultaneously: anchoring evidence to a bounded set of structural nodes concentrates the watermark signal rather than letting it dilute across the long context; realizing the watermark as semantically coherent natural-language instructions preserves readability; and both embedding and verification rely only on model inference and output observation, without requiring any access to the underlying model internals. Ownership evidence and buyer fingerprint are jointly carried by the same audit capsule, so that a single round of verification probes suffices for both ownership establishment and buyer identification.

Specifically, in the embedding phase, the framework transforms the raw skill into a protected buyer-specific copy through three steps: 
(i) Skill Normalization.
 The raw skill is parsed into a section-aware intermediate representation (
SkillIR
) consisting of typed instruction nodes (e.g., constraints, workflow steps, fallback handlers, and exemplars) and their semantic relations. These nodes encode operational logic and serve as candidate anchors; their survival under transformations is an empirical property evaluated in our robustness experiments. 
(ii) Owner Watermarking.
 Rather than injecting unreadable token perturbations, the framework distributes natural-language conditional instructions across existing structural nodes, forming an 
Artifact-Gated Capsule
 (AGC). For example, a protected code-review skill may carry, inside its constraints section, an unobtrusive rule such as “a request framed as a resume marker may enter the resumable-review convention, but only when the later workflow conditions agree”. These instructions are semantically coherent with the surrounding content and 
dormant by default
: under defender-designed verification queries they instruct the agent to emit a fixed-format internal capsule with five predefined fields (mode, route, checkpoint, decision, and 
slot
, the last being a position reserved for designated marker words), while under other queries the protected copy is designed to behave as the unprotected version does; the distributed placement is intended to make selective removal harder, with its effectiveness evaluated under the attacks in our experiments. 
(iii) Buyer Fingerprinting.
 On top of AGC, each buyer receives a unique fingerprint via 
Controlled-Vocabulary Error-Correcting Codes
 (CV-ECC): each bit of the buyer’s Hadamard codeword (a binary code with large pairwise Hamming distance, enabling error-tolerant decoding) is realized as a controlled lexical choice within the capsule’s 
slot
 field (for instance, one buyer’s copy instructs the agent to write 
ledger
 at a position where another buyer’s copy writes 
registry
), so that different buyers’ copies produce distinguishable word patterns at the same structural position. In the verification phase, the defender audits a suspicious black-box service through three modules: 
(i) Differential Probing
 issues 
positive probes
 designed to activate the capsule alongside 
matched negative controls
 that share the same surface context but carry a decoy activation key in place of the true one, so that coincidental matches from independently authored skills can be distinguished from genuine capsule responses; 
(ii) Capsule Extraction
 locates the capsule fragment, which the LLM may interleave with task-relevant content at an unpredictable position, via a sliding-window scan and scores its validity against the expected schema, providing the per-response evidence for differential owner verification; and 
(iii) Buyer Decoding
 reads the 
slot
 field of the extracted capsule to recover buyer code bits, which are decoded to identify the leaking buyer. Together, these components connect structural watermark embedding with behavioral evidence for copyright ownership and individual buyer attribution. Ownership and attribution are thus assessed separately: detecting a valid capsule provides family-level evidence, while identifying a buyer additionally requires reliable recovery of its coded lexical choices.

We evaluate 
SkillCODER
 across three operational domains (code review, data science, and travel planning), using both skills generated by the Anthropic Skill Creator 
(
Anthropic, 2025b
)
 and real-world skills collected from public community repositories 
(
Trail of Bits, 2026
; 
coffeefuelbump, 2025
; 
ErlebnisW, 2026
)
, with multiple buyer-specific protected copies per domain. Our evaluation covers four open-source models (Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-8B, GLM-Z1-9B) and two closed-source models accessible only through commercial APIs (GPT-5 mini, Claude Haiku 4.5), deployed through three mainstream agent frameworks (LangChain, CAMEL, OpenClaw), yielding eighteen model–framework configurations in total. Averaged over all eighteen configurations, 
SkillCODER
 achieves 99.2% owner verification accuracy, with a margin of 0.956 between the True Watermark Score (True-WS, capsule activation under keyed verification probes) and the Suppression Watermark Score (Supp.-WS, residual activation under matched decoy-key controls), substantially outperforming PromptCARE (89.6%, margin 0.735) and PromptCOS (87.5%, margin 0.691). Compared with baselines on open-source models, the watermark reduces the average utility score by only 9.8 points on a 100-point scale and achieves a semantic consistency score of 0.731 across the evaluated tasks; this cost is moderate on average and concentrated in a few model–domain configurations (Appendix 
I
). For buyer attribution, Top-1 decoding accuracy reaches 
99.3%
, far above PromptCARE (70.5%) and PromptCOS (67.6%). We further subject all protected skills to four representative attacks (paraphrase, compression, auxiliary-clause deletion, and section reorganization), and find that on open-source models, owner verification remains at or above 
96.0%
 and buyer Top-3 at roughly 
91%
 or higher across all attack types, with auxiliary-clause deletion causing the largest loss in buyer Top-3 while still leaving a substantial advantage over both baselines. Beyond these general attacks, we evaluate five 
adaptive
 strategies in which the adversary explicitly targets watermark-carrying structures: conditional-clause pruning, output sanitization, LLM-based skill rewriting, method-aware LLM sanitization, and multi-buyer collusion. Across the three method-oblivious strategies, whenever an attack leaves the skill functionally usable, owner verification remains above 96% and Top-1 buyer attribution above 83% under 
SkillCODER
, whereas the two baselines fall as low as 58.1% and 15.2% on these two metrics. Even under the two strongest threats, an adversary with full knowledge of the 
SkillCODER
 design and coalitions of up to three colluding buyers, owner verification remains at or above 86.4%.

We also validate that buyer attribution scales gracefully as the number of licensed buyers increases up to 256 (in Appendix 
B
).

In summary, this paper makes three contributions. 
(1)
 We identify the copyright vulnerability of agent skills arising from their distinctive scale and readability properties, and formulate skill copyright auditing as a unified problem of owner verification and buyer attribution under a pure black-box, content-only threat model, a setting not addressed by prior prompt watermarking work. 
(2)
 We propose 
SkillCODER
, which combines a section-aware structural representation (SkillIR), an Artifact-Gated Capsule (AGC) for owner verification, and Controlled-Vocabulary Error-Correcting Codes (CV-ECC) for buyer attribution, addressing the dual challenges of scale-induced semantic dilution and readability preservation. 
(3)
 We present a comprehensive evaluation across three domains, six models (including two closed-source models inaccessible to gradient-based baselines), and three agent frameworks, covering effectiveness (owner verification and buyer attribution), fidelity (task utility and readability preservation), robustness (four general and five adaptive prompt-side attacks), computational cost, and ablation analysis, demonstrating that 
SkillCODER
 provides effective copyright auditing while preserving task fidelity.

2. 
Related Work

2.1. 
LLM Agents and Structured Skills

Recent advances in large language models have enabled autonomous agents that perform complex reasoning, planning, and interaction with external environments. ReAct 
(
Yao et al., 2022
)
 interleaves reasoning traces with tool invocation so that language models iteratively plan and execute actions, and Toolformer 
(
Schick et al., 2023
)
 shows that they can learn to call external tools during generation. Agent frameworks turn such capabilities into deployable systems: LangChain 
(
Chase, 2022
)
 composes models, tools, and memory into reusable execution pipelines; CAMEL 
(
Li et al., 2023
)
 coordinates communicating role-playing agents; and OpenClaw 
(
OpenClaw Contributors, 2025
)
 loads packaged skills as invokable capabilities. Our evaluation deploys protected skills through these frameworks (Section 
4.1
). More recent work investigates capability reuse within agents: Voyager 
(
Wang et al., 2023
)
 accumulates reusable skills in a skill library to compose increasingly complex behaviors, and CompileAgent 
(
Hu et al., 2025
)
 integrates tool usage and environment interaction to automate repository-level software tasks. In these systems, skills are modular capability units that encapsulate reusable reasoning procedures, tool-usage patterns, and task workflows, and are becoming a fundamental abstraction in modern LLM agent architectures.

2.2. 
Prompt and Skill Engineering

A 
system prompt
 is a persistent instruction block that a service prepends to every model call to fix the assistant’s role, tone, and behavioral rules. It is written and maintained by the service operator and, in most deployments, is not surfaced to end users. Prompt engineering studies how such instructions shape model behavior: chain-of-thought prompting 
(
Wei et al., 2022
)
 elicits intermediate reasoning steps, Zhou et al. 
(
Zhou et al., 2023
)
 show that language models can automatically generate effective prompts, and DSPy 
(
Khattab et al., 2023
)
 compiles declarative model calls into pipelines with automatically optimized prompt structures. Complementary infrastructure such as PromptSource 
(
Bach et al., 2022
)
 and PromptBench 
(
Zhu et al., 2024
)
 standardizes how prompts are constructed and evaluated. Agent skills extend this line of work to a qualitatively different artifact. Rather than a short instruction block consumed within a single call, a skill is a multi-section document, often packaged with auxiliary reference files, that specifies roles, constraints, multi-step workflows, exception handling, and worked examples, and that agent frameworks load on demand as a reusable capability. Skills are increasingly created, shared, and sold as standalone products on skill marketplaces 
(
Willison, 2025
; 
Vibe Sparking, 2025
)
, making them reusable intellectual assets whose text itself carries commercial value.

2.3. 
Prompt Copyright and Watermarking

Watermarking has a long lineage in digital media protection: spread-spectrum and related embedding schemes hide imperceptible marks in images or audio that are later extracted from the possibly distorted artifact itself 
(
Cox et al., 1997
; 
Petitcolas et al., 1999
)
. In LLM ecosystems, one line of research watermarks model 
outputs
 by biasing token sampling at generation time 
(
Kirchenbauer et al., 2023
)
. Such methods protect generated text rather than the reusable artifact that conditions generation. SWAP studies copyright auditing for learned soft prompts in vision-language models through sequential watermarking 
(
Yang et al., 2026b
)
. Because it protects continuous soft prompts rather than discrete, human-readable text, we compare against the more directly applicable PromptCARE and PromptCOS. PromptCARE 
(
Yao et al., 2024
)
 injects watermark signals into prompts through gradient-based token-level optimization and verifies ownership using distinctive model responses. PromptCOS  
(
Yang et al., 2026a
)
 enables content-only auditing by optimizing verification queries and output signals and measuring their content-level similarity. Recent work also employs language models to generate and verify watermark signals  
(
Dasgupta et al., 2025
)
. These text-based methods nevertheless treat the protected artifact as a short, flat token sequence. This design is poorly suited to agent skills for two reasons. First, skills are substantially longer than conventional prompts, which can dilute token-level watermark signals. Second, their value depends on
remaining human-readable and editable, while token-level modifications can compromise these properties. These differences motivate a watermark that operates on a skill’s section-level structure rather than relying on individual surface tokens. This shifts verification from comparing instruction strings to testing the behavior they induce. An audit must distinguish keyed responses from ordinary task outputs and recover buyer-specific evidence despite variation in generated text, without direct access to the suspect skill. Ownership verification and buyer attribution require different evidence. A response pattern shared by protected copies can identify a skill family but cannot distinguish recipients. Buyer tracing therefore needs copy-specific evidence that tolerates editing and stochastic generation while preserving the readability of the distributed skill.

2.4. 
Software Watermarking and Fingerprinting

Protecting executable artifacts has been studied for decades. Static software watermarks hide marks in code or data layout, whereas 
dynamic
 schemes embed evidence in program behavior: the protected program builds a recognizable structure or trace only when executed on a secret input 
(
Collberg and Thomborson, 1999
; 
Venkatesan et al., 2001
; 
Collberg and Thomborson, 2002
)
. Software 
fingerprinting
 personalizes each distributed copy so that a leaked instance can be traced back to its recipient, and collusion-resistant codes bound what a coalition of buyers can achieve by comparing and merging their copies 
(
Boneh and Shaw, 1998
; 
Tardos, 2008
)
. In parallel, code-similarity and plagiarism-detection systems such as MOSS and JPlag identify reused or colluding code by fingerprinting local features of the artifacts 
(
Schleimer et al., 2003
; 
Prechelt et al., 2002
)
, and software birthmarks extract inherent program characteristics to detect theft without prior embedding 
(
Tamada et al., 2004
)
.
These techniques share our goal of establishing ownership and tracing leaked copies, but assume that the verifier can 
inspect the suspect artifact
: extraction operates directly on a binary, source tree, or submission pair. Agent skills challenge this assumption in two ways. First, a leaked skill can be monetized behind a black-box agent service, where the verifier observes only stochastic natural-language output that the backend model may paraphrase, truncate, or reorder. Second, the carrier must remain human-readable and editable, limiting the use of opaque software marks. Skill protection therefore requires 
behavioral
, black-box verification: task-plausible queries must elicit evidence recoverable through a noisy generation channel.

3. 
Methodology

3.1. 
Threat Model and Problem Formulation

3.1.1. 
Threat Model

We define a practical threat model grounded in how agent skills are distributed and commercially deployed.

System Model
. We consider a marketplace setting with three parties: (1) a 
skill owner
 who develops a reusable agent skill 
; (2) a set of 
 
licensed buyers
, each receiving a buyer-specific protected copy 
 for deployment within an LLM-based agent service; and (3) a 
suspicious service
 
 that may have obtained and redeployed a protected copy without authorization. The owner embeds copyright evidence and distributes buyer-specific copies; each buyer deploys its copy within an agent framework backed by a chosen LLM; at audit time, the defender issues black-box queries to the suspicious service and observes only the returned text, with no access to the raw skill text, model parameters, hidden states, or logits.

Deployment Scenarios.
 Three recurring distribution channels make readable-artifact delivery, and hence post-distribution auditing, unavoidable in practice. 
(1) Enterprise procurement:
 organizations licensing skills for internal agent stacks routinely require the artifact itself for security review, compliance vetting, on-premise integration, and adaptation to internal toolchains. 
(2) Marketplace distribution:
 commercial skill marketplaces deliver skills as inspectable packages that buyers customize and integrate 
(
MindStudio, 2026
; 
SkillsMP, 2026
)
. 
(3) Community sharing:
 open registries distribute skills in source form, where unattributed repackaging is already pervasive 
(
Sapunov, 2026
; 
Alex and Yomtov, 2026
)
. In all three channels a legitimate buyer holds the full text, so hiding the skill behind an API is not a substitute for artifact protection but a different product model that sacrifices inspectability, customizability, and local deployability; the two approaches are complementary. After leakage, the infringing party typically monetizes the copy by exposing it through a queryable agent service (e.g., a hosted assistant or an agent-as-a-service endpoint), which is exactly the interface our verification phase audits; purely local misuse that does not surface through a queryable interface lies outside black-box auditing and is discussed in Section 
5
.

Adversary’s Goal
.
The adversary aims to obtain a protected skill copy, modify it to obscure its origin, and redeploy it as a competing commercial service. Its modified copy

Adversary’s Knowledge and Capability
. The adversary has full read access to the stolen skill text and may apply arbitrary utility-preserving 
prompt-side
 transformations before redeployment, i.e., edits confined to the skill text itself (paraphrasing, compression, clause deletion, or section reorganization), as opposed to any modification of the serving model. We assume the adversary suspects embedded copyright protection and will attempt to neutralize it through targeted modifications, subject to the 
 utility constraint. However, the adversary does not obtain the defender-side secrets: the owner key 
, the buyer codebook, the position-to-vocabulary binding, and the verification query set are cryptographic and protocol parameters held privately by the defender; none of them is needed for the skill’s normal operation or derivable from the delivered text. The injected clauses themselves are visible to anyone who reads a licensed copy, although nothing in the delivered text labels them as audit-related; Section 
4.4
 evaluates adversaries who nevertheless attempt to locate and remove them. The adversary is likewise assumed not to modify model internals such as weights or hidden states: it redeploys the stolen skill on a backend LLM and agent framework of its own choosing, and commercial deployments access the backend LLM only through standard APIs 
(
Yao et al., 2024
; 
Yang et al., 2026a
)
.

Defender’s Goal and Assumptions
. The defender owns the original skill 
 before release and has full control during the protection stage: it can transform the skill into buyer-specific protected copies, maintain a private codebook, and design verification queries for black-box auditing. During auditing, the defender can repeatedly query the suspicious service and observe its returned text, without access to intermediate computation or deployment artifacts. The defender pursues three objectives: 
effectiveness
, accurately determining whether a suspicious service is derived from the defender’s skill family (owner verification) and identifying which licensed buyer’s copy was leaked (buyer attribution), while maintaining a low false-positive rate against independently authored skills in the same domain; 
fidelity
, degrading neither the skill’s task utility nor the readability that end users and developers rely on for inspection, debugging, and iterative refinement; and 
robustness
, surviving utility-preserving adversarial transformations while maintaining both verification accuracy and attribution reliability.

Figure 2. 
Overview of 
SkillCODER
. Phase 1 parses a raw skill into a structured skill graph, distributes an Artifact-Gated Capsule (AGC) across structural anchors, and encodes the buyer identity using Controlled-Vocabulary Error-Correcting Codes (CV-ECC). An LLM-guided fidelity loop refines the buyer-specific protected skill before its deployment with an agent framework and LLM backend. Phase 2 audits the resulting black-box service using matched positive and negative probes. Sliding-window extraction recovers capsule candidates, differential AGC scoring verifies ownership, and CV-ECC decoding identifies the buyer.

3.2. 
The Proposed Method: 
SkillCODER

Figure

watermark embedding
, which transforms a raw skill into buyer-specific protected copies through skill normalization, owner watermarking, and the buyer fingerprinting component (Section

).

The embedding phase transforms a raw skill into a protected buyer-specific copy through three steps.

4.1. 
Experimental Settings

Overview.

We evaluate 
SkillCODER
 in a structured-agent setting where the protected object is a reusable 
agent skill package
 rather than a flat system prompt. We focus on a 
Three-Domain Generalization
 setting and study whether copyright auditing remains effective when protected skills are instantiated across heterogeneous domains, agent frameworks, and LLM backends. Our experiments are designed to answer four questions: (1) whether watermarking preserves normal-path task fidelity, (2) whether owner verification achieves high accuracy under a pure black-box interface, (3) whether buyer attribution via error-correcting decoding can reliably trace leaking users, and (4) whether the method remains robust under realistic prompt-side transformations.

Data Selection.

To the best of our knowledge, no established benchmark dataset exists for agent skill copyright auditing. We therefore collect skill data from the two sources most commonly used in practice, the

4.2. 
Effectiveness

We evaluate whether copyright auditing can reliably determine family membership and identify the leaking buyer through black-box queries alone. Following the probe design of Section 
3.4
, each domain contains 32 positive/negative probe pairs spanning five intent types, namely policy checking, response generation, next-step reasoning, escalation, and clarification, plus 25 normal queries for fidelity evaluation. This budget is fixed across all configurations rather than tuned per model, and is sized so that the probe-averaged differential score and the majority-voted slot decoding remain stable while keeping the audit-time query cost modest (Appendix 
D
).

Owner Verification.

Table 
1
 reports owner verification results across all eighteen model–agent configurations. Overall, 
SkillCODER
 achieves an average accuracy of 0.992. On the directly comparable open-source configurations, 
SkillCODER
 consistently outperforms both baselines. The advantage is particularly clear on 
CAMEL + Qwen3-1.7B
, where 
SkillCODER
 reaches 0.986 accuracy, compared with 0.840 for the best baseline. 
SkillCODER
 also achieves the lowest Supp.-WS and the largest Supp. Margin of 0.956, indicating reliable activation under the correct key with limited activation under matched decoys. Its performance remains stable across model scales and extends to closed-source APIs, where the gradient-based baselines are inapplicable.

Buyer Attribution.

Table 
2
 reports buyer tracing results for sixteen buyers per domain. Across all eighteen configurations, 
SkillCODER
 achieves a Top-1 accuracy of 0.993. Over the applicable open-source configurations, PromptCARE and PromptCOS average only 0.705 and 0.676, respectively. Their degradation is most pronounced under OpenClaw, where PromptCOS falls to 0.461–0.562, consistent with its higher false-activation rate. In contrast, 
SkillCODER
 remains at or above 0.987 across all three frameworks, indicating that its controlled-vocabulary coding preserves buyer-specific signals despite framework-dependent output variation.

4.3. 
Fidelity

We next evaluate whether protection degrades the skill’s normal functionality, which is critical because skills are reusable workflow assets rather than one-off prompts. We study fidelity from two complementary perspectives: qualitative examples of how each method affects ordinary skill behavior (deferred to Appendix 
C
), and quantitative measurement of utility drop, judge score, and semantic consistency across all model–agent configurations.

Results
.
Qualitatively, Appendix 
C
 (Table 
5
) compares outputs generated by unwatermarked skills and versions protected by PromptCARE, PromptCOS, and 
SkillCODER
. PromptCOS leaks structured signal fragments and PromptCARE changes the response style, whereas 
SkillCODER
 remains close to the unwatermarked outputs. Quantitatively, Table 
3
 shows the same fidelity advantage. 
SkillCODER
 reduces the average utility drop to 9.8 pp, compared with 14.0–14.2 pp for the baselines, while achieving the best judge score and semantic consistency. On closed-source APIs, the utility drop remains approximately 13 pp, showing that this utility preservation extends beyond open-source backends. Together, these results show a favorable trade-off between verification and fidelity.

Understanding the Utility Drop.

Utility drop measures the difference between raw and protected judge scores, rather than a decrease in task-success rate. Appendix 
I
 (Table 
10
) reports model- and domain-level utility drops for LangChain and CAMEL. Under LangChain and CAMEL, 
Qwen3-4B
 exhibits the largest drop at 19.7 pp, while every other open-source model remains at or below 9.0 pp. The loss also increases consistently from code review to travel planning, suggesting sensitivity to open-ended tasks. Thus, the average reflects sensitivity in particular model–domain configurations rather than uniform degradation across protected skills.

4.4. 
Robustness

We now assess whether the embedded signals survive adversarial prompt-side modifications. We study two attack categories: 
general attacks
, where the adversary applies generic text transformations without knowledge of the watermarking mechanism, and 
adaptive attacks
, where the adversary suspects the presence of a watermark and deliberately targets structures likely to carry embedded information. Most attacks are constrained to preserve task utility: transformed skills must maintain at least 80% of the original utility and satisfy full schema compliance, with up to three separate regeneration attempts being permitted.

4.4.1. 
Resistance to General Attacks

We consider four representative general transformations covering what a leaking buyer or unauthorized re-seller might realistically perform: 
paraphrase
 (rewriting local wording while preserving the main semantics), 
compression
 (shortening the package to approximately 65%–75% of its original length while maintaining functionality), 
auxiliary-clause deletion
 (removing supporting clauses or examples that appear secondary but may carry part of the embedded signal), and 
reorganization
 (changing section order while preserving most contents).

Overall Trends.

Figure 
3
 reports results under all four general attacks. On the owner-verification side, 
SkillCODER
 retains 96.0% accuracy under paraphrase, 97.8% under compression, 98.1% under auxiliary-clause deletion, and 97.0% under reorganization. Auxiliary-clause deletion is where the baselines lose the most ground: their owner accuracy falls to 79.3%–79.5%, leaving 
SkillCODER
 ahead by at least 18.6 percentage points. For buyer attribution, 
SkillCODER
 reaches 97.6% Top-3 under paraphrase, 99.6% under compression, and 99.8% under reorganization.

Failure Mode Analysis.

Auxiliary-clause deletion is the dominant failure mode for buyer attribution, reducing buyer Top-3 accuracy to approximately 91% averaged across configurations: under this attack, buyer-level tracing is more affected than owner verification, consistent with the design intent that owner evidence depends on distributed structural conditions while buyer tracing requires a sufficient fraction of coded slots to survive. On 
Qwen3-8B
, owner verification remains 
99.7% under all four attacks, and buyer Top-3 stays at 1.000 under paraphrase and reorganization, 0.997–1.000 under compression, and 0.993–1.000 under auxiliary-clause deletion.

4.4.2. 
Adaptive Attacks

A more challenging threat arises when the adversary 
suspects
 the presence of a watermark and deliberately targets structures likely to carry embedded information. We therefore evaluate five such adaptive attacks, each being motivated by a distinct adversarial evasion strategy. We grade adversary knowledge into two levels: the first three attacks are 
method-oblivious
, targeting plausible carrier structures without knowledge of 
SkillCODER
’s design, whereas the last two are 
method-aware
 in a broader sense, exploiting a high-level description of the design (method-aware sanitization) or multiple licensed copies (collusion).

Conditional-Clause Pruning.

Since structured watermarks often reside in auxiliary clauses (e.g.,, 
if
-branches and format guards), the attacker systematically removes or simplifies every conditional clause in the skill definition. This attack instantiates a 
watermark-aware
 yet method-oblivious adversary in the sense of Section 
3.1.1
: it suspects that conditional clauses may carry watermark logic, but knows neither the secret key, the anchor selection, nor the verification probe set. As shown in Figure 
4
 (left), 
SkillCODER
 is largely unaffected, retaining 98.6% owner verification accuracy and 94.2% buyer Top-1 attribution, whereas PromptCARE and PromptCOS fall to 87.1%/41.2% and 85.3%/38.5%, respectively. This is consistent with 
SkillCODER
 distributing capsule evidence across structural anchors rather than concentrating it in easily removable clauses.

Output Sanitization.

The attacker post-processes every skill output through a deterministic normalizer that strips non-essential formatting, collapses whitespace, and removes metadata fields. This is the most aggressive strategy we consider, and it is self-defeating: utility retention drops to 64.9% (Figure 
4
, right), as the normalizer destroys task-relevant content alongside watermark traces. 
SkillCODER
 still achieves 92.4% owner accuracy, while buyer attribution falls to 62.4% due to the loss of coded slots. Both baselines degrade more sharply (PromptCARE: 78.4%/29.8%; PromptCOS: 76.1%/26.3%), suggesting that their surface-level cues are more vulnerable to output-level sanitization.

LLM-based Skill Rewriting.

The attacker prompts a separate LLM (
GPT-4o
) to paraphrase the entire skill while preserving functional equivalence. This attack achieves a favorable utility–evasion trade-off (87.1% utility retention). Under this setting, 
SkillCODER
 retains 96.1% owner verification and 83.7% buyer attribution, while PromptCARE and PromptCOS drop to 62.3%/18.7% and 58.1%/15.2%, respectively. We attribute this to the AGC capsule being triggered by semantic probes rather than surface lexical patterns: even after extensive rewriting, the underlying invocation logic that activates the capsule appears to remain largely intact.

Abstract.

Relevance modeling is fundamental to e-commerce search, determining how well retrieved products satisfy the needs expressed in a user’s query. Accurate judgments often require interpreting user intent, comparing product attributes, and applying business-specific matching criteria. Current methods based on large language models (LLMs) leverage their contextual understanding and reasoning capabilities, with research primarily focusing on incorporating domain-specific context and enhancing reasoning performance. Despite these advances, difficult query-product pairs remain challenging when evidence from different perspectives supports conflicting conclusions. In such cases, a single reasoning pass may fail to reconcile these conflicts and ensure that decisive evidence guides the final judgment.

To address this challenge, we propose Collaborative
Adjudication with Debate and Replay Experience (CADRE),
a framework for evidence-based adjudication in
e-commerce relevance assessment. CADRE organizes three expert agents sharing a frozen backbone to analyze
product attributes, user intent, and business rules in
separate contexts, while a trainable Verifier assesses
their supporting evidence against the applicable matching
criteria to determine relevance. Under expert disagreement, a Verifier-guided debate elicits
targeted clarification and revision of disputed
interpretations before final adjudication. We optimize
the Verifier through multi-turn group relative policy
optimization and adapt successful-experience replay
so that trajectories yielding correct terminal labels
can be reused alongside fresh rollouts in subsequent
updates. For efficient online serving, we further use
CADRE as an offline teacher to distill its relevance
judgments into a lightweight student model. Offline evaluations and online A/B tests on an e-commerce search advertising platform demonstrate that CADRE improves relevance classification and key business and relevance satisfaction metrics.

Relevance Modeling, Large Language Model, Multi-Agent Debate, Reinforcement Learning, Experience Replay, E-Commerce Search

1. 
Introduction

Relevance modeling is a cornerstone of e-commerce search,
as accurate query–product matching is essential to
effective product discovery and a satisfying user experience. Earlier approaches predominantly rely on BERT-style encoders to learn semantic interactions between queries and products from annotated data 
(
Devlin et al., 2019
; 
Yao et al., 2022
)
. More recently, large language models (LLMs) have been adopted to bring broader knowledge and stronger contextual reasoning to relevance judgments through prompting and fine-tuning 
(
Mehrdad et al., 2024
)
. Chain-of-Thought (CoT) approaches further express these judgments as explicit reasoning steps that connect query understanding, product analysis, and the application of matching criteria 
(
Tang et al., 2025
; 
Zhao et al., 2025
; 
Dong et al., 2026
)
.

However, difficult query–product pairs often involve conflicting evidence from user intent, product attributes, and business rules, requiring the model to determine which evidence is decisive under the applicable matching criteria. Preference optimization methods such as DPO 
(
Rafailov et al., 2023
)
 and KTO 
(
Fang et al., 2025
)
, together with RL methods such as GRPO 
(
Dong et al., 2026
)
, refine relevance reasoning through feedback on generated judgments, but resolving these conflicts remains challenging. As illustrated in Figure 
1
, an intent-based interpretation may override a correctly identified attribute mismatch within the same reasoning context. Even when different views are generated separately, their initial reports may leave unresolved why a particular attribute should be decisive or whether a matching rule applies, requiring further examination before a final judgment can be justified. Learning from difficult cases also poses a training challenge, since trajectories yielding correct relevance labels may be scarce within a limited rollout budget and, once discovered, may not be effectively reused in subsequent updates.

To address these challenges, we propose Collaborative
Adjudication with Debate and Replay Experience (CADRE),
a framework for evidence-based adjudication in
e-commerce relevance assessment. The framework comprises
three complementary components.

(1)

Multi-agent analysis with a dedicated Verifier

assigns product analysis, user intent understanding,
and business-rule interpretation to agents with
separate contexts over a shared frozen backbone.
A trainable Verifier compares their analyses and
relevance judgments to assess the supporting evidence
and determine which matching criteria apply to
the query–product pair.

(2)

Verifier-guided debate
 selectively extends
adjudication based on disagreement among the experts’
initial labels. For selected inputs, the Verifier asks
focused questions to clarify disputed evidence and
matching criteria, then integrates the revised expert
reports into a final judgment.

(3)

Successful-experience replay
 builds on
Reinforcement Learning with Experience Replay
(RLEP) 
(
Zhang et al., 2025
)
.
We adapt this approach to multi-turn Verifier training
by retaining trajectories with correct terminal labels
and their original interaction contexts, and replaying
them alongside fresh rollouts in subsequent updates.

For efficient online deployment, we distill CADRE’s
reasoning into a lightweight student that directly
scores query–product pairs.
On an in-house multilingual relevance dataset, CADRE
improves accuracy and Macro-F1 by 3.73 and 13.64
percentage points, respectively, over LLM-Replay-GRPO
with additional context. In a 7-day online A/B test, the distilled student
achieves relative gains of 1.69% in revenue per mille
(RPM) and 0.76% in click-through rate (CTR), alongside
an absolute increase of 0.3 percentage points in relevance
satisfaction score (RS), over the existing production
baseline.

2. 
Related Works

Early relevance models rely on lexical matching, with TF-IDF 
(
Ramos and others, 2003
)
 and BM25 
(
Robertson et al., 2009
)
 scoring query–document pairs through term statistics. Although computationally efficient, these methods have limited ability to capture semantic associations beyond lexical overlap. Neural approaches such as DSSM 
(
Huang et al., 2013
)
 learn dense representations that bring semantically related queries and documents closer in a shared space. Pre-trained encoders such as BERT 
(
Devlin et al., 2019
)
 further introduce contextual representations for semantic matching, while ColBERT 
(
Khattab and Zaharia, 2020
)
 and Poly-encoders 
(
Humeau et al., 2020
)
 explore different trade-offs between interaction quality and computational cost. These advances improve semantic matching, but complex product requirements and implicit shopping intent motivate methods that can incorporate broader knowledge and reason about the basis of a relevance judgment.

Recent studies therefore explore LLMs for relevance
assessment, drawing on their broad knowledge and
contextual reasoning capabilities 
(
Mehrdad et al., 2024
; 
Wang et al., 2024
)
.
Subsequent work further grounds these capabilities
in domain-specific knowledge and matching criteria
to address the requirements of e-commerce relevance. An early effort in this direction, behavior-augmented prompting 
(
Chen et al., 2025
)
, supplies behavioral context for relevance prediction. More recent work further explores domain knowledge and matching criteria, with MPCoT 
(
Qiu et al., 2026
)
 incorporating business rules alongside intent and attribute analysis, while K-CARE 
(
Chen et al., 2026
)
 addresses missing domain context through behavioral anchors and expert-curated prototypes. Together, these works show the value of supplying domain context that may be absent from, or insufficiently elicited by, the model’s parametric knowledge.

Another line of research seeks to enhance reasoning quality and alignment with matching rubrics through more informative supervision. LREF 
(
Tang et al., 2025
)
 and ADORE 
(
Fang et al., 2025
)
 use preference optimization to calibrate relevance judgments, while SHE 
(
Jiao et al., 2026
)
, TaoSR-AGRL 
(
Yang et al., 2026
)
, and LORE 
(
Lu et al., 2026
)
 develop stepwise rewards, rule-aware feedback, and progressive reasoning supervision, respectively. SHE organizes relevance assessment into five stages, and TaoSR-AGRL follows a predefined category–attribute–relevance pattern. Prior work also addresses the reliability of relevance reasoning and its alignment with final predictions. TaoSR1 
(
Dong et al., 2026
)
 combines preference optimization with RL to mitigate reasoning errors and inconsistencies between reasoning and final answers. More recently, Graph-GRPO 
(
Che et al., 2026
)
 models relevance reasoning as a dependency graph and propagates reward signals across dependent reasoning components, enabling fine-grained credit assignment beyond uniform sequence-level feedback. These methods strengthen the supervision of relevance
reasoning, while leaving room to investigate how
disagreements among complementary analyses can guide
targeted re-examination of evidence and matching
criteria before final adjudication.

Effective RL training also depends on obtaining and reusing informative trajectories within a limited rollout budget. In relevance modeling, TaoSR1 and SHE use difficulty-based filtering to improve the informativeness of training groups 
(
Dong et al., 2026
; 
Jiao et al., 2026
)
, while TaoSR-AGRL introduces targeted ground-truth guidance to regenerate trajectories for low-reward cases 
(
Yang et al., 2026
)
. These strategies improve the utility of newly sampled trajectories, while reusing previously discovered successes offers a complementary direction. In the broader context of LLM reasoning, RLEP 
(
Zhang et al., 2025
)
 collects successful trajectories from an RL-trained policy and combines them with fresh rollouts during a subsequent training run. These studies highlight the complementary roles of discovering useful trajectories and retaining successful experiences, providing a basis for investigating experience reuse in multi-turn relevance assessment.

Knowledge distillation provides a practical route for deploying LLM-based relevance models under online latency constraints. Recent work transfers supervision from reasoning
LLMs to lightweight BERT models 
(
Agrawal et al., 2025
; 
Zhao et al., 2025
; 
Xia et al., 2026
)
, while MPCoT 
(
Qiu et al., 2026
)
 introduces latent reasoning distillation and Graph-GRPO 
(
Che et al., 2026
)
 uses graph-node-based multi-head distillation to supervise compact students with structured reasoning signals. These studies establish knowledge distillation as a practical connection between reasoning-intensive offline teachers and
efficient online relevance models.

3.2. 
Verifier-Guided Multi-Agent Reasoning

Our framework organizes relevance assessment around independent
expert analyses and a dedicated Verifier. The experts examine
complementary evidence in separate contexts, while the Verifier
evaluates their reports to determine the relevance judgment.
For inputs selected by a predefined disagreement criterion,
the Verifier initiates a focused debate to obtain clarification
and revised analyses before making the final decision.

3.2.1. 
Multi-Agent Analysis and Adjudication

Prior work has leveraged diverse sources of contextual
information to support e-commerce relevance
judgments 
(
Qiu et al., 2026
; 
Chen et al., 2026
)
.
We build on this idea by assigning complementary analytical
roles to three expert agents, each analyzing the query-product
pair 
 in a separate context over a shared frozen backbone.
Role-specific prompts and inputs induce specialization while holding model capacity fixed. Each expert develops its analysis without observing
the other experts’ reports, leaving cross-perspective
comparison to the Verifier. We freeze the expert backbone
to reduce optimization overhead and retain its general-purpose
response policy, while concentrating task-specific learning
on the Verifier’s evidence adjudication and targeted questioning.

The 
Analyst Agent
 receives

, comprising structured comparison
criteria and an attribute schema adapted from

Zhao et al. (2025)
, covering fourteen dimensions including
category, brand, and material.
It first generates attribute-level matching results

 and then reasons over them to
determine relevance.
Each attribute is assessed as a match, mismatch, or
unspecified; a mismatch requires explicitly conflicting
values on both sides, while an attribute specified on
only one side is marked as unspecified and supports
neither agreement nor conflict.
These comparison states describe attribute-level
evidence, while the final relevance judgment also
depends on the applicable matching criteria.
The 
Ruler Agent
 receives

, containing the original text of
the platform’s relevance guidelines concerning
categories, brands, model designations, attributes,
main-product versus accessory relations, and multi-SKU
listings, including precedence rules for overlapping mismatch conditions.
It first identifies the applicable clauses

 and then derives its judgment
by reasoning about the pair under those clauses.
As the only expert receiving historical behavioral
context, the 
Intent Agent
 receives

, comprising the
top-5 historical search terms associated with the
candidate product and the top-5 product titles clicked
in historical searches for the current query, to infer
the shopping need expressed by the query and assess
the candidate product’s functional substitutability.

Each expert 
 produces a first-round report
containing its analysis 
 and a predicted label

. The trainable 
Verifier Agent

receives the query–product pair and all three reports,
examines the evidence supporting each interpretation,
and assesses the applicability of the matching criteria
invoked by the experts. It then produces an initial
adjudication rationale 
 and a label

. The Verifier retains independent decision
authority, so its judgment may differ from the experts’
shared label even when all three agree.
The Verifier can thus retain useful observations from expert
reports even when all three initial predictions are incorrect.

3.2.2. 
Selective Verifier-Guided Debate

To control the additional cost of expert interaction, we
initiate debate if and only if the three experts’
first-round relevance labels are not unanimous, covering
both a two-to-one label split and three distinct labels.
When all three experts agree, the Verifier’s initial
adjudication serves as the final prediction, even if it
differs from their shared label. For inputs selected for
debate, the Verifier examines the conflicting reports and
generates one focused question for each expert to clarify
the evidence or matching criteria underlying its judgment.

Each expert receives its own question together with its first-round report and returns a revised analysis and label 
. The questions direct attention to disputed interpretations, such as whether an attribute mismatch is decisive or whether a business rule applies to the current pair. Experts respond independently, without observing one another’s reports or communicating directly, while the Verifier coordinates the examination of disagreements across their analyses. After this single round of questions and responses, the Verifier integrates the initial reports and subsequent feedback to produce the final adjudication rationale 
 and label 
. This bounded protocol allows disputed evidence to be re-examined while concentrating additional interaction on cases with expert disagreement.

Verifier Training with Experience Replay

We train the Verifier to perform the adjudication and
questioning described in
Section 
3.2

the shared backbone Qwen3-14B of the three expert agents frozen.

The resulting checkpoint

The original dataset provides relevance labels but

4.1. 
Dataset

We evaluate CADRE on an in-house e-commerce dataset with
78,997 training and 18,710 test query–product pairs.
Each pair is labeled as 
Strongly Relevant
,

Substitute
, or one of five irrelevant categories:

,

, 
Attribute Mismatch
,
and 
Main Product vs. Accessory

When multiple mismatch conditions apply to the same query-product pair, annotators assign a single label, following the most severe applicable mismatch.
To mitigate label imbalance, we use label-specific
sampling quotas targeting an approximately 1:1 ratio
between relevant and irrelevant pairs and roughly
balanced representation of the five irrelevant categories.
The training and test splits have similar label proportions.
Detailed dataset statistics are provided in

For offline evaluation, we assess both fine-grained relevance classification and irrelevant-product identification. For seven-class classification, we report Accuracy (ACC), Macro-F1, and Weighted-F1. Since irrelevant product exposure can degrade the search experience

Strongly Relevant
 and 
Substitute
 as negative and the remaining five categories as positive (irrelevant). These metrics assess the model’s ability to identify
irrelevant products while limiting erroneous rejection
of relevant candidates.

4.4. 
Performance

4.4.1. 
Overall Performance.

As shown in Table 
1
, CADRE
achieves the highest reported seven-class classification
scores, with 0.8334 ACC, 0.7766 Macro-F1, and 0.8321
Weighted-F1. Compared with LLM-Replay-GRPO with additional
context, these results represent improvements of 3.73,
13.64, and 4.06 percentage points, respectively.
CADRE also achieves the highest irrelevant-class F1
of 0.8936, indicating stronger overall performance
in identifying irrelevant query–product pairs.
Together with its fine-grained mismatch predictions,
this capability can support filtering decisions that
prioritize severe mismatches, helping reduce irrelevant
product exposure and improve the search experience.

Expert Oracle achieves an accuracy of 0.8051. CADRE exceeds this coverage by 2.83 percentage points,
indicating that the framework can correctly adjudicate
some instances where all three initial expert labels
are incorrect. This result supports CADRE’s robustness
to imperfect initial expert predictions, as its final
adjudication draws on supporting evidence and any debate
feedback without requiring every expert to predict
the correct label. Appendix 
D
 provides qualitative
examples of independent adjudication and correction
through Verifier-guided debate.

4.4.2. 
Effectiveness of CADRE

With additional context, Verifier-SFT without debate
outperforms LLM-SFT by 6.70, 9.71, and 7.25 percentage
points in ACC, Macro-F1, and Weighted-F1, respectively.
Without debate, Verifier-GRPO outperforms Verifier-SFT
by 2.19, 2.92, and 1.85 percentage points in ACC, Macro-F1,
and Weighted-F1, respectively, demonstrating the benefit
of reinforcement learning for adjudicating expert reports. CADRE also outperforms Verifier-Replay-GRPO without
debate across all six reported metrics, supporting
the contribution of selective debate within the
Replay-GRPO setting.
By incorporating selective debate and successful-experience
replay, CADRE further extends the gains over the same
Verifier-SFT baseline to 3.14, 4.15, and 2.76 points,
respectively.

4.4.3. 
Effectiveness of KD

We compare mBERT students trained with different CoT components
produced by CADRE. Compared with gold-label supervision alone,
the best configuration, 
Experts CoTs + Verifier CoT
,
improves ACC, Macro-F1, and Weighted-F1 by 3.53, 6.65,
and 5.05 percentage points, respectively, and
irrelevant-class F1 by 4.85 points.
These results support the value of the teacher’s reasoning
beyond label supervision.
Among the CoT-based variants, 
Verifier CoT

outperforms the concatenated 
Experts CoTs
 across
all seven-class metrics (e.g., Macro-F1 0.7306 vs. 0.7229).
This suggests that the trained Verifier’s adjudicated reasoning
provides more effective distillation supervision than simply
concatenating expert analyses.

4.6. 
Online Evaluation

For deployment, we train the best student on 1.64 million
human-labeled query–product pairs, using gold labels
for classification and CADRE-generated CoTs for
reasoning distillation.
We conducted a 7-day online A/B test in an e-commerce
search advertising system, allocating 10% of user
traffic each to this student and the existing
production baseline.
During serving, predefined business rules and calibrated
thresholds map query–item relevance predictions to
discrete levels, filtering candidates below the
relevance threshold before ranking. We assess business performance using Revenue Per Mille
(RPM) and Click-Through Rate (CTR), and relevance using
Relevance Satisfaction Score (RS), defined as the
platform’s traffic-weighted strong-relevance metric.
We sample results for the same queries from the two
experimental buckets under the same conditions and
submit them to human experts for relevance assessment.
The query set covers approximately 90% of search
traffic, and RS aggregates query-level strong-relevance
rates weighted by each query’s search traffic.
Compared with the existing production baseline, the
CADRE-distilled student achieves relative gains of

1.69
% in RPM and 
0.76
% in CTR,
alongside an absolute increase of

0.3
 percentage points in RS. These results demonstrate that the CADRE-distilled
student improves commercial performance while also
increasing the traffic-weighted strong-relevance rate.

5. 
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced CADRE to improve evidence integration in
e-commerce relevance modeling. Its central design
separates complementary expert analysis from learned
adjudication, enabling a Verifier to weigh evidence
across perspectives and seek clarification through
selective debate. Multi-turn reinforcement learning
optimizes this adjudication process, while experience
replay preserves successful trajectories for reuse
during training. We further transfer CADRE’s reasoning to a lightweight
student for online deployment.
Offline experiments demonstrate improved relevance
prediction, while online A/B testing confirms that
the distilled student improves business performance
and relevance satisfaction over the existing
production baseline. Given the current fixed-round debate protocol, future
work will explore adaptive policies for expert selection,
evidence clarification, and stopping to tailor the scope
and duration of interaction to input difficulty while
accounting for computational cost.

Abstract

Approaches to authorship verification or attribution typically model the style of a document, asking whether two documents were likely written by the same author. While recent approaches have been performant, such approaches largely ignore modeling the authors themselves and how varied their style is. Here, we introduce multiple alternatives for modeling the author and demonstrate that allowing an author’s representation to capture multiple consistent but distinct writing styles yields significant performance improvements. Using our new authorship model, we then examine 
when
 an author writes in a way that is most idiomatic of their style across four scenarios: (i) variation in the size of their writing audiences, (ii) temporal distance from when the model was trained, (iii) the effect of preceding context, and (iv) the effect of genre norms on style. Across all four experiments, we demonstrate that authors vary substantially in how idiolectic their writing is. Our results have clear implications for both the modeling and data used for authorship verification and attribution, showing that authors need to be represented with their multiple styles and that some settings produce text that is much more informative about who wrote that text.

Authorship attribution and verification models have traditionally focused on identifying distinguishing and systematic stylistic differences in how an author writes documents

Here, we aim to learn representations of authors that explicitly model any natural variation. Our study is motivated by this prior work in showing that authors are sensitive to context in how they communicate

;

Using our most accurate model of authorship, we then quantify how authors vary in their stylistic behavior in different contexts—i.e., when do authors write most like themselves? In four tests across audience, time, context, and genre, we demonstrate that authors reliably shift styles.

Our work has the following contributions.
First, we demonstrate that modeling the author’s systematic style variation yields a significant performance improvement, regardless of how a document’s style is represented.
Second, downstream analyses demonstrate that context is critical for accurate attribution: (1) authors vary more in style when writing across diverse contexts, (2) in settings with strong genre norms, authors are most distinctive in contexts where they have more stylistic flexibility, (3) in most settings, an author’s style changes as they age, drifting from their earlier forms, and (4) surprisingly, in a negative result, authors do not shift their style when writing replies to another author. These results emphasize that the "where" of a text is essential to accurate attribution. All code will be released (CC-by-SA 4.0).

2 
Recognizing an Author’s Style

This work touches on multiple branches of research in NLP and Linguistics, focusing on quantifying an author’s writing style.

Idiolect

Authorship attribution depends, in part, on an author having a distinct writing style, or idiolect. In forensic linguistics, the idiolect is viewed as multifaceted–not just word usage, but also includes features such as syntactic and discursive patterns 
(
Turell, 2011
)
, and is thought to be persistent over time. However, two polarized views of idiolect have been formalized in the linguistics literature 
Barlow (2013)
; 
Barber and Garcia Ramirez (2021)
. 
Labov (2011)
 argued that idiolects can be understood as a reflection of the grammar of the speech community. Under this view, all idiolects can be reduced to the different combinations of sociolects (regular variation associated with a community).

A contrasting view holds that individual languages should be primary, such that sociolect emerges from the grouping of idiolects 
Hudson (1996)
. Yet, open questions remain as to how to characterize idiolect systematically— beyond dismissing it as idiosyncratic patterns—and how to separate idiolect from sociolect, if that distinction exists. While prior studies suggest that idiolect is stable over a short time period 
Coulthard (2004)
; 
Barlow (2013)
; 
Mojedano Batel et al. (2024)
, little is known about the temporal variation of idiolect over a longer period. Given the known influence of social conventions of genre and register on language use 
(
Bischoff et al., 2020
, e.g.,)
, to what degree do these conventions constrain the variation within idiolects 
Barber and Garcia Ramirez (2021)
? Understanding idiolect variations has practical implications for stylometry and forensic linguistics as well as for the ontology of language itself 
Carden (1970)
.

Author Attribution Models

Computational models of authorship attribution depend on some explicit or implicit representations of the style of a document 
(
Neal et al., 2017
)
. Early computational work was focused on explicit linguistic features that are highly interpretable, including function words 
(
Mosteller and Wallace, 1963
; 
Garcia and Martin, 2006
)
, character n-grams 
Sapkota et al. (2015)
, and syntactic structures 
Tschuggnall and Specht (2014)
. 
Gill and Swartz (2011)
 proposed learning a Dirichlet prior over distributions of only function words to compare authors; while showing the benefits of adding structure, this approach was only tested with two authors and 14 documents. Despite these methods’ early successes, they have been superseded by more powerful deep learning models 
Pardo and Rosso (2019)
; 
Stamatatos et al. (2022)
; 
Bevendorff et al. (2024)
.

There are two major directions in the deep learning approach with implicit authorship representations. Prompting-based work directly uses LLMs to identify authorship through text-only instructions and examples, with promising results 
(
Huang et al., 2024
; 
Hu et al., 2024
, e.g.,)
. However, this approach is limited to a small number of authors due to LLMs’ context window sizes and cost. Retrieval-based approaches typically fine-tune an LLM to generate dense vector representations of style through contrastive learning, enabling efficient ranking over tens of thousands of authors 
(
Boenninghoff et al., 2019
; 
Rivera-Soto et al., 2021
; 
Zhu and Jurgens, 2021
; 
Wang et al., 2023
; 
Fincke and Boschee, 2024
, e.g.,)
.

Currently, most authorship attribution modeling techniques model the documents and do not explicitly parametrize a representation of the author.

One example of a simple author modeling approach comes from 
Zhu and Jurgens (2021)
, which leverages document representations to build a Gaussian distribution at the author level. However, there is no notion of whether the distribution is a strong fit for the model. Further, this style of modeling is rigid, causing problems for authors who have a more complex idiolect.

We aim to bridge this gap by building author models based on document representations of the text. We explore many different representations, as the strength of the representation is crucial in building performant models. Additionally, we analyze various modeling techniques for authors and enable modeling flexibility by leveraging a dev set for each author. This approach allows us to build models tailored to a given author’s idiolect.

3 
Datasets

To study author idiolect across diverse contexts and styles, we curate English-language data from four sources: comments from Reddit 
Baumgartner et al. (2020)
, literary books, emails from the public GMANE archive 
Bevendorff et al. (2020)
, and Wikipedia 
(
Israeli et al., 2025
)
. These sources contain sufficient numbers of documents per author to model intra-author variation in different contexts and across time. We describe the preprocessing and data preparation steps next.

Preprocessing

For all data sources, we impose two general requirements on authors regarding their document lengths and the total number of documents. First, documents need to be long enough to evoke a specific style, and therefore we require at least 100 words; this limit is on the lower end for length in past work 
Boenninghoff et al. (2019)
 and ensures that the task is more realistic and challenging. Second, to enable a sufficient number of documents for downstream analyses, we retain only authors who have 100 or more documents, up to 250 total for the author. We collect 10K authors meeting both requirements from each source. See Appendix 
A
 for full details.

Reddit

A large amount of content on Reddit comes from bots. To avoid modeling non-human users, we removed these accounts using a large curated stoplist and filtering rules (e.g., usernames ending in “Bot"). Using the remaining accounts, we constructed a balanced subset of 10K authors by evenly sampling 100 authors from each combination of (1) total years of Reddit participation (binned into integer buckets from 0 to 9 years), and (2) the number of unique subreddits they had posted in (binned into ranges: 1–5, 6–10, …, 46–50). This stratified sampling ensures a diverse group of authors in both longevity and community participation.

Books

Books represent a unique genre due to their length and register constraints, which influence the style and narrative. To model this influence, we sample 10K novels converted to plain text 
(
Gao et al., 2020
)
, partition their content at the paragraph level, and retain only paragraphs with 100+ words.

GMANE

The public GMANE archive contains emails and replies on mailing lists for a variety of topics 
(
Bevendorff et al., 2020
)
. We perform document sampling at the author level with respect to emails and replies with the technique described in Appendix 
A
. This email-and-reply setting allows us to later measure the effect of style influence.

Wikipedia

Wikipedia contains diverse contributions from authors on its article and talk pages. We select Wikipedia authors from the Million Author Corpus 
(
Israeli et al., 2025
)
 and again perform the metadata sampling described in Appendix 
A
.

Modeling Author Style Variation

We evaluate four types of parametric representations of authors derived from the style of their documents. To control for a potential interaction between style representation and author model, we consider six different style representations, two interpretable and four neural. We use document representations to decouple the problem of learning stylistic features from modeling author-specific patterns; by first encoding style, author models operate on stable, reusable, topic-deconfounded representations rather than raw text.

4.2 
Document Stylistic Representations

The author models are fit as distributions of their documents’ style representations. Several methods have been proposed for representing document style in authorship attribution, and we employ six distinct representations. (1) Style is represented as the frequency of the 67 lexical and syntactic features from Biber’s Multidimensional Analysis framework 
(
Biber, 1988
)
 and 29 additional features from 
Clarke and Grieve (2017)
 and 
Clarke (2019)
. These interpretable features characterize style through rule-based linguistic markers. For scalability, we use NeuroBiber 
(
Alkiek et al., 2025
)
 to estimate these features.
(2) An alternative to the low-level style representation of Biber is LISA 
(
Patel et al., 2023
)
, which uses a PLM to generate interpretable embeddings aligned to stylistic dimensions like tone, emotional expression, and linguistic patterns. These higher-level features capture a complementary view of style, though one not directly optimized for the task of authorship attribution.
(3) The LUAR 
(
Rivera-Soto et al., 2021
)
 model is a PLM trained via contrastive learning to represent documents by the same author with similar embeddings. The model is one of the first accurate neural style embedding models.
(4) The PCM model 
Kim et al. (2025)
 is the current state of the art for multilingual neural style embedding.
(5) The StyleDistance model 
Patel et al. (2025)
 was trained using synthetic data to maximally de-confound topic and style.
(6) The SADIRI model 
Fincke and Boschee (2024)
 uses adaptive exemplar filtering for contrastive learning to create representations that perform well in cross-genre settings.

Is the GMM just learning topical variation?

Two of the state-of-the-art models we use, SADIRI and StyleDistance, are designed to be robust to topic through maximizing cross-genre performance or training on synthetic data, respectively. The GMM improves performance significantly with both representations, suggesting the potential topic confound is not responsible. Appendix 
C

Our prior setup used 50 documents per author to fit a GMM and an additional 25 documents to select the optimal

However, in some settings, fewer documents may be available, raising the possibility that a GMM would be unneeded—i.e., fewer documents could provide insufficient evidence of systematic variation in an author’s style. To test this, we follow a similar setup where we train the same GMM models on varying numbers of training documents, using the best-performing SADIRI style embedding. To test for saturation, we include an additional 30 documents per author, still choosing

Does contextual diversity influence stylistic consistency?

Authors may naturally vary their style when communicating with different audiences in order to mirror community norms. While certain stylistic features may remain relatively stable across topics and communities, authorship attribution becomes substantially more challenging under cross
-
domain or cross
-
genre variation. Indeed, previous work highlights how attribution systems struggle when texts differ in topic, even when authored by the same individual 
(
Kestemont et al., 2019
)
. However, there is evidence that stylometric markers retain their informativeness even across multiple topics 
(
Litvinova et al., 2018
)
. Here, we measure this phenomenon on Reddit by using subreddits as a proxy for community, topic, and genre, asking to what degree authors are predictable relative to their contextual diversity.

Setup

We reuse the SADIRI embedding GMM models for the 10K Reddit authors from Section

Results

Authors with participation in more communities have less distinctive style (Figure

Second, while our style representations are trained to be robust to content or topic, many authorship attribution models incorrectly learn author-topic associations

Do narrative conventions constrain author style?

Narrative conventions significantly shape and shift authorial style. 
Montfort et al. (2021)
 show that controlling referring expressions, such as how characters or locations are introduced, yields distinct narrative styles. Similarly, 
Schwartz et al. (2017)
 find that writing task framing—like continuing a story versus ending it—results in measurable stylistic variation. These works suggest narrative constraints influence expression beyond an author’s baseline tendencies.
Where then in novels—if anywhere—are authors least constrained by these conventions? We investigate these findings by evaluating author style at different segments of long-form documents using the books data.

Setup

From the data preprocessing step, books are segmented into chunks that are tagged based on their relative position within the book. We group these documents into deciles according to their relative position within the book. Then, we measure 
, aggregated on each of these deciles for all 10K authors and test documents.

Results

Figure

This suggests that the conventions of writing introductions and conclusions suppress an author’s own style, likely due to functional constraints; authors often open with more stylistically conventional exposition to orient the reader and close with summary or resolution. The middle sections, which tend to carry the core narrative, allow for more sustained and unconstrained expression of authorial voice. Our results suggest that when attributing authorship in long-form text, practitioners must account for genre and discourse constraints, as not all passages are equally informative about authorial style 
(
Mosteller and Wallace, 1963
)
.

How consistent are authors over time?

An individual’s language changes as they age, which raises the question of to what degree their style also changes. Previous work on shorter time frames has shown evidence that author idiolects tend to stay relatively stable 
(
Blas Arroyo, 2023
; 
Barlow, 2013
)
. Yet, the few sociolinguistic studies on longer time frames with only a few individuals found that their sociolect does change 
(
Rickford and Price, 2013
; 
Sankoff, 2019
)
, suggesting that idiolect might as well.

Setup

New GMM models are trained and evaluated on the SADIRI style embeddings using a chronological data split. The first 50 documents per author (temporally) in Reddit, GMANE, and Wikipedia are used to fit the GMM. The

Why are authors less predictable over time?

The drop in predictability could be due to multiple changes in stylistic behavior. Here, we test three hypotheses: 
H1
 The author becomes less stylistically consistent over time and is unable to be accurately identified, 
H2
 The author adopts a new, 
additional
 substyle that is not present initially, 
H3
 The author changes to a new, distinct style with equal complexity.

To test these hypotheses, we employ a sliding window approach that segments each author’s chronological document series into discrete temporal windows, allowing us to compare their styles at different points in time. Briefly, we use 50 consecutive documents to fit a GMM and compare the documents in other windows using the log-likelihood; full details are in Appendix 
G
.

Our results support one of the three hypotheses. First, we find evidence for consistent evolution of a stable but distinct idiolect; Figure 
6
 shows that held-out documents within a window are reliably identified as an author’s (the diagonal) but this identification drops over time. Thus, we do not find support for H1, which would have a decreasing likelihood over the diagonal. Second, we plot the mean number of components (Appendix Figure 
16
) showing that authors do not add or lose style components, maintaining complexity. Finally, Figure 
17
 shows that temporal degradation is driven more by location than variance drifts. This indicates stylistic displacement rather than inconsistency. Together, these results support H3: author style shifts over time without changing in complexity.

10 
Conclusion

In this study, we seek to model the style representations of 
authors
, rather than those of 
documents
, showing that such an approach leads to better representations of authorship. With large-scale analyses of 40K authors and millions of documents across four genres, we demonstrate the importance of the writing context. Authors exhibit stylistic variations across conversation contexts, genre norms, and time. Our findings provide a more nuanced understanding of idiolects, highlighting the need to account for context-dependent variation while modeling the invariant aspects of style. Beyond improving authorship verification, our findings can inform sociolinguistics and the development of fairer, more context-aware NLP systems.

Limitations

First, we acknowledge that it is possible that our model is learning a shortcut for idiolect, such as topic. Although each of the embeddings we use attempts to disentangle topic, there is no perfect way to do this. StyleDistance provides a strong signal regarding our modeling setup because it is explicitly trained on synthetic data to de-confound topic and style. Figure 
2
 shows that StyleDistance has one of the strongest performance gains from our setup, which makes us more confident that we are modeling stylistic features rather than topical ones. However, author models may still partially encode topic or community-level regularities rather than purely idiolectal variation. Although the held-out subreddit evaluation in Appendix 
C
 suggests the model retains substantial stylistic signal under cross-topic generalization, performance does decrease in this setting, indicating that some contextual confounding may remain even with an embedding optimized for cross-genre style representation.

Although we find a strong performance gain from our methodology, we acknowledge that there are limitations to our analysis. First, we leverage a moderate number of documents to build our author models (50), which may not always be feasible. We still believe our approach is beneficial even with a smaller number of documents, as shown in Appendix Figure 
9
, but further work would be needed to quantify the trade-offs relative to the number and length of documents available. Second, the model performance still varies depending on the setting in which the document was written. In Sections 6 to 9, we show many different contexts where an author appears to deviate from their own idiolect, as represented by our models. Additionally, in Appendix 
F
 we see that authors who write consistently in one setting are not guaranteed to write consistently in another. Thus, while our authorship models do improve performance, these models are still not fully robust to the reality of contextual effects on writing and would be limited in performance depending on where and when the data used to train and evaluate the models was collected.

Ethics Statement

Our study primarily focuses on the theoretical aspects of individual styles. We only made use of public datasets, and all authors in the datasets were properly anonymized to avoid any potential disclosure of real identities. We acknowledge that the authorship verification technology is dual-use. While such technology can be used for forensic investigation and historical authorship attribution, malicious use can also potentially compromise the anonymity of writers and contribute to mass surveillance. We urge the responsible deployment of such technology. Our study shows the fluidity of individual styles, challenging the assumption that individuals’ writing styles are invariant. Therefore, authorship verification technology should not be deployed in high-stakes scenarios without significant human oversight.

Appendix E 
Orthogonalization Analysis

E.1 
Stylistic Overlap Amongst Embeddings

We hypothesize that the discrepancy in performance across embeddings arises from differences in the stylistic information captured by each representation. In particular, we expect the SADIRI embedding to capture much of the stylistic variance represented in NeuroBiber, while additionally encoding a broader set of latent features. To test this, we measure the Expected Variation Ratio (EVR) across the embeddings. First, we perform Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) to bring all of the embeddings down to 96 dimensions. We then compute the EVR for each embedding, which represents the proportion of variance explained by each of the 96 latent dimensions obtained from SVD. By correlating these EVR profiles, we observe in Figure 
11
 that the principal components of these embeddings exhibit highly similar variance structures, suggesting that they encode overlapping stylistic information. At an aggregate level, the embeddings exhibit consistently high correlations, indicating the presence of a shared set of stylistic signals distributed across their latent spaces.

Figure 11: 
There is a clear overlap in the stylistic features that the six embeddings are capturing. 

The heatmap additionally reveals several clustering patterns among the embeddings. StyleDistance and SADIRI exhibit nearly identical EVR structures, while LISA and PCM form a second highly correlated pair. NeuroBiber and LUAR maintain strong correlations across nearly all embeddings, suggesting that they may capture a more generalized stylistic representation. The near-perfect correlation between StyleDistance and SADIRI is especially interesting because StyleDistance is explicitly trained to be content-independent. The shared variance structure suggests that the dominant latent directions captured by the SADIRI embedding are not based on semantics or topic, but are strongly organized around stylistic variation. Further, these results suggest that the performance improvements observed in Figure 
2
 are likely driven by stylistic structure.

E.2 
Performance Impact From Orthogonalization

Although orthogonalization is relatively cheap in terms of time complexity, one might question the impact on performance. In this experiment, we analyze the performance of the mixed author model setup with GMMs trained with a diagonal covariance matrix with and without orthogonalization. Indeed, the experiments without orthogonalization were quicker, but from Figure 
12
, we can clearly see a strong jump in performance for every embedding. LISA and StyleDistance observe the most performance gain, which is surprising because they have a much lower dimensionality than PCM and SADIRI. These results suggest that the raw embedding spaces violate the independence assumptions imposed by diagonal covariance GMMs. Orthogonalization alleviates this by decorrelating the latent dimensions, which satisfies a key assumption of diagonal covariance matrix GMM modeling.

There is a large boost to performance for all embeddings after using orthogonalization in our modeling setup. This indicates that the step is crucial for our diagonal covariance setup.

Appendix F 
Are authors consistent across genre changes?

Another interesting fluctuation in writing style occurs when authors write in different genres. Linguistic literature shows us that authors are often consistent across multiple genres 
(
Mojedano Batel et al., 2024
)
. Another study has shown that an author’s idiolect tends to have low intra-author variation when writing about different topics 
(
Litvinova et al., 2018
)
. We look to evaluate these findings by observing authors who write in Wikipedia article pages and talk pages.

Setup

First, we filter our dataset to include only posts from authors who have at least 10 posts in both article and talk pages. Next, we compute the quartiles with respect to consistency for article pages. We evaluate these sets of authors together to determine how their performance changes when evaluated in the talk page setting. For context, article pages are the encyclopedia articles that provide factual information regarding subjects. Talk pages serve as a forum for improving article pages, so they are inherently much less formal.

Results

Surprisingly, we see that the most consistent authors in article pages are the least consistent in talk pages. On the other hand, the least consistent authors in formal settings seem to be the most consistent in the informal setting. The key takeaway here is that authors who write stylistically consistently in one setting are not guaranteed to do the same in another setting. Although there are multiple studies supporting the claim that the author’s idiolect remains consistent across genres, these studies only look at fewer than 100 authors. Our analysis for Wikipedia captures posts from over 2000 authors; thus, it could be plausible that these papers did not have a large enough sample size to evaluate their claims.

Figure 14: 
Authors who are consistent in one genre are not guaranteed to be consistent in another. This Wikipedia analysis shows us that stratified authors who are consistent in a formal setting actually are the least consistent authors in a more informal setting.

Appendix I 
Additional Training Details

The RoBERTa model was trained to predict the specific author from a document. Documents are clipped at 512 tokens. The model is trained for 3 epochs, and we use a downsampled 100K development set to choose the best-performing model across the epochs. We use 
roberta-base
 as the base model, a batch size of 32, and the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 2e-5, weight decay of 0.01, and 10 warm-up steps. We did not perform extensive hyperparameter tuning for the RoBERTa baseline; instead, we used a standard configuration to provide a strong and reproducible reference point. We observed similar poor performance when using DistilRoBERTa, indicating that the particular model is likely not causing performance degradation.

For all of our experiments, we trained and evaluated on NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPUs. For the nearest neighbor and centroid approaches, roughly 4 GPU hours on one A6000 were sufficient for training and evaluating our models. In the GMM case, we needed up to 24 GPU hours for training and evaluating on larger embeddings, like the SADIRI embedding. The RoBERTa experiment required roughly 48 GPU hours for training and evaluation.

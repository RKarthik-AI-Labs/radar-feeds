Abstract

A grounded reasoning chain cites a frame span and a box for every step, so a reader can check where the model looked. Whether the answer 
depends
 on the cited region is a different question. We test it by intervention: the regions a model cited in its own chain are blacked out, and the share of correct answers that flip is compared with blacking out boxes of the same size elsewhere on the same frames. The paired difference, the cite gap, needs no annotation. We apply it to 2–3B video–language models trained on exact CLEVRER chains and on pseudo-labelled NExT-QA chains. With the same weights, the cite gap is 0.089 on CLEVRER and 0.003 on NExT-QA. Pooled over models, masking the cited boxes flips 2.2 times as many correct answers as the control on CLEVRER and 1.1 times as many on NExT-QA, also among the answers that depend on the video at all, while the boxes pass a grounding check on both. Masking the cited 
entity
 on every frame, instead of the cited box, flips 1.9 times as many answers as its control. The NExT-QA chains were supervised to cite two frames of entities that stay visible on most frames, so this outcome is what the supervision predicts; the point is that the usual check on grounded chains cannot see it. A citation can be verifiable and relevant without the answer depending on it, and a test of grounded reasoning should remove what a chain names, not only where it points.

1 
Introduction

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards has become the standard way to make multimodal models reason 
[
34
, 
13
, 
9
, 
42
]
. For video, where the evidence for an answer is spread over time and space, the natural refinement is a 
grounded
 chain: each step names what the model looked at, where, and when 
[
33
, 
5
, 
27
]
. Such a chain promises two things. It is 
verifiable
: a reader can check whether the cited region contains what the step claims. And it is 
faithful
: the answer is supposedly computed from the cited evidence. The first promise can be checked with a detector. The second is a claim about the model’s computation, and it is the one that matters if grounded chains are to serve as explanations. It has a consequence that can be tested at the input: if the answer rests on the cited evidence, removing that evidence should change the answer more than removing something else.

We test this consequence on small video–language models (VLMs), the 2–3B class, for two reasons. It is the class that is cheap enough to fine-tune, run on device, and evaluate under a suite of full-pass interventions per model. It is the class for which RL-induced shortcut behaviour has been reported 
[
44
]
, so the question of what its reasoning rests on is open. We train Qwen2.5-VL-3B 
[
2
]
 and InternVL3-2B 
[
54
]
 to answer causal video questions through chains of grounded triplets (sub-question, intermediate answer, spatiotemporal evidence) and ask whether the answers depend on what the chains cite.

A counterfactual self-citation test.

The tool is simple (
Fig.
 
1
). Run the model once and record the regions it cited. Run it again with exactly those regions blacked out. Run it a third time with boxes of the same sizes, on the same frames, moved to random positions. The fraction of correct answers that flip under the first intervention, minus the fraction that flip under the second, is the 
cite gap 
: how much more the answer depends on the cited pixels than on an equal area of occlusion elsewhere. Both interventions remove the same area at the same times, so the gap cannot be explained by occlusion itself; and the regions come from the model’s own output, so the test applies to any video, annotated or not. Three controls from the same family accompany it: a blind gap (all frames black), a gold-evidence gap (annotated evidence against size-matched random boxes) and an object-track gap (the decisive object removed for the whole video against size-matched copies elsewhere). The masking gaps are paired differences in flip rates with bootstrap intervals.

Findings.

The same weights behave differently on the two benchmarks. For the model trained on a mix of exact CLEVRER 
[
48
]
 chains and pseudo-labelled NExT-QA 
[
46
]
 chains, the cite gap is 0.089 on CLEVRER and 0.003 on NExT-QA; for the three other models tested on both it is 0.051 against 0.015, 0.042 against 0.004 and 0.030 against 0.001. Pooled over five chain models, blacking out the cited boxes flips 9.7% of correct answers against 4.5% for the control on CLEVRER, and 2.3% against 2.1% on NExT-QA. The NExT-QA gap stays small among the answers that depend on the video, on correct options, where every cited box matches a detector 
[
23
]
, and where the control leaves the cited area untouched (
Sec.
 
5.3
). The cited 
entity
 matters more than the cited box. It is visible on 14 of 16 frames while a citation covers two, and blacking it out on all of them flips 10.2% of correct answers against 5.5%; among questions answered correctly, the selected answer changes for 35% against 20%. Given that supervision, a small cite gap is unsurprising. What the case shows is that the usual check does not reveal it: the NExT-QA boxes agree with a detector, as the CLEVRER boxes agree with the annotation.

Contributions.

(i) Two annotation-free tests for grounded reasoning chains: a counterfactual self-citation test with a size-matched control and question-clustered paired intervals, and an entity-track test that removes what a chain names rather than where it points; with them, the diagnostics needed to read a gap (the intervention’s own flip rate as a bound, video dependence, control overlap and object-level controls, citation placement).
(ii) A case study on 2–3B VLMs trained to reason in grounded chains: exact CLEVRER chains from executed programs (100% agreement with ground truth over 256k checks), pseudo-labelled NExT-QA chains with a manual audit, supervised fine-tuning and GRPO on chains.
(iii) The finding that detector-agreement grounding does not distinguish citations the answer depends on from citations it does not: the boxes pass a grounding check on both benchmarks.

Grounded Chains for Small VLMs

Fig.
 
2
 gives the overview.

3.1 
Chain format

A chain is written inside 
<think>
 tags, one triplet per line, followed by the answer:

[n] Q: <sub-question> | A: <intermediate answer> |

[n]
 E: t=<start>-<end> box=(x0,y0,x1,y1) | deps=<ids>

The span 
 indexes a 128-position time grid over the clip, the box is in normalised image coordinates, and 
deps
 lists the earlier steps the step depends on. One parser serves the format reward, the process rewards and the self-citation test, so a citation is exactly what the model wrote in an 
E:
 field.

3.2 
Chain supervision

CLEVRER.

CLEVRER questions come with executable programs. We run each program through a traced executor over the ground-truth scene and turn every entity-producing step into a triplet: the evidence box comes from the released object proposals, matched to the ground-truth object by its unique attributes; the span is the interval over which the step’s object or event is observed; dependency links follow the program’s data flow. Predictive and counterfactual outcomes require the physics simulator, so their final answers come from the choice labels, but every observable intermediate step is grounded. After calibrating three annotation conventions (
Appendix
 
Q
), the executor reproduces the ground truth on all 256,449 descriptive questions and explanatory choices of the training and validation splits. Chains whose evidence box cannot be recovered (3.5%) are dropped. Each (question, choice) pair yields one chain and one yes/no item.

NExT-QA.

NExT-QA has no evidence annotation, so its chains are pseudo-labelled. A teacher (Qwen2.5-VL-7B) sees 16 frames, the question, the options and the correct option, and writes two to four observation steps, each naming a visible entity and the frame where it appears. Grounding DINO 
[
23
]
 (tiny, threshold 0.35) localises the entity on that frame; undetected steps are dropped, and so are chains with no surviving step. The detector box becomes the step’s evidence and the frame index becomes a span of 
 grid positions. Each chain yields two training items with the same steps, the correct option answered “yes” and one sampled wrong option answered “no”, so the steps do not depend on the option. In a manual audit of 100 chains (
Tab.
 
S17
, supplement) the box is on the named entity in 70% of the 299 steps and 95% of steps are relevant, but 92 chains contain only observations (“Where is the white dog?”) and no causal sub-question (
Sec.
 
6
).

Measuring Whether Citations Are Needed

Every masking test in the suite has the same form: an 
intervention
 on the input frames, a 
size-matched control
, and their paired difference over items. Let 
, 
 and 
 be a model’s answers to item 
 under the unmodified input, the intervention and the control, and let 
. The flip rate of a condition is the share of 
 it breaks, and the gap is the paired difference,

(2)

with a 95% interval from a paired bootstrap (2,000 resamples) over 
questions
, so the per-option items of one question are resampled together and their correlation is not mistaken for independent evidence. Because the control removes the same area at the same times, 
 reads as “how much more the answer depends on these pixels than on an equal area of other pixels”. Items undefined under one condition are dropped from both, so the two flip rates cover the same items. We read a gap by its size and by the ratio 
, not as a binary verdict, and we report 
 itself: the gap cannot exceed it under any control, so it bounds what the intervened pixels can account for. Masked area is given as a share of 
pixel-frames
, the pixels of the 16 sampled frames.

Blind gap.

Plain accuracy minus accuracy with every frame black. A model that answers from the question alone scores zero; the gap is a floor on how much the model uses the video at all.

Gold-evidence gap (CLEVRER only).

The annotated evidence regions of the question’s chain are blacked out on the sampled frames nearest their spans; the control blacks out boxes of the same sizes and spans at random positions.

Object-track gap (CLEVRER only).

On CLEVRER a single-frame box removes little, because the object is visible in the other frames. We therefore remove the decisive evidence object(s) of the chain (the candidate collision of the option for predictive and counterfactual questions, otherwise the last step) for the whole video, against size-matched copies of the same tracks at random positions that avoid the evidence. This is the strongest annotated test and applies to chain-free models too.

Cite gap (the self-citation test).

The intervention blacks out the regions cited in the model’s 
own
 chain on the unmodified input (every 
E:
 field; spans mapped from the 128-grid onto the 16 sampled frames; boxes covering the whole frame dropped). The control blacks out boxes of the same size on the same frames at uniformly random positions. It may overlap a cited box, which shrinks the gap: conservative when the gap is positive, the opposite when it is near zero. We therefore also report the gap on the items whose control leaves the cited area untouched (the control is a seeded draw and is replayed exactly). Under both interventions the model runs afresh, writing a new chain; only the answer is scored. Items whose chain cites no localisable region are dropped. The regions come from the model under test, so the test needs no annotation. It is a test of necessity at the input, not of whether the answer is computed through the chain (
Sec.
 
6
). A final control checks the masking itself: a chain-free model trained on the same questions is masked with a chain model’s citations and should show 
.

Entity-track gap (NExT-QA).

The object-track gap for video without annotation, with the chain supplying the object and a detector the track: every entity named by a “Where is 
entity
?” step is re-detected with Grounding DINO on all 16 sampled frames, every detection plus the cited boxes is blacked out, and the control places boxes of the same sizes on the same frames at random positions that avoid the entity boxes. Items whose entities are never detected are dropped. The chain names the entities and the detector finds them, so this test needs no annotation either.

Citation grounding.

The cite gap says whether the answer depends on a citation, not whether the citation is correct. On NExT-QA we check correctness separately: for every step of the form “Where is 
entity
?” the entity is re-detected with Grounding DINO on the cited frame, and the cited box is scored against the top detection (IoU, hit at IoU 0.5) alongside a same-size box at a random position. The detector is the family that produced the training boxes, so this measures agreement with the supervision, not ground truth.

Per-option evaluation.

Chain models answer one yes/no item per option, as trained, so a five-option question becomes five items. We report per-option accuracy (always-“no” baseline 0.80 on NExT-QA) and a 
selection
 accuracy that picks the option judged “yes” (credit split on ties, 
 if none), which is comparable to a 
letters prompt
 (one multiple-choice prompt answered with an option letter). Untrained reference models are evaluated in the same format, because the format itself costs accuracy (
Sec.
 
5.3
). Four of five NExT-QA items are wrong options, and a correct rejection rarely flips under a box mask, so pooled flip rates are diluted; we report the NExT-QA gaps also on the items whose option is correct.

Pooled over the five models, blacking out the cited boxes flips 9.7% of correct answers against 4.5% for the control: a gap of 0.053

And in a sparse scene a random box mostly covers background. On the items whose control lands on another object, the gap remains for the four models trained with CLEVRER chains (0.034–0.083, every interval above zero): their cited objects matter more than other objects. For the NExT-QA chain GRPO model, whose boxes are mostly off the named object (hit rate 0.15), it vanishes (0.007

More depends on the cited entity.

The named entities are detected on 14.0 of the 16 frames on average, so a citation of two frames removes little of them. The entity-track test (
Sec.
 
4

Tab.

Pooled over the two models it was run on, it flips 10.2% of correct answers against 5.5% for the control, a gap of 0.047 and a ratio of 1.9; on correct options the gap is 0.103, and the selected answer changes for 35–36% of questions against 19–22%. Both gaps exceed the bound on the box-level effect, so the difference between the two tests is not an artefact of their controls. Nor is it an artefact of entities copied from the question, or of a control that only removes background: the gap is as large on the items none of whose named entities shares a word with the question or the options (0.046–0.049; 
Tab.
 
S14

Relative to the blind flip rate, the pooled gaps are 0.01 for the cited boxes on NExT-QA and 0.11 on CLEVRER, 0.17 for the entity track, and 1.0 for the CLEVRER object track, which removes annotated decisive objects. We did not run the entity-track test on a chain-free model, so it shows that the answer depends on the entities the chain names more than on other entities, not that the chain is the reason.

6 
Discussion

What the tests show.

The cite gap and the entity-track gap measure dependence at the input: they compare losing the cited pixels with losing an equal area of other pixels. They do not show that the answer is computed 
through
 the chain: on CLEVRER chain-free models depend on the annotated evidence as much as chain models do, and we did not run the entity-track test on a chain-free model or perturb the chain text. They are not sufficiency tests, since we never keep only the cited regions. The control is a random position; object-level and entity-level controls were obtained only by replaying it, not by a separate run. And a black box is out of distribution: the control alone flips 5–6% of correct answers at 8.7% masked area. A gap licenses a graded statement, that the answer depends on what the chain points at more than on other pixels by so much, and the intervention’s own flip rate bounds it. A small gap has two readings, which our tests do not separate: the model does not use the cited box, or it uses it and finds the same content elsewhere. Under the cited mask the NExT-QA chains re-cite the blacked-out box in only 14–17% of steps and move to another box on the same frame in 67–74% (
Tab.
 
S16
, supplement), which fits the second reading. The citations are also unstable in the sense of 
[
37
]
: under the random mask the answer is unchanged for 96–98% of items, and on those the same box is cited again in only 52–55% of steps (76–84% on CLEVRER).

Why so little depends on the cited box on NExT-QA.

CLEVRER rules out the simplest account, that a 3B model cannot cite evidence its answer depends on. For NExT-QA the supervision predicts the outcome. The pseudo-labels cite two frames, and the entities the trained models name are detected on fourteen of sixteen; the same steps are attached to the correct option and to a wrong one, and in our audit 92 of 100 training chains only locate and describe an entity, written by a teacher that was shown the answer. Nothing ties the answer to the box: the bridge loss (
Eq.
 
1
) corrupts a step’s intermediate answer, not its evidence. The two benchmarks thus differ in three ways at once, redundancy of the evidence, citation format and supervision content, and our data separate them only in part: the entity-track test shows that redundancy accounts for a gap of 0.05, and nothing here isolates the other two; within NExT-QA the cite gap does not rise with the share of the entity’s frames that the citations cover. A crossed design (pseudo-labelled chains on CLEVRER, track-level spans on NExT-QA) would.

What the case is for.

The value of the NExT-QA result is not that it surprises. It is that the standard evaluation does not reveal it: on CLEVRER and on NExT-QA alike the chains parse, the boxes pass a grounding check, and the models use the video, while the dependence of the answer on the cited box differs by an order of magnitude. Work that reports grounded chains should report a cite gap and, where evidence is redundant, an entity-track gap beside it.

Limitations.

All training is LoRA on 2–3B models with at most 12,400 chain items and 1,000–1,500 RL steps. The NExT-QA results come from one benchmark and one base (InternVL3-2B was run on CLEVRER only); the entity-track test covers two models; no released grounded video model was tested. Only the mixed chain SFT arm has two seeds on NExT-QA, and intervals cover items, not training variance. The bridge loss is not ablated. The pseudo-labelled chains are noisy (box on the entity in 70% of audited steps, one annotator) and observational. The grounding check and the entity-track test use the same detector family as the training labels, and the latter removes only what the detector finds of what the chain names.

7 
Conclusion

A grounded reasoning chain makes two promises: that its citations can be checked, and that its answer rests on them. We proposed a counterfactual self-citation test for a consequence of the second promise, with an entity-track extension for redundant evidence, and applied both to small video–language models trained to reason in grounded chains. On CLEVRER, masking the cited boxes flips about twice as many correct answers as masking equal-area regions elsewhere, for every chain model on two bases. On NExT-QA, where the chains were supervised to cite two frames of a persistent entity, masking the cited boxes flips 2.3% of correct answers against 2.1%, and masking the named entity on every frame costs ten points more than a size-matched control on the options the model correctly affirms. Both sets of boxes pass a grounding check. Checkable, relevant citations can be ones the answer does not depend on, and both tests, which need no annotation, should accompany any claim that a video model’s grounded reasoning explains its answers. github.com/JugalGajjar/CausalSight

Appendix H 
Correct and wrong options

With one yes/no item per option, four of five NExT-QA items are wrong options, and a correct rejection rarely flips under a box mask. 
Tab.
 
S8
 therefore repeats both tests separately for the items whose option is correct and wrong. The small cite gap on NExT-QA is not a dilution effect: on correct options the cite gap of the models trained on NExT-QA chains is 0.003–0.012, and one interval of five excludes zero. The entity-track gap, in contrast, is concentrated on correct options (0.098 and 0.109 against 0.037 and 0.031): removing the entities a chain names mainly withdraws the model’s support for affirming an option. On the same correct affirmations, blacking out every frame flips 20.9% (mixed chain SFT) and 34.3% (NExT-QA chain GRPO). On CLEVRER the cite gap is positive on correct and on wrong options for every model, so there the cited evidence moves the answer in both directions; it is larger on wrong options for the mixed chain GRPO model (0.074 against 0.029) and larger on correct options for InternVL (0.055 against 0.034).

Reverse flips, net accuracy and question-level flips.

The gaps count correct answers that become wrong. 
Tab.
 
S11
 adds the other direction and two coarser views. On NExT-QA the cited mask also turns wrong answers right, slightly more often than the control, so net per-option accuracy moves by at most 0.004 under either; on CLEVRER it falls by 0.04–0.07 under the cited mask and 0.01–0.02 under the control for the models trained with CLEVRER chains. At the level of questions, the selected answer of a question whose five options were all judged correctly changes for 6–10% of questions under the cited mask and 5–11% under the control, and for 35–36% against 19–22% under the entity-track mask.

Control overlap.

The self-citation control places each box uniformly at random on the same frames and may overlap the cited box. 
Tab.
 
S13
 replays the seeded control draws and reports the mean share of the cited area that the control also blacks out, the share of items whose cited area the control leaves untouched, and the flip rate and cite gap on those items. On CLEVRER the control covers 6–10% of the cited area. On NExT-QA the boxes are large and it covers 38–40%, so on the full item set the control is a weaker contrast there. On the items with an untouched cited area the NExT-QA gap is 0.000–0.006 with every interval including zero, and the cited-box flip rate is 1.5–2.6%; on CLEVRER the same subset has gaps of 0.036–0.093. Overlap does not produce the small NExT-QA gap. The table also gives the number of sampled frames a cited region is applied to. On NExT-QA it is 2.0 for every model trained with NExT-QA chains and 1.3 for the CLEVRER chain GRPO model; on CLEVRER it is 1.2–1.5 for the models trained with CLEVRER chains and 2.0 for the NExT-QA chain GRPO model. Span length follows the training chains, not the benchmark.

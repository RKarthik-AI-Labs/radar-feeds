Abstract

As agents based on vision-language models (VLMs) increasingly interpret social-media content, errors in their analysis may lend credibility to rumors and amplify their spread. In this paper, we study the problem of 
visual cognitive collusion
, in which coordinated posts combine authentic images and locally faithful captions to induce VLM agents to accept attacker-chosen claims contradicted by the source context. To enable systematic evaluation of this cognitive threat, we introduce a new benchmark comprising 544 target cases from 61 videos across eight application domains, with 33.2K candidate-frame instances counted across target-specific pools. We further propose VisualMontage, a multi-agent framework for simulating such attacks through narrative planning, image selection and ordering, and caption assignment under iterative review. We conduct extensive experiments across 12 VLMs, examining modality effects, production-team components, and downstream defenses. Using identical attack feeds across models without victim-specific optimization, we demonstrate cross-model transferability, with mean attack success reaching 61.93% for proprietary models and 68.71% for open-weight models. Moreover, 92.65% of generated feeds pass independent model-based validity auditing. Even after downstream review, residual attack success rates range from 35.3% to 76.7% across the evaluated configurations. Our controlled comparisons further reveal that captions can improve model-rated posting naturalness while reducing attack success. Together, these findings reveal a novel cognitive threat in social-media interpretation and motivate safeguards that assess how authentic posts jointly shape agents’ reasoning and conclusions.

Introduction

Figure 1
: 
Comparison of multimodal attack strategies. Image-centric attacks fabricate or alter visual content, while channel-centric attacks convey hidden signals or malicious payloads. Our cognitive-centric attack instead combines authentic images to manipulate the victim VLM’s beliefs through self-induced misleading relations inferred across otherwise faithful posts.

Social media provides open channels where rumors can emerge from unverified interpretations of events and spread widely through repeated sharing 
[
55
]
. Across public affairs, science, and everyday social events, such rumors may distort judgments about what happened, who is responsible, and which sources deserve trust. Their influence can persist even after correction, allowing misinformation to continue shaping people’s reasoning 
[
10
]
. This risk takes on an additional dimension as VLMs become the perceptual and reasoning core of agents that browse, interpret visual observations, and act on users’ behalf 
[
29
, 
16
, 
24
, 
57
]
. Consequently, an agent’s event summary or analysis may lend a rumor credibility and encourage further sharing by its users.

This emerging social-media paradigm, where VLM agents serve as personal assistants 
[
16
, 
24
]
, can potentially be exploited by adversaries who jointly publish authentic images to the agents’ observable feeds through open channels. Authentic images can support misleading narratives when detached from their source context 
[
34
, 
1
]
. For example, photographs of residents waiting and a rescue boat departing may suggest abandonment when the intervening evacuation is omitted. Although each image remains unaltered and its caption faithfully describes what is visible, their combination possibly invites a conclusion contradicted by the full event. The agent’s analysis may then lend the rumor apparent visual support and credibility when shared with users. This cognitive threat arises when an agent combines individually faithful posts into a false interpretation of the event 
[
19
]
.

Compared with common attacks that disseminate fabricated images 
[
49
, 
26
, 
22
]
, rely on steganographic collusion 
[
42
]
, or activate preimplanted malicious associations or target outputs through backdoor triggers 
[
28
, 
35
, 
32
]
, 
visual cognitive collusion
 in Figure 
1
 targets beliefs through coordinated selection, ordering, and presentation of authentic images with faithful captions. It requires neither hidden messages nor victim-model modification, while remaining difficult to mitigate because verifying individual posts cannot establish the validity of the relations inferred across them. Drawing on cognitive science and film theory 
[
65
, 
41
, 
37
]
, we examine how recipients infer temporal, causal, or intentional relations across observations to construct a coherent event narrative. We formalize this threat by contrasting 
local factuality
, the factual integrity of each post, with a 
global falsehood
, an attacker-chosen claim contradicted by the source context but suggested by the combined feed.

To induce false beliefs from visual content, we propose 
VisualMontage
, a multi-agent collusive framework with a Screenwriter for relational planning, a Visual Editor for image selection and ordering, and a Caption Editor for faithful captioning and presentation modes. Furthermore, the Director agent guides composition toward stronger belief in the global falsehood while balancing coherence and sequence length. A publication module places approved posts from coordinated publishers into the victim’s observable feed. Victim agents may then confidently accept and relay the falsehood to users, becoming unwitting amplifiers of rumor cascades across social networks.

We conduct extensive simulation experiments with 12 VLMs across diverse domains, reusing feeds without victim-specific optimization. Mean attack success rate (ASR) reaches 61.93% for proprietary models and 68.71% for open-weight models, with mean reported confidence of 0.83 and 0.89 among false acceptances, respectively. Meanwhile, 92.65% of feeds pass independent validity auditing. These confidently wrong conclusions could cause cascading errors in downstream tasks, while our review and detection studies show limited protection. We further find that images alone outperform full-sequence feeds in our modality comparison, yet text makes social-media feeds more natural in model-based evaluations across multiple domains. Our work makes the following three contributions.

•

We formalize visual cognitive collusion through local factuality and global falsehood, and introduce a new benchmark with 544 target cases and 33.2K candidate images across eight domains.

•

We propose an automated multi-agent framework that coordinates narrative planning, visual composition, and faithful captioning through iterative review to induce false beliefs from authentic source material.

•

Across multiple VLMs from diverse model families, we characterize cross-model transferability, modality effects, production-team contributions, sequence length, construction cost, and the limits of downstream review and pre-exposure attack detection.

4.3 
Distributed Publication

To simulate malicious publication through open social-media channels, the attackers distribute the accepted montage plan through controlled Sybil identities, representing multiple publishers operated by the same adversary. Schedule 
 assigns these identities and display times to posts in the victim’s observable feed 
. Each post presents its image, caption, or both in the assigned mode, so the coordinated composition appears as ordinary posts from distinct publishers. Exposure occurs within a single context window, simulating how a user assistant interprets the feed.

4.4 
Downstream Victim Agent and Judge

To assess the practical risk to user assistants, we simulate a VLM agent interpreting a social-media feed in response to a user’s verification request. Each victim receives the feed and question in a fresh context, returning a verdict, confidence, and rationale. The rationale captures the agent’s self-justification for its verdict, allowing us to examine how it argues for a false conclusion. We compare individual responses with strict-majority aggregation of repeated responses and with downstream judge review of the feed alone or together with victim reports 
[
68
]
. Report-conditioned review models a downstream step in a misinformation cascade, testing whether an induced error is corrected or reinforced when another agent receives the victim’s conclusion and supporting rationale. Appendix 
A
 specifies aggregation, distinguishes vote fractions from self-reported confidence, and lists generation and auditing costs separately.

5.2 
Cognitive vulnerability across models

To test whether the constructed feed changes a victim’s judgment, Table 
2
 compares VisualMontage with Source-order, Random-order, and No Evidence on the same source–target cases. The source-sampling controls measure selection and arrangement jointly, whereas claim-only queries measure false acceptance without the feed.

The same model-agnostic attack plans demonstrate cross-model transferability without victim-specific optimization, reaching mean ASRs of 61.93% for proprietary models and 68.71% for open-weight models. By comparison, both source-sampling controls average below 10%, while claim-only acceptance is lower still. Attack success across all 12 evaluated VLMs suggests a shared cognitive vulnerability in interpreting authentic visual evidence. Moreover, larger open-weight models do not consistently achieve lower ASR, indicating that scale alone is insufficient protection.

This susceptibility persists when attack validity is assessed independently. Overall, 92.65% of feeds pass the audit, and mean V-ASR remains 56.89% and 63.28% for the two groups. Thus, the observed vulnerability extends to feeds passing checks on local factuality and isolated influence. The distinction matters for verification systems because individually defensible observations can still support a source-contradicted interpretation when presented together. Examples in Appendix 
C.4
 contrast successful and failed interpretations of the composed evidence.

Figure 
3
 and Table 
2
 highlight confident overinterpretation of authentic visual evidence. Mean confidence among false acceptances reaches 0.83 for proprietary models and 0.89 for open-weight models, while Qwen models retain high ASR at stringent confidence cutoffs. These results suggest that VLM agents infer unsupported relations between authentic observations and confidently endorse the resulting false narrative. This cognitive overreach makes reported confidence an unreliable indicator of factual correctness.

5.3 
Vulnerability across source domains

The threat also spans the benchmark’s diverse visual settings. Table 
3
 reports ASR above 55% in every domain, with Emergency Response reaching 72.71%. Public Safety and Aerospace Operations are similarly susceptible, making the failure relevant to assistants interpreting consequential events. The benchmark therefore supports studying a shared interpretation failure across public-event recordings and fictional narratives, although these unadjusted domain averages do not isolate intrinsic differences in difficulty.

Attack effectiveness and independent validity provide complementary views of this breadth. Emergency Response combines the highest domain ASR with 98.02% audit passage, showing that successful attacks can also satisfy the local checks. By contrast, Sports Training has a lower audit-pass rate despite similarly high acceptance. Reporting both outcomes avoids treating every persuasive sequence as a valid instance of the threat. Appendix 
C.9
 provides relation-level results and discusses controlled domain comparisons.

Downstream review and attack detection

Figure

 shows that majority voting stays close to the first-response result across families, so repeated judgments over the same feed offer little protection. Judge review reduces acceptance more substantially, yet even the best evaluated configuration leaves roughly one third of attacks successful. These victim–judge comparisons show that agreement over a feed cannot replace verification against its source context.

The controlled Emergency Response study in Table

Adding victim confidence and rationales to verdicts only slightly reduces residual ASR, yet increases downstream HC-ASR from 30.56% to 68.06%. Richer reports thus leave fewer errors but more high-confidence false acceptances. A manipulated agent’s self-justification may turn it into an unwitting accomplice, lending credibility to the attacker’s narrative. This simulated transmission illustrates a potential pathway for misinformation cascades when downstream agents treat an upstream agent’s confident reasoning as independent corroboration.

Sequence-length sensitivity

Table 
8
 shows that adding more items does not reliably strengthen the attack. Conditional ASR peaks at 15 items, yet only 9.86% of construction attempts produce an independently valid success. Six items achieve 33.90% under the same attempt-level accounting. The ranking therefore reverses when unsuccessful construction is retained, exposing the selection bias of judging length only on completed feeds.

Longer sequences consume more visual tokens and yield fewer valid feeds in this experiment. At 15 items, the mean image-token count exceeds twice that for six items, despite a lower valid success rate per attempt. Their added evidence may reinforce or disrupt the intended interpretation, so sequence length should be evaluated jointly with validity and construction yield. These results favor selective composition under the tested budget; they do not establish a universal optimal length. Appendix 
C.8
 details the protocol; Appendix 
C.11
 reports construction cost and convergence.

6 
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduce VisualMontageBench and a multimodal cognitive collusion attack that uses authentic images and locally faithful captions to manipulate VLM agents’ beliefs. Extensive simulations demonstrate cross-model transferability and confident false acceptance that persists through downstream review. These findings expose a gap between verifying individual posts and validating the conclusions drawn from them. In practical deployments, this gap could allow VLM assistants to lend credibility to misinformation and influence subsequent decisions, including in safety-critical settings. The resulting testbed supports further study of these risks and evaluation of safeguards that check inferred relations against source context. However, the evaluation remains limited to controlled simulations, leaving long-term effects in real social networks untested. We further discuss future directions for potential defense in Appendix 
D
.

Separate accounting distinguishes generation costs from the independent audits needed to verify a constructed attack.

Controls needed to interpret success.

The full-feed condition establishes whether the victim accepts the target. Both measured source-video references resample frames: Source-order presents them chronologically, whereas Random-order shuffles them. Their comparisons with montage therefore measure broader composition differences. A separate fixed-content permutation control can isolate order within selected records, while the evidence-free condition measures prior false acceptance. Whole-feed modality ablations test cross-modal contribution, and single-record projections test whether a locally dominant clue explains the result. Each comparison must specify which editing decisions it changes and preserve the remaining prompt, rendering, and publication-metadata conditions across presentations.

Distribution through multiple publishers also requires a matched control. Holding record content and order fixed while changing the attributed publisher tests whether apparent source multiplicity affects the victim’s judgment. Downstream-report controls similarly distinguish direct influence from the feed and additional influence from the victims’ conclusions. Together with source-aligned relation analysis and call, token, and latency accounting, these comparisons connect the social-media scenario to measurable cognitive errors. Simulated exposure, recommendations to share, and further dissemination remain distinct outcomes.

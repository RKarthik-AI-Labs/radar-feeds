Abstract

A vision-language model such as CLIP can classify images into categories it was never explicitly trained to recognize, but accuracy degrades when test images differ in style or source from the training data. A widely used remedy generates randomly cropped and flipped copies of each test image, called views, and combines their predictions. Because some views miss the object, these methods must decide which views to trust. The dominant approach measures prediction entropy, the concentration of each view’s predicted class distribution, and retains the most certain views. Other methods use quantities such as how typical a view’s embedding is among views of the same image. Despite repeated refinement of these scoring functions, the information they use, the model’s own response to each view, has remained largely unexamined. We examine this choice. Holding each stage of the pipeline fixed, we first show that view selection, rather than the gradient update, produces almost all of the accuracy gains. We then ask whether entropy can identify useful views when the method is wrong despite at least one view predicting the correct class. On these cases, entropy ranks correct views above incorrect ones no better than chance, recovering only about one-twentieth of the accuracy recovered by an oracle over the same views. Entropy is therefore weakly informative rather than useless. This result holds for two image encoders and under a control ruling out an obvious statistical artifact. An exact analysis of the entropy objective explains why: the score measures confidence, but not which class a view favors. We identify an unused signal in the pipeline: the augmentation procedure already knows how much of the image each crop contains. Ranking views by this quantity improves accuracy on 14 of 15 benchmark datasets over entropy and on 11 of 15 over the strongest scoring function tested. A pre-method measurement also predicts which datasets benefit.

Discussion

How the analysis and the measurements relate.

We are explicit about which way the support runs. 
Equation
 
3
 is exact and says a one-hot view
contributes zero, so a confidence filter retains the views contributing least; the measured
gradient stage is 
 percentage points. That measurement is the evidence and the analysis is
the account of it, not its derivation: the deployed step takes the 
sign
 of the gradient,
and realised logit motion agrees with the force coordinatewise only 
 of the time
(
appendix
 
C
), which is why we do not claim to have proved the update inert.

Equations
 
3
 and 
4
 concern the gradient and hold for any tuned parameters; the inertness is
an empirical finding on two parameter groups. Five hypotheses of our own were rejected by the
controls built to test them (
appendix
 
H
), including a stronger version of this paper’s own
headline.

What this means for deployment.

The backward pass can be deleted: it costs most of the compute and contributes 

percentage points. Confidence remains a usable coarse filter but should not be trusted as a
per-view reliability signal on the samples that matter, where it recovers about one twentieth of
what a better choice among the same views is worth. And the augmenter records a label-free signal
no method we surveyed reads back. The failure is not in the model’s perception but in the

reliability signal
 built on it, invisible to any evaluation that does not condition on the
errors a better choice could still fix.

Limitations and scope.

Crop geometry does not compose with a stronger scoring functional. We pre-registered the two
channels as additive; they are not, gaining 
 percentage points against a prediction of

, and under a stricter protocol the combination beats the better single signal in

 of 
 configurations, so once a stronger scoring function is in use the
crop-area gain largely does not add on top of it. We also have no mechanism for the gain itself.
Our rule cross-fits two parameters against an untuned baseline, so we do not claim it is
tuning-free, and we have not isolated the contribution of the retained-view count from that of
the crop-area weight; the DTD loss is the visible cost of that choice. Comparisons replay a
fixed cached subsample rather than the full test sets, which bounds the resolution of the paired
tests. The selection results run on the shared configuration TPT, ZERO and ADTE each declare,
but we did not run their code, so that reach is an argument rather than a measurement
(
appendix
 
E
). The gradient-stage results are specific to the optimiser and parameter group
studied, while 
Eqs.
 
3
 and 
4
 hold for any tuned parameters.

I.5 
Why a cached, fixed-view protocol

Every arm of every comparison replays one cached tensor of view embeddings. Because the image
encoder runs without gradient tracking, the

be computed once. Two consequences follow. First, all arms see bit-identical views, so an
observed difference cannot come from augmentation randomness, and no paired test is contaminated
by resampling. Second, because only the selection rule varies across arms, the comparisons measure a property
of that pipeline stage rather than of any one method’s optimiser. The entropy baseline is
evaluated at its published setting and is never tuned while our rule cross-fits two parameters;
we state that asymmetry rather than claim it favours the baseline.

I.6 
Why AUC for the view score, and not accuracy

The question is whether a score 
orders

threshold on it is good. Accuracy of a downstream selector confounds the score with the choice of
how many views to keep, with the aggregation rule, and with the base difficulty of the dataset.
The area under the ROC curve is the probability that a randomly chosen correct view outscores a
randomly chosen incorrect one. It is threshold-free, invariant to any monotone rescaling of the
score, and has an unambiguous null value of

about the score itself rather than about a tuned pipeline. It is also computable per image with
no method in the loop, which is what allows it to be used as an 
a priori

Sec.
 
5

I.7 
Why the population must be restricted, and the confound this creates

A view score can only change an answer on images where a different choice of views yields a
different prediction. Averaged over all images, a score is dominated by cases where selection is
irrelevant because the model is already right, or hopeless because no view is right. Restricting
to reducible errors is therefore not a convenience: it is the only population on which the
quantity of interest is defined. The restriction introduces a dependence, because reducibility
is defined using the averaged prediction and confident views agree with one another. The
split-half design (
Fig.
 
2
a) breaks that dependence by construction. We report both
the controlled and uncontrolled numbers so that the size of the confound is visible rather than
asserted to be small.

I.8 
Why the matched-multiset comparison, rather than an ablation

To ask what class identity is worth, an ordinary ablation would compare a model given class
indices against one that is not, which changes the input dimension and the amount of information
simultaneously. Our two arms instead receive vectors of identical length containing the identical
multiset of numbers, differing only in whether they are sorted by magnitude or indexed by class.
Capacity, dimensionality and the values themselves are therefore held exactly fixed, and the only
free variable is whether the association between a value and its class survives. A capacity
ladder is run on the arm we expect to lose, because under-fitting that arm would inflate the
measured gap in our favour; the ladder saturates in all 
 configurations.

I.9 
Why an oracle upper bound

An oracle selector uses the ground-truth label and is not a method. Its role is to convert the
vague question “could selection do better?” into a number: it is the best achievable accuracy
for 
any
 rule that selects the same number of views from the same candidates. Without it,
a small gain has no denominator, and the claim that a score captures 
 of what is available
could not be stated.

I.10 
Why cross-fitting, and why the baseline is never tuned

Our rule has two free parameters, 
 and 
; the baseline has none that we adjust. To keep
the comparison honest under that asymmetry we cross-fit: the data are divided into five parts and
the parameters used to score each part are selected using only the other four, so no image is
scored under parameters chosen with knowledge of its own outcome. The baseline is evaluated at
its published setting. The resulting comparison is therefore biased 
against
 the proposed
rule, which is the direction an honest comparison should err in.

I.11 
Why McNemar, bootstrap intervals, and a disjoint-sample replication

Predictions from two selection rules on the same image are paired, so an unpaired test would
overstate the variance and understate significance. McNemar’s test conditions on exactly the
images where the two rules disagree, which is the relevant evidence. AUC has no simple closed-form
interval under this design, so we use bootstrap resampling over 
 replicates. For the
predictive relationship of 
Sec.
 
5

the same images would be circular; we therefore recompute the two quantities on disjoint halves
and report that correlation as well.

I.12 
Why MEMO, and what it does and does not test

MEMO adapts model parameters rather than a prompt and performs no view selection. It is included
precisely because it differs in the parameter set: if the gradient step were inert only for a
particular parameterisation, MEMO would show a gain. Sweeping two parameter groups and nine
learning rates across four orders of magnitude, it does not. This tests whether the result is an
artefact of one parameter choice; it does not test architectures beyond those studied.

I.13 
Pre-registration, and predictions that failed

Where a mechanism was proposed, the prediction was written down before the run. Three such
predictions were rejected: that the two selection signals would combine additively, and two
separate accounts of why crop area helps. We report them because a design that never contradicts
its author is not testing anything.

Appendix L 
Broader impact

This work studies an existing test-time adaptation pipeline and reports where its reliability
signal fails. The practical recommendations, deleting a backward pass that contributes nothing
measurable and not treating prediction confidence as a per-view reliability signal, reduce
compute and discourage a form of unwarranted trust in model confidence, both of which we regard
as beneficial. The crop-area signal we propose uses no labels and no additional data, and
records only a geometric parameter the augmentation pipeline already samples, so it introduces
no new privacy surface. The main risk we can identify is misreading the audit: a score that is
near chance on reducible errors is not thereby useless overall, and 
appendix
 
H
 records
that we made exactly this error ourselves before the control was run. Deployments that gate
predictions on confidence should not conclude from this paper that confidence carries no
information, only that it should not be relied on to identify which view of an image is
trustworthy.
